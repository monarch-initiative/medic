# Comprehensive Review of Drug Therapies for Marfan Syndrome: Current Standard of Care, Investigational Agents, and Emerging Treatment Strategies

Marfan syndrome represents one of the most challenging heritable connective tissue disorders affecting modern clinical practice, with the disease predominantly determined by alterations in the FBN1 gene encoding fibrillin-1, a glycoprotein that serves as a critical component of the extracellular matrix microfibrils.[6][6] The hallmark complications of this disorder—most notably aortic root aneurysm, progressive aortic dilatation, and catastrophic aortic dissection—have driven intensive investigation into pharmacological interventions capable of slowing disease progression and reducing premature mortality.[13][23] Over the past two decades, the therapeutic landscape for Marfan syndrome has evolved substantially, transitioning from empirical hemodynamic management with beta-blockers to mechanism-based approaches targeting the transforming growth factor beta signaling pathway.[1][5][12] This comprehensive review synthesizes current evidence regarding approved pharmacotherapies, investigational agents in clinical development, drug contraindications, and emerging therapeutic strategies, providing clinicians and researchers with a contemporary understanding of the evolving treatment options available for patients with this life-threatening genetic disorder.

## Understanding Marfan Syndrome Pathophysiology and Its Implications for Drug Development

### The Molecular Basis of Marfan Syndrome and Cardiovascular Manifestations

Marfan syndrome is fundamentally caused by mutations in the FBN1 gene that result in either reduced production or abnormal structure of fibrillin-1 protein.[6][6] The normal biological function of fibrillin-1 extends far beyond its structural role in providing elasticity to connective tissues; rather, fibrillin-1-rich microfibrils serve a critical regulatory function by binding and sequestering latent transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) in the extracellular matrix.[1][1] When fibrillin-1 is deficient or dysfunctional, the sequestration capacity for TGF-β is compromised, leading to excessive activation and release of this potent signaling molecule.[6][6] This dysregulation of TGF-β signaling represents the primary pathophysiological driver of aortic pathology in Marfan syndrome, distinguishing it from non-syndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm and providing the mechanistic basis for contemporary therapeutic approaches.

The pathological cascade initiated by excessive TGF-β signaling involves multiple interconnected mechanisms that collectively result in progressive weakening of the aortic wall structure.[1][1] Elevated TGF-β-induced signaling promotes increased transcription and activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, which mediate degradation of elastic fibers in the vascular wall.[13][1][1] Simultaneously, TGF-β stimulation triggers excessive collagen synthesis and deposition, resulting in maladaptive remodeling of the aortic wall that paradoxically reduces structural integrity despite increased collagen content.[1] The cumulative effects of elastic fiber fragmentation, MMP upregulation, and abnormal collagen deposition create a vicious cycle of progressive aortic root dilatation, loss of vessel compliance, and eventually aortic dissection or rupture if untreated.[1][13][23] Understanding this molecular pathophysiology has proven instrumental in identifying therapeutic targets and in designing rational drug combinations that address multiple steps within the pathological cascade.

### Cardiovascular Manifestations Requiring Pharmacological Intervention

The cardiovascular manifestations of Marfan syndrome represent the primary determinants of life expectancy and represent the focus of most pharmacological interventions.[13][23][33] Aortic root dilatation is the most frequent cardiovascular manifestation of Marfan syndrome, occurring as a result of the pathological remodeling processes described above.[13][33] Progressive aortic root enlargement creates two distinct clinical problems: first, functional aortic regurgitation resulting from failure of the aortic valve leaflets to coapt properly as the root dilates, and second, mechanical stress that predisposes to catastrophic aortic dissection when the aortic root diameter exceeds critical thresholds.[13][23][33] Mitral valve prolapse represents another common cardiovascular feature of Marfan syndrome, with approximately 60-80% of patients demonstrating some degree of mitral valve abnormality, ranging from asymptomatic prolapse to severe mitral regurgitation requiring surgical intervention.[1][23] The pathophysiology of mitral valve disease in Marfan syndrome also appears related to dysregulated TGF-β signaling, with preclinical studies demonstrating that TGF-β antagonism can rescue the mitral valve phenotype in mouse models of the disease.[1][1] Beyond structural valvular disease, Marfan syndrome predisposes to progressive ventricular dysfunction and cardiomyopathy in some patients, particularly those with severe mitral regurgitation or chronic aortic regurgitation.[11][11]

## Approved Pharmacological Therapies for Marfan Syndrome

### Beta-Adrenergic Receptor Blockers: Foundation of Standard Care

Beta-adrenergic receptor blockers represent the longest-established pharmacological treatment for cardiovascular disease in Marfan syndrome, with clinical use dating back to pioneering observations in the 1970s demonstrating that reduction in aortic pressure derivative (dP/dt) provided greater protection against aortic dissection than blood pressure reduction alone.[5][18] The mechanistic rationale for beta-blocker therapy in Marfan syndrome remains primarily hemodynamic; these agents reduce heart rate and myocardial contractility, thereby decreasing the hemodynamic stress imposed on the proximal aorta, which is the site of most severe pathology in Marfan syndrome.[1][5][1] By titrating beta-blockers to achieve target heart rates of 60-70 beats per minute at rest and fewer than 100 beats per minute during submaximal exercise, clinicians can substantially reduce the rate of increase in aortic pressure over time.[1][18]

The most extensively studied and commonly prescribed beta-blocker for Marfan syndrome is atenolol, a cardioselective agent with a long half-life and favorable side effect profile compared to earlier agents such as propranolol.[1][5][18][18] In pediatric patients with Marfan syndrome, atenolol has emerged as the beta-blocker of choice due to its favorable pharmacokinetics and tolerability.[1] In adult patients, atenolol, metoprolol, and bisoprolol are all utilized in clinical practice.[1] The landmark randomized controlled trial establishing beta-blocker efficacy, conducted in the 1990s, enrolled 32 patients with Marfan syndrome assigned to propranolol therapy and compared them with 38 untreated controls over approximately 10 years of follow-up.[5] While overall event-free survival did not differ significantly between groups at study completion, the treated group demonstrated significantly lower event rates during intermediate years of follow-up, suggesting a meaningful protective effect during a critical window of aortic disease progression.[5]

However, a Cochrane systematic review analyzing the available evidence regarding beta-blocker efficacy in Marfan syndrome revealed substantial limitations in the evidence base, with only one adequately powered randomized controlled trial meeting inclusion criteria.[18] The analysis demonstrated that propranolol compared with no treatment reduced the rate of enlargement of aortic diameter, yet the trial did not demonstrate significant differences in mortality, aortic dissection rates, aortic regurgitation, heart failure, or need for cardiac surgery.[18] Notably, the quality of evidence was judged as low due to high risk of bias in the single available trial, highlighting the somewhat counterintuitive situation that the longest-established treatment for Marfan syndrome cardiovascular disease rests on relatively limited high-quality evidence.[18] Nevertheless, beta-blockers remain the standard of care and are recommended as first-line therapy for all Marfan syndrome patients who can tolerate them, with dosages adjusted according to heart rate response during exercise to achieve optimal hemodynamic effect.[5][15][33]

A significant limitation of beta-blocker monotherapy emerged as larger cohorts were followed and more sophisticated imaging techniques became available: despite optimal beta-blocker treatment, many patients continue to experience progressive aortic root dilatation, necessitating eventual surgical intervention.[1][5] This incomplete efficacy likely reflects the fact that beta-blockers address only the hemodynamic component of aortic pathology while leaving the underlying molecular pathophysiology—TGF-β dysregulation and matrix remodeling—fundamentally untreated.[1][5] The recognition of this mechanistic limitation provided the impetus for investigation of mechanism-based therapies targeting TGF-β signaling and its downstream effectors.

### Angiotensin Receptor Blockers: Mechanism-Based Therapy Targeting TGF-β Signaling

The discovery that angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) blockade could antagonize TGF-β signaling represented a major breakthrough in Marfan syndrome therapeutics, translating molecular insights from animal models into novel clinical approaches.[1][4][5][12] Initial mechanistic work demonstrated that angiotensin II, beyond its well-established role in blood pressure regulation, modulates transforming growth factor beta activity through both receptor-dependent and receptor-independent mechanisms.[5][12] Critically, preclinical studies in mouse models of Marfan syndrome showed that AT1R blockade not only reduced hemodynamic stress but also attenuated the underlying tissue pathology by reducing TGF-β signaling.[1][5][12] A seminal 1998 animal study demonstrated that losartan, an AT1R antagonist, produced full correction of phenotypic abnormalities in the aortic wall of Marfan syndrome mice, an effect substantially superior to that achieved with atenolol monotherapy.[1]

Following these promising preclinical observations, clinical trials began evaluating AT1R blockers in patients with Marfan syndrome. A small pilot study of 18 children with severe Marfan syndrome and moderate-to-severe aortic root dilatation who received losartan 1.4 mg/kg per day in addition to beta-blocker therapy demonstrated significant reduction in the rate of change of aortic root diameter and sinotubular junction diameter over a mean two-year observation period, providing initial clinical evidence of efficacy.[5] These early observations sparked a series of larger randomized controlled trials comparing different AT1R blockers with beta-blockers or placebo controls.

Losartan, typically dosed at 1.3-1.4 mg/kg per day in children and scaled appropriately in adults, has been the most extensively studied AT1R blocker in Marfan syndrome, with its orphan drug designation for Marfan syndrome treatment dating to December 2011.[3] A meta-analysis of seven randomized controlled trials involving 1,352 patients with a weighted average follow-up of 37.8 months demonstrated that losartan monotherapy was associated with a significantly smaller change in aortic root diameter compared with control groups (weighted mean change 0.44 mm with losartan versus 0.58 mm in controls, mean difference -0.13 mm; 95% confidence interval -0.24 to -0.02; p=0.02).[2][25] When subgroup analyses compared losartan with beta-blockers versus losartan with standard therapy, no significant interaction was observed, suggesting similar efficacy across different comparison groups.[2][25] However, the composite outcome of aortic surgery, dissection, or mortality did not differ significantly between losartan and control groups in this meta-analysis, suggesting that while losartan effectively slows aortic dilatation, its impact on hard clinical outcomes remains uncertain.[2][25]

A more recent and rigorous individual patient data meta-analysis involving collaborative analysis of seven randomized trials with 1,442 eligible participants provided more definitive evidence of losartan's mechanism-based benefits.[7][7][21][46] During a median follow-up of three years, allocation to an ARB approximately halved the annual rate of change in aortic root Z score (mean annual increase 0.07 with ARB versus 0.13 with control; absolute difference -0.07; 95% CI -0.12 to -0.01; p=0.012).[7][7][21] Notably, prespecified subgroup analyses demonstrated that the protective effects of ARBs were particularly pronounced in patients with confirmed pathogenic variants in fibrillin-1 (heterogeneity p=0.0050), providing strong biological support for the mechanism-based effects of TGF-β antagonism in patients with established FBN1 mutations.[7][7][21] Furthermore, the beneficial effects of ARBs were independent of concurrent beta-blocker use, suggesting additive benefit when both therapies were combined (heterogeneity p=0.54).[7][7][21]

The AIMS (Irbesartan in Marfan syndrome) trial, a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial conducted at 22 centers throughout the United Kingdom, provided particularly robust evidence for AT1R blockade efficacy using irbesartan, a selective AT1R antagonist.[14] This trial enrolled individuals aged 6-40 years with clinically confirmed Marfan syndrome and randomized them following a lead-in phase of open-label irbesartan to either 150 mg irbesartan (increased to 300 mg as tolerated) or matching placebo.[14] The trial demonstrated that irbesartan treatment was associated with lower rates of aortic dilatation in children and young adults with Marfan syndrome compared with placebo, with this treatment effect apparent at one year and maintained throughout the five-year observation period.[14] Importantly, the AIMS trial confirmed that AT1R blockade was well tolerated, with no differences in serious adverse events or suspected drug-related adverse events between irbesartan and placebo groups.[14] The aortic root diameter reduction observed with irbesartan treatment relative to placebo ranged from 0.67 to 1.37 mm over follow-up periods of two to five years, representing a clinically meaningful slowing of progressive aortic dilatation.[14]

The long-term clinical outcomes study extending the COMPARE trial cohort (which compared losartan with atenolol), with a median follow-up of eight years and capturing 94% of the original cohort participants, demonstrated that continued use of losartan was significantly associated with reduced incidence of aortic dissection and death compared with patients in whom losartan was not added to their treatment regimen.[35] This long-term follow-up provided reassuring evidence that the short-term benefits of losartan on aortic root Z scores translate into meaningful reductions in serious adverse cardiovascular outcomes during extended follow-up periods.

### Comparative Efficacy: ARBs Versus Beta-Blockers

The question of whether AT1R blockers provide superior efficacy compared with beta-blockers has been extensively investigated through multiple randomized trials. Three trials involving 766 eligible participants directly compared ARBs with beta-blockers in terms of their effects on aortic root Z score change.[7][7][46] During a median follow-up of three years, the annual change in aortic root Z score was similar in both treatment groups (annual increase -0.08 with ARB versus -0.11 with beta-blocker; absolute difference 0.03; 95% CI -0.05 to 0.10; p=0.48).[7][7][46] This lack of significant difference between ARBs and beta-blockers suggests that both drug classes provide comparable hemodynamic and biological benefits for aortic root dilatation prevention. However, individual patient circumstances may favor one class over the other; for instance, ARBs are generally reported to be better tolerated than beta-blockers, with fewer reports of exercise intolerance, fatigue, and depression.[4] Conversely, current clinical practice guidelines and expert recommendations suggest that beta-blockers should remain first-line therapy, with losartan introduced when beta-blockers are not tolerated or provide inadequate aortic protection.[15][33]

## Investigational and Emerging Pharmacological Approaches

### Angiotensin Receptor Blocker-Neprilysin Inhibitors: Novel Dual-Target Therapy

A promising emerging class of drugs receiving increasing attention for Marfan syndrome management comprises angiotensin receptor blocker-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), exemplified by sacubitril/valsartan, a fixed-dose combination that combines AT1R blockade with inhibition of neprilysin, a neutral endopeptidase responsible for degradation of natriuretic peptides and other cardioprotective mediators.[1][11][11] The ARNI class has demonstrated substantial benefits in heart failure management, with clinical trials establishing superiority of sacubitril/valsartan over ACE inhibitors alone in reducing cardiovascular mortality and hospitalization rates.[11][11] Preliminary preclinical data from mouse models of Marfan syndrome suggest that a low dose of sacubitril/valsartan can inhibit ascending aorta dilation to a greater extent than the ARB valsartan alone, likely through neprilysin-mediated inhibition conferring additional benefits beyond simple AT1R blockade.[1] The mechanism of ARNI benefit in Marfan syndrome appears to involve dual inhibition of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system through valsartan-mediated AT1R blockage combined with neprilysin inhibition, which increases protective natriuretic peptides (atrial natriuretic peptide, B-type natriuretic peptide, C-type natriuretic peptide, bradykinin, and adrenomedullin).[11][11]

A case report of sacubitril/valsartan use in an MFS patient demonstrated impressive clinical improvement in MFS-related cardiomyopathy, with progressive increases in left ventricular ejection fraction (from 30% to 42%), reduction in left ventricular end-systolic and end-diastolic dimensions, and reduction in left ventricular mass index over nine months of follow-up.[11][11] The patient maintained freedom from acute heart failure episodes and demonstrated significant quality of life improvement until the nine-month follow-up assessment.[11][11] While this represents only a single case report and larger clinical trials are needed to establish the role of ARNIs in Marfan syndrome management, the theoretical benefits of dual pathway inhibition combined with the promising preclinical and case report data suggest that ARNIs represent a rational therapeutic candidate deserving of formal clinical investigation in appropriately designed trials.[1][11]

### Matrix Metalloproteinase Inhibitors: Targeting Downstream Effectors of TGF-β Signaling

The observation of increased matrix metalloproteinase expression in vascular tissue samples obtained from ascending aortic aneurysms of patients with Marfan syndrome prompted investigation into whether MMP inhibition could prevent or slow aortic pathology in this disorder.[1][1][10] Doxycycline, a tetracycline antibiotic with well-documented non-antibiotic pleiotropic effects including broad-spectrum MMP inhibition, has emerged as a particularly promising candidate for this approach.[1][1][10] The mechanistic basis for doxycycline's benefit in Marfan syndrome involves both direct inhibition of MMP enzyme activity and highly efficient reduction of aortic MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression levels.[1][1][10] By reducing MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity, doxycycline effectively delays elastic fiber fragmentation and aortic aneurysm progression in mouse models of Marfan syndrome.[1][1][10]

Multiple preclinical studies have systematically evaluated doxycycline's effects on aortic pathology using well-established mouse models of Marfan syndrome (Fbn1^mgR/mgR^ and Fbn1^C1039G/+^ strains).[1][1][10] In the Fbn1^mgR/mgR^ model, doxycycline treatment significantly delayed aortic aneurysm rupture and prolonged survival compared with untreated controls, an effect mediated through inhibition of tissue MMP-2 and MMP-9 and prevention of elastic fiber fragmentation.[10] A recent long-term treatment study demonstrated that sustained doxycycline administration not only ameliorated aortic pathology in the Fbn1^C1039G/+^ model but also remarkably improved skin elasticity and structural integrity, suggesting potential benefits extending beyond the cardiovascular system.[1][1] Most strikingly, long-term doxycycline treatment demonstrated superior efficacy compared with the beta-blocker atenolol in preventing thoracic aortic aneurysm development in Fbn1^C1039G/+^ mice, accompanied by improved endothelial function, enhanced elastic fiber properties, and overall superior aortic wall structure.[1][1][37]

This superior efficacy of doxycycline compared with atenolol in animal models is conceptually important because it suggests that targeting the underlying pathophysiological mechanism (TGF-β-mediated MMP activation and matrix degradation) may provide greater benefits than treating only the hemodynamic manifestations of the disease.[1][1][37] However, the translation of these impressive preclinical results into human clinical trials has remained challenging. A 2011 review identified pharmacological and genomic evidence suggesting that combination therapy with losartan and doxycycline would prevent aortic complications of Marfan syndrome, with some investigators recommending that if ongoing well-designed trials confirm their efficacy, the outlook for Marfan syndrome patients would improve considerably.[12] Notably, investigations in Marfan mouse models have demonstrated that combination therapy with losartan and doxycycline was considerably more effective at preventing thoracic aortic aneurysm than either agent alone, providing additional biological rationale for this combination approach.[12]

Despite the compelling preclinical evidence supporting doxycycline use in Marfan syndrome, human clinical trials have not yet definitively established its efficacy or optimal dosing. Furthermore, concerns regarding long-term antibiotic use, including photosensitivity and potential for adverse drug interactions, have limited clinical application of this approach in routine practice. Nevertheless, doxycycline remains under investigation and continues to be considered a rational repurposing candidate for Marfan syndrome management, particularly in combination strategies targeting multiple pathophysiological pathways.

### Statin Therapy: Targeting Ras-Dependent Signaling Pathways

Statins, a class of drugs traditionally utilized for cholesterol reduction through inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase, have emerged as unexpected therapeutic candidates for Marfan syndrome based on their ability to inhibit prenylation of Ras and downstream signaling through extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK).[19][1] Beyond their primary cholesterol-lowering effects, statins inhibit farnesylation and geranylgeranylation, critical post-translational modifications required for membrane localization and activation of small GTPases including Ras.[19][1] This additional mechanism is relevant to Marfan syndrome because excessive TGF-β signaling in Marfan aortas is associated with elevated Ras-dependent ERK signaling, which in turn drives MMP activation and matrix degradation.[19][1]

In the Fbn1^C1039G/+^ mouse model of Marfan syndrome, pravastatin and a more lipophilic statin (MA) significantly reduced aneurysm growth compared with vehicle control, whereas a non-lipophilic statin (PA) did not produce significant aneurysm size reduction.[19][1] Histological analysis revealed that statin-treated animals demonstrated reduced elastin breakdown compared with controls.[19][1] Mechanistically, both phosphorylated c-Raf and phosphorylated ERK1/2, which were elevated in control Marfan mice, were significantly reduced in statin-treated animals.[19][1] These findings establish that aneurysm reduction following statin treatment is associated with decreased Ras-dependent ERK signaling, with MMP activity reduction occurring through diminished Ras signaling and subsequent ERK-dependent MMP activation.[19][1]

The clinical implications of these preclinical findings suggest that optimal medical therapy for Marfan syndrome aortic disease might ideally include drugs that block multiple locations within the TGF-β signaling pathway, including beta-blockers for hemodynamic effects, losartan for direct TGF-β antagonism, and potentially statins for ERK pathway inhibition.[19][1] While human clinical trials of statin therapy in Marfan syndrome are limited, some observational data suggest that statins may reduce aortic aneurysm growth rates in humans with various forms of thoracic aortic disease, including bicuspid aortic valve–associated aortopathies.[19] The potential for statins to provide disease-modifying benefits in Marfan syndrome without substantial additional adverse effects makes this class an attractive repurposing candidate deserving of formal clinical investigation.

### Novel Molecular Targets: mTOR, PIEZO1, and Cell-Type Specific TGF-β Inhibition

Recent investigations have identified emerging molecular targets that may open entirely new therapeutic avenues for Marfan syndrome management. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) has emerged as a candidate therapeutic target, with its activation implicated in driving thoracic aortic aneurysm development in Marfan syndrome models.[40] When rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, was administered intraperitoneally to Fbn1^mgR/mgR^ mice for two weeks and aortic changes analyzed by echocardiography, pathological staining, and immunofluorescence, rapamycin significantly inhibited mTOR downstream targets including p-RPS6, tumor necrosis factor alpha, MMP-2, and MMP-9, suggesting a potentially important alternative pathway to explore therapeutically.[40]

Another intriguing emerging target involves PIEZO1, a mechanosensitive ion channel recently implicated in vascular biology and aortic disease pathophysiology.[40] Injection of Yoda 1, a PIEZO1 agonist, inhibited TGF-β pathway activation, enhanced extracellular matrix integrity, and protected vascular smooth muscle cells from apoptosis, thereby inhibiting and reversing thoracic aortic aneurysm progression in experimental models.[40] Remarkably, long-term Yoda 1 treatment exhibited no significant side effects, indicating good safety and therapeutic potential.[40] These findings suggest that PIEZO1 acts as a protective molecule in the context of Marfan aortic disease, with PIEZO1 deficiency exacerbating aneurysm progression, and that targeting PIEZO1 signaling represents a promising therapeutic strategy with Yoda 1 emerging as a strong candidate for clinical safety trials.[40]

Cell-type specific inhibition of TGF-β signaling has emerged as another sophisticated approach deserving investigation. Ablation of TGF-β receptor 2 (TGFBR2) specifically in myeloid cells reduced macrophage infiltration into the aortic wall of Marfan syndrome mice and attenuated phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and ERK1/2.[1][1] These findings indicate that TGF-β-driven Smad2/3 and ERK1/2 activation specifically in myeloid cells plays an important role in promoting aortic aneurysm development in Marfan syndrome and is not essential for normal aortic development.[1][1] These observations suggest that cell-type-specific inhibition of TGF-β signaling in myeloid cells might represent a potential future treatment option capable of avoiding unwanted effects of global TGF-β antagonism while achieving therapeutic benefit through selective pathway inhibition.[1][1]

## Drug Contraindications and Potentially Harmful Therapies

### Calcium Channel Blockers: Paradoxical Acceleration of Aortic Disease

A critically important clinical observation emerged from investigations examining the effects of calcium channel blockers (CCBs) in Marfan syndrome: contrary to initial expectations based on their hemodynamic properties, CCBs appear to accelerate rather than slow aortic disease progression in this disorder, representing a potential iatrogenic harm if prescribed to Marfan syndrome patients.[16][1] This counterintuitive finding emerged from both preclinical animal studies and retrospective human observational data, providing convergent evidence for a deleterious drug-disease interaction.

Marfan syndrome mice treated with the dihydropyridine-class CCB amlodipine demonstrated dramatically accelerated aortic aneurysm expansion, rupture, and premature lethality compared with untreated Marfan controls, despite the expected hemodynamic benefits of reduced contractility and blood pressure lowering.[16] This deleterious effect was not limited to amlodipine; the phenylalkylamine-class CCB verapamil similarly exacerbated the histological changes observed in the ascending aortic wall of Marfan mice, with the effect specific to the proximal aorta where Marfan syndrome pathology is most severe (the descending thoracic aorta was unaffected).[16] Mechanistically, the CCB-induced acceleration of aortic aneurysm appears to be both extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK1/2) dependent and angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) dependent, suggesting a complex interaction involving enhanced pathological signaling pathways rather than simple loss of hemodynamic protection.[16]

Human observational data provided sobering clinical confirmation of these preclinical findings. In a registry analysis of Marfan syndrome patients, those who received CCBs as compared with other antihypertensive agents demonstrated markedly increased odds of aortic dissection (odds ratio 12.5; p=0.032), with this strong association remaining after statistical correction for either systolic blood pressure or aortic root size at baseline enrollment.[16] Furthermore, CCB-treated Marfan patients demonstrated significantly increased odds of requiring aortic surgery (odds ratio 5.5; p<0.001) when compared with patients receiving other antihypertensive agents, an effect that remained significant after correction for blood pressure or aortic size.[16] These alarming observational findings, combined with the mechanistic evidence from animal models, led to the clinical conclusion that calcium channel blockers should be avoided in patients with Marfan syndrome, even though they are sometimes considered as an alternative for patients intolerant of beta-blockers.[16] Instead, for beta-blocker-intolerant patients, verapamil (a non-dihydropyridine CCB used as a hemodynamic agent) or other alternative antihypertensive strategies such as hydralazine should be considered, with hydralazine being particularly appealing for pregnant women with Marfan syndrome due to its known safety profile in pregnancy.[16]

### Fluoroquinolone Antibiotics: Iatrogenic Risk of Aortic Complications

In December 2018, the United States Food and Drug Administration issued a formal warning regarding fluoroquinolone antibiotics (including levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin, and ofloxacin) and their association with increased risk of aortic aneurysms and aortic dissections, specifically targeting warnings to older adults and patients with predisposing conditions including Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, atherosclerosis, peripheral vascular disease, and history of aneurysms.[24][27] This warning was based on accumulating pharmacovigilance data suggesting that the risk of aortic complications—already significantly elevated in these patient populations—increased further with fluoroquinolone exposure.[24][27] While the absolute increase in aortic risk attributable to fluoroquinolones is modest in the general population, the relative increase in already high-risk populations such as Marfan syndrome can be very significant.[27]

The mechanistic basis for fluoroquinolone-associated aortic complications remains incompletely understood but likely involves direct effects of these antibiotics on connective tissue properties, potentially through effects on collagen cross-linking or through inhibition of enzymes involved in normal vascular remodeling.[24][27] Both the National Health Service in the United Kingdom and the FDA recommend against use of fluoroquinolone antibiotics in individuals with a higher inherent risk of aortic aneurysm and dissection, including those with Marfan syndrome.[24][27] In nearly all clinical situations where a fluoroquinolone antibiotic might be considered in a patient with Marfan syndrome, alternative antibiotics from different drug classes are available and should be preferred, making the avoidance of fluoroquinolones a straightforward clinical decision.[27]

## Combination Pharmacological Approaches and Synergistic Strategies

### Beta-Blocker and Angiotensin Receptor Blocker Combination Therapy

One of the most important developments in Marfan syndrome pharmacotherapy involves recognition that combining beta-blockers with ARBs provides synergistic or at minimum additive benefits beyond what either drug class achieves as monotherapy.[1][7][7][21][46] This combination approach is mechanistically rational because beta-blockers primarily address the hemodynamic component of aortic pathology through reduction in heart rate and contractility, while ARBs target the underlying molecular pathophysiology through TGF-β antagonism, potentially addressing complementary pathogenic mechanisms.[1][7] The landmark individual patient data meta-analysis analyzing the effects of both drug classes separately and in combination concluded that if tolerated, combination therapy with both a beta-blocker and an ARB could reduce the rate of enlargement of the aortic root by at least one half, and potentially by substantially more.[7][7][21][46] This combination effect is particularly important when projected over multiple years of treatment, as the cumulative delay in aortic dilatation achieved through combination therapy—estimated as reducing the annual rate of aortic root Z score increase from approximately 0.13 to 0.07 or potentially lower—could substantially delay or eliminate the need for aortic root replacement surgery in many patients.[7][7][21][46]

Clinical trials have verified the feasibility and safety of combining these drug classes. Following the long-term clinical outcomes analysis of the COMPARE trial (which directly compared losartan with atenolol), researchers discovered that a potential clinical benefit of combined treatment strategy with both ARBs and beta-blockers was demonstrated when analyzing longer-term outcomes.[1] The data support an assumption of favorable combined treatment benefit for limiting aortic aneurysm growth and preventing long-term adverse outcomes in patients with Marfan syndrome.[1] Current recommendations from multiple expert sources and institutions now favor combination therapy for most patients with Marfan syndrome who can tolerate both drug classes, with the understanding that this combination therapy may ultimately provide substantially greater clinical benefit than either monotherapy alone.[1][7][7][21][33][46]

### Beta-Blocker and Doxycycline Combination: Targeting Multiple Pathophysiological Pathways

Investigations in mouse models of Marfan syndrome have demonstrated that combination therapy with losartan (representing the ARB class) and doxycycline (representing the MMP inhibitor class) was considerably more effective at preventing thoracic aortic aneurysm than either agent administered alone.[12] The mechanistic rationale for this combination is compelling: losartan or other ARBs target TGF-β dysregulation and its signaling consequences, while doxycycline directly inhibits MMPs and thereby prevents elastic fiber degradation that results from excessive MMP activity.[12] By simultaneously targeting both the upstream TGF-β dysregulation and the downstream MMP-mediated tissue destruction, this combination approach addresses complementary aspects of the pathophysiological cascade driving Marfan aortic disease.[12]

While human clinical trials directly comparing combination therapy (either ARB+doxycycline or beta-blocker+doxycycline) with monotherapy or placebo controls have not yet been completed, the compelling preclinical evidence and the theoretical advantages of multitarget therapy have prompted some expert recommendations suggesting that combination pharmacological therapy targeting multiple pathophysiological pathways would appear logical and rational.[12] However, practical considerations regarding long-term antibiotic therapy, potential drug interactions, and the unknown effects of sustained doxycycline exposure limit enthusiasm for immediate clinical adoption of this combination approach pending results of properly designed prospective trials.[12]

## Special Populations and Treatment Considerations

### Pregnancy and Reproductive Planning in Marfan Syndrome

The management of cardiovascular disease in pregnant women with Marfan syndrome presents unique therapeutic challenges because many of the standard antihypertensive agents used in non-pregnant Marfan patients are teratogenic and therefore contraindicated during pregnancy. Both ACE inhibitors and ARBs, which block the renin-angiotensin system, are well-established teratogens associated with increased risk of congenital malformations when used during pregnancy, particularly in the second and third trimesters.[31] First-trimester ACE inhibitor exposure has been associated with a 2.7-fold increased risk of major congenital malformations, with a 3.7-fold increased risk of cardiovascular malformations and a 4.4-fold increased risk of central nervous system malformations.[31]

For pregnant women with Marfan syndrome requiring antihypertensive therapy, hydralazine emerges as a particularly appealing option given its established safety profile during pregnancy, with minimal teratogenic risk documented in the medical literature.[16] Beta-blockers generally are considered relatively safe during pregnancy, though concerns regarding potential fetal effects have prompted careful consideration of individual beta-blocker agents and dosing strategies in this population.[16] The combination of hydralazine with beta-blockers represents a reasonable approach for many pregnant Marfan syndrome patients requiring substantial blood pressure control and aortic stress reduction during the physiologically demanding period of pregnancy.

### Pediatric Management and Age-Appropriate Dosing

The management of Marfan syndrome in pediatric populations requires careful attention to age-appropriate dosing of medications, with doses typically scaled according to body surface area and weight rather than using fixed adult doses.[1][4][5] Beta-blockers in pediatric Marfan syndrome patients are commonly initiated at lower doses and titrated upward to achieve target heart rate goals of less than 100 beats per minute during submaximal exercise, as pediatric patients generally tolerate higher resting heart rates than adults.[1][5] Losartan in pediatric patients is typically dosed at approximately 1.3-1.4 mg/kg per day, substantially higher on a per-kilogram basis than typical adult dosing.[1] As children grow, medication doses require regular adjustment to maintain optimal hemodynamic and biological effects, necessitating close monitoring and frequent dose modifications.[1]

A particularly important consideration in pediatric Marfan syndrome management involves the early institution of cardioprotective therapy even in children without significant aortic dilatation at baseline. Current recommendations suggest that all Marfan syndrome patients who can tolerate beta-blockade should be treated with these agents regardless of the presence or absence of aortic dilatation, based on the understanding that early intervention when aortic disease is mild or absent provides the greatest opportunity to prevent progressive dilatation.[5][15][33] This preventive approach reflects the recognition that aortic disease in Marfan syndrome is progressive and that intervening early in the disease course, before substantial structural damage has accumulated, likely yields better long-term outcomes.

### Mental Health Considerations and Fatigue Management

Living with Marfan syndrome imposes substantial psychological burdens on patients and families, with recent research documenting significant associations between Marfan syndrome diagnosis and psychological symptoms including fatigue, anxiety, and depression.[42][33] A cross-sectional study evaluating two cohorts of Marfan syndrome patients compared with healthy controls demonstrated that severe fatigue was experienced by 37% of the total MFS cohort, a substantially higher prevalence than in general population controls.[42] Multivariate analysis revealed that anxiety was significantly associated with chronic pain (p=0.022) and symptoms of depression were associated with unemployment (p=0.024).[42] Interestingly, exploratory analysis suggested a trend toward reduced severe fatigue in patients using losartan (p=0.084), though use of beta-blockers was not associated with fatigue levels, potentially suggesting that losartan's superior tolerability compared with beta-blockers may extend to fatigue symptoms.[42]

These findings underscore the importance of screening for psychological symptoms in all Marfan syndrome patients and providing appropriate psychological interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy when indicated. The chronic nature of Marfan syndrome, the need for lifelong medical treatment, the ways Marfan syndrome affects appearance and physical function, limitations on physical activity that can restrict social interactions, and family planning stressors all contribute to psychological burden that warrants clinician attention.[33] A holistic approach to Marfan syndrome management that addresses not only the cardiovascular and skeletal manifestations but also the psychological impact of the diagnosis is essential for optimizing quality of life and adherence to medical therapy.

## Additional Therapeutic Considerations and Emerging Approaches

### Physical Activity and Exercise: Moderate-Intensity Training as Adjunctive Therapy

Historically, recommendations for Marfan syndrome patients emphasized restriction of physical activity out of concern that vigorous exercise might precipitate aortic dissection. However, recent investigations in mouse models of Marfan syndrome and preliminary human studies have suggested that carefully structured, moderate-intensity exercise programs may provide unexpected benefits including improved aortic architecture and function.[30] In one comprehensive study using the Fbn1^C1039G/+^ mouse model, physical activity through forced treadmill or voluntary wheel running dramatically improved the structural architecture of the aorta, reducing mechanical tensions and halting progressive dilation while even reducing aortic diameter.[30] Mechanistically, moderate-intensity training (at approximately 55% of VO2 max) significantly reduced expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 proteins in aortic tissue, while high-intensity exercises (75-85% VO2 max) paradoxically induced aortic aneurysm formation.[30]

These findings suggest a bimodal dose-response relationship whereby moderate-intensity exercise provides protective benefits through reduced MMP expression and improved elastic fiber integrity, while excessive intensity can be detrimental.[30] The practical implication of these findings is that Marfan syndrome patients should not necessarily be discouraged from all physical activity; rather, working closely with physical therapists and cardiologists to identify safe, moderate-intensity exercises and sports may provide meaningful cardiovascular benefits beyond the general health advantages of physical conditioning.[33]

### Gene Therapy: Future Directions in Disease-Modifying Treatment

Gene therapy represents an exciting frontier in Marfan syndrome therapeutics, offering the potential to address the fundamental genetic defect underlying the disease by modifying cells to produce therapeutic effects.[29][40] This approach encompasses several distinct strategies, each with unique advantages and limitations. DNA and RNA-based gene silencing technologies, including antisense oligonucleotides, small interfering RNAs, and microRNAs, contain specific sequences complementary to disease-related target mRNAs, allowing selective silencing of pathogenic gene expression.[29] Transcription factor decoy oligodeoxynucleotides (TFD) compete in the cytoplasm or nucleus with promoter regions to bind activated transcription factors, thereby interfering with aberrant expression of disease-related genes affected by specific transcription factor pathways.[29] These consensus TFD oligonucleotides have been successfully tested in preclinical animal studies and represent candidates that could potentially be directly introduced into initial human clinical trials to assess safety and efficacy.[29]

Delivery of gene therapies represents one of the major technical challenges in translating these approaches to clinical application. Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) have emerged as preferred vectors for many gene therapy applications due to their established safety and efficacy across numerous ongoing clinical trials and at least eight human diseases.[29] AAV-based vectors efficiently deliver both siRNA and RNA TFD into dividing and nondividing mammalian cells, making them particularly suitable for delivering therapeutic genetic material to vascular smooth muscle cells in the aortic wall.[29] Notably, AAV transduction does not result in intimal hyperplasia in experimental Marfan aortic grafts, representing a favorable safety profile.[29] Importantly, local treatment of the ascending aorta through catheter-based delivery or surgical approaches appears feasible without risking systemic adverse reactions, potentially allowing therapeutic genetic material to be delivered directly to affected tissues.[29]

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology has successfully repaired FBN1 pathogenic loci in Marfan syndrome patient-derived cells in multiple laboratories.[40] When CRISPR-Cas9 technology was applied to repair FBN1 mutations in induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from Marfan syndrome patients, genetically corrected cell lines exhibited normal karyotypes and maintained trilineage differentiation capabilities.[40] Off-target mutagenesis represents one principal concern in genome editing, but characterization of repaired cells has demonstrated absence of off-targets or insertion deletions, indicating that current CRISPR-Cas9 approaches can achieve safe and reliable repair of FBN1 mutations.[40] Base editing (BE) represents an alternative genome editing approach that avoids double-strand breaks and reduces off-target risks compared with standard CRISPR-Cas9, offering potentially enhanced safety.[40]

The application of iPSC technology to Marfan syndrome research has generated considerable optimism regarding understanding of disease pathogenesis and identification of new therapeutic strategies. Researchers have utilized iPSC-derived vascular smooth muscle cell models to screen thousands of small molecules and identify recurrent therapeutic targets, including glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β) as a potential drug target that has subsequently been pursued through inhibitor development.[40] These pluripotent stem cell–based approaches provide powerful platforms for disease modeling, target identification, and preclinical validation of therapeutic interventions before translation to human trials.

### Nutritional and Metabolic Approaches: Emerging Investigation of Ketogenic Diet

A novel approach currently under investigation in an exploratory clinical trial involves assessment of whether carefully supervised ketogenic diet can safely help people with Marfan syndrome.[8] This pilot clinical trial represents recognition that altered glucose metabolism may contribute to weakened aortic contraction in Marfan syndrome, with the hypothesis that restoring metabolic balance in artery wall cells might stabilize the aorta and inform future drug development.[44] While this nutritional approach remains highly experimental and results from rigorous clinical trials are not yet available, the theoretical rationale and exploratory clinical investigation highlight the evolving understanding of Marfan syndrome pathophysiology and the diverse therapeutic approaches currently being explored.[8][44]

## Conclusion and Implications for Future Clinical Practice

Marfan syndrome represents a complex genetic disorder requiring sophisticated and increasingly personalized approaches to pharmacological management. The landscape of available therapeutic options has evolved dramatically over the past two decades, progressing from purely hemodynamic management with beta-blockers to mechanism-based approaches targeting the underlying TGF-β dysregulation that drives aortic pathology. Current standard of care involves combination therapy with beta-blockers and angiotensin receptor blockers, with individual patient data meta-analyses demonstrating that this combination provides substantial reductions in aortic root growth rates that, when sustained over multiple years, are expected to delay or potentially eliminate the need for surgical aortic root replacement in many patients.[7][7][21][46] Both beta-blockers and ARBs provide approximately 50% reductions in the annual rate of aortic root Z score increase compared with untreated controls, and the additive effects of combination therapy suggest even greater protective benefit.[7][7][21][46]

Future refinement of Marfan syndrome management will likely involve several developments. First, emerging biomarkers may enable identification of patients at particularly high risk for rapid disease progression, allowing more intensive pharmacological and potentially surgical intervention in this higher-risk subgroup while avoiding unnecessary aggressive treatment in patients with slower disease progression.[44] Second, the completion of ongoing clinical trials investigating ARNIs, novel molecular targets such as PIEZO1, and multitarget combination approaches will establish whether incremental benefits beyond standard dual therapy with beta-blockers and ARBs can be achieved.[1][40] Third, gene therapy approaches and CRISPR-Cas9-based corrections of FBN1 mutations, while still in early developmental stages, may eventually provide disease-modifying interventions capable of halting or even reversing Marfan syndrome pathology at its fundamental molecular roots.[29][40]

Critical gaps remain in our understanding of optimal management strategies for special populations including pregnant women, pediatric patients, and patients with specific comorbidities or drug intolerances. The discovery of the paradoxical harmful effects of calcium channel blockers and the FDA warning regarding fluoroquinolone-induced aortic complications underscore the importance of ongoing pharmacovigilance and mechanistic investigation to identify potentially harmful therapies that might otherwise be prescribed to Marfan syndrome patients. The substantial psychological burden associated with Marfan syndrome, including high rates of fatigue, anxiety, and depression, demands that future management approaches incorporate screening for and treatment of psychological symptoms as part of comprehensive care strategies.[42][33]

The promise of future gene therapy, combined with ongoing refinement of current medical therapies and identification of novel therapeutic targets, offers hope that the life expectancy and quality of life for patients with Marfan syndrome will continue to improve substantially in the coming decades. Current standard of care—combination therapy with beta-blockers and ARBs, careful avoidance of contraindicated medications such as calcium channel blockers and fluoroquinolones, regular cardiovascular monitoring, and integrated multidisciplinary care addressing the multisystem manifestations of this disease—provides a rational, evidence-based approach to reducing premature morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable population. Ongoing clinical trials and mechanistic investigations will further refine these approaches and expand the therapeutic armamentarium available to clinicians managing this challenging genetic disorder.
