# Drug Therapies for Medium-Chain Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Deficiency: A Comprehensive Review of Approved, Investigational, and Emerging Treatments

Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCADD) represents the most common inherited metabolic disorder of fatty acid beta-oxidation, affecting patients across all age groups with potentially life-threatening metabolic consequences[1][1]. The current therapeutic landscape for MCADD reflects a critical clinical gap: despite the identification of MCADD decades ago and its inclusion in newborn screening programs in most developed nations, no disease-modifying pharmaceutical therapies have received regulatory approval specifically for this indication. Instead, clinical management has remained fundamentally dependent upon dietary intervention, with treatment strategies focusing on preventing metabolic decompensation through frequent feeding schedules, carbohydrate supplementation, and the careful avoidance of prolonged fasting periods[1][6][10]. However, recent years have witnessed substantial progress in the development of novel pharmacological approaches, including anaplerotic therapies, nitrogen scavengers repurposed from other metabolic disorders, and investigational gene therapy vectors. This comprehensive report synthesizes current knowledge regarding all available and emerging drug therapies for MCADD, examining approved medications, active clinical trials, repurposing candidates, contraindicated agents, and combination treatment strategies supported by contemporary evidence.

## Current Regulatory Status and the Absence of Disease-Specific Approved Therapies

The pharmaceutical management of MCADD currently presents a paradoxical situation wherein no drugs have received regulatory approval specifically for the treatment of this disease, despite its identification as a treatable metabolic disorder with a well-characterized molecular basis[1][3]. The ACADM gene, located on chromosome 1p31, encodes the medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase enzyme, and pathogenic variants in this gene result in impaired capacity to metabolize medium-chain fatty acids, leading to the characteristic clinical presentation of hypoketotic hypoglycemia during periods of metabolic stress[1][6][18]. The molecular confirmation of biallelic ACADM mutations, combined with biochemical evidence of accumulated C8-acylcarnitine (octanoylcarnitine) in plasma and elevated C8/C10 ratios in tandem mass spectrometry analysis, provides definitive diagnosis of the condition[1][18][18]. Nevertheless, the standard of care remains entirely dietary in nature, relying upon frequent meals containing high carbohydrate content and restricted fat intake not exceeding thirty percent of total daily energy, supplemented in some cases with medium-chain triglyceride oil and uncooked cornstarch to maintain blood glucose during nocturnal fasting periods[6][10][10].

This therapeutic void reflects both the natural history of MCADD when appropriately managed with dietary intervention alone and the substantial technical and regulatory challenges associated with developing pharmaceuticals for rare genetic disorders. Prognosis for MCADD patients identified through newborn screening has improved dramatically since the implementation of such programs, as early diagnosis and initiation of dietary management prevents the acute metabolic crises that historically claimed lives and caused permanent neurological damage[1][10][11]. However, evidence from real-world clinical experience demonstrates that dietary management alone does not entirely eliminate metabolic decompensation episodes, particularly during intercurrent viral illnesses when children experience decreased appetite and concurrent increased energy demands due to fever[6][11][33]. Additionally, adolescents and adults with MCADD continue to experience symptomatic episodes characterized by profound fatigue, muscle pain, and exercise intolerance, suggesting that current standard therapies, while life-saving, do not fully address the underlying metabolic dysfunction[1][1][31].

The regulatory classification of MCADD as an orphan disease, affecting fewer than 100,000 individuals in the United States and comparably small populations in other developed nations, fundamentally shapes the pharmaceutical development landscape[1][1]. Orphan drug designation confers certain regulatory advantages, including expedited review pathways and extended periods of market exclusivity for approved therapies, yet the limited patient population remains economically unattractive for many pharmaceutical developers[1]. Consequently, most pharmaceutical development efforts for MCADD have been undertaken by specialized companies focused on rare genetic disorders or through investigator-initiated clinical research supported by academic medical centers and advocacy organizations[1][9].

## Approved Therapies for Related Conditions with Potential MCADD Applications: Triheptanoin

While no pharmaceuticals have received regulatory approval specifically for MCADD, one medication has received FDA approval for a related rare metabolic disorder that shares significant pathophysiological overlap with MCADD and is currently undergoing clinical investigation for MCADD treatment. Triheptanoin, marketed under the trade name **Dojolvi** (UX007 in clinical trial nomenclature), received FDA approval on July 31, 2020, for the treatment of long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD) in pediatric and adult patients[7][15][15]. This approval represents the first and only FDA-approved pharmaceutical therapy for any form of fatty acid oxidation disorder, marking a watershed moment in the therapeutic management of these metabolic diseases[7][15][15].

### Mechanism of Action and Pharmacology of Triheptanoin

Triheptanoin is a synthetic, highly purified, medium odd-chain triglyceride composed of three heptanoic acid (C7) molecules esterified to glycerol[15][31]. The heptanoic acid component, also designated as a seven-carbon fatty acid or C7, represents a unique pharmacological entity distinct from standard medium-chain triglycerides (which typically contain even-chain fatty acids such as C6, C8, C10, and C12) and from long-chain fatty acids (typically containing sixteen or more carbons)[13][15][15]. The therapeutic rationale for triheptanoin in fatty acid oxidation disorders derives from its capacity to provide anaplerotic substrates to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle or citric acid cycle[14][15][15]. During normal fatty acid oxidation, medium-chain and long-chain fatty acids undergo repeated cycles of beta-oxidation within mitochondria, producing acetyl-CoA units that enter the TCA cycle for complete oxidation and energy generation. However, in fatty acid oxidation disorders, this process becomes impaired, resulting in both insufficient energy production and accumulation of toxic fatty acid intermediates.

The unique property of heptanoic acid lies in its metabolism by short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (SCAD), a different enzyme than those responsible for metabolizing longer chain fatty acids[9][14]. Upon ingestion and absorption, triheptanoin is hydrolyzed to free heptanoic acid in the gastrointestinal tract and absorbed into the portal circulation. Once in hepatic mitochondria, heptanoic acid undergoes activation to heptanoyl-CoA, followed by sequential cycles of beta-oxidation by SCAD and related enzymes. Critically, this process yields propionyl-CoA units rather than the acetyl-CoA produced by oxidation of even-chain fatty acids[14][30]. Propionyl-CoA subsequently enters the TCA cycle via conversion to succinyl-CoA through the action of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, thereby providing direct anaplerotic substrates that replenish depleted TCA cycle intermediates[15][15][30]. This anaplerotic mechanism bypasses the enzymatic defect present in LC-FAOD patients, allowing energy generation to proceed via an alternative metabolic route.

### FDA Approval for Long-Chain Fatty Acid Oxidation Disorders and Clinical Evidence

The FDA approval of triheptanoin in 2020 was predicated upon evidence from two primary clinical trials demonstrating safety and efficacy in LC-FAOD patients[15][15]. The approval documents indicate that triheptanoin functions as a source of calories and fatty acids for treatment of pediatric and adult patients with molecularly confirmed long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, with approved dosing up to thirty-five percent of daily caloric intake[15][15]. Clinical trial evidence demonstrated that patients receiving triheptanoin experienced dramatic reductions in episodes of hypoglycemia, rhabdomyolysis, and other acute metabolic decompensation events compared to historical controls and period preceding treatment initiation[13][31].

A pivotal retrospective study examining the largest cohort of LC-FAOD patients treated with triheptanoin reported that treatment resulted in essentially complete elimination of hypoglycemic episodes in most patients studied[13]. This dramatic improvement in hypoglycemia prevention represents the most clinically significant benefit of triheptanoin therapy, as severe hypoglycemia serves as a primary driver of morbidity and mortality in fatty acid oxidation disorders[13][15]. Additionally, the same analysis demonstrated significant reductions in rhabdomyolysis episodes and associated hospitalizations, though episodes of muscle-related complications continued at a reduced frequency compared to baseline, suggesting that additional therapeutic interventions may be necessary to completely eliminate muscle manifestations[13].

More recent real-world evidence from Italian patients with LC-FAOD switching from standard medium-chain triglyceride oil supplementation to triheptanoin therapy documented striking clinical improvements[31]. Among nine LC-FAOD patients receiving mean triheptanoin doses of 1.5 ± 0.9 g/kg/day (representing approximately 23.9% ± 8.9% of total daily caloric intake) over a mean treatment duration of 2.2 ± 0.9 years, the number of intercurrent catabolic episodes during triheptanoin treatment decreased significantly from 22.0 ± 22.2 episodes during prior MCT oil therapy to 4.3 ± 5.3 episodes (p = 0.034)[31]. Furthermore, the number of metabolic decompensations requiring hospitalization fell from 18.3 ± 17.7 episodes during MCT therapy to 2.0 ± 2.5 episodes during triheptanoin treatment (p = 0.014)[31]. Mean creatine kinase levels measured outside acute metabolic decompensation episodes were lower during triheptanoin therapy compared to MCT oil treatment in seven of nine patients, and no intensive care unit admissions were required during the triheptanoin treatment period[31].

### Triheptanoin Investigation for MCADD: Preclinical and Clinical Evidence

Despite initial biochemical concerns regarding the potential for triheptanoin to accumulate toxic metabolites in MCADD patients due to the theoretical inability of MCAD to metabolize heptanoyl-CoA, recent preclinical investigations have revealed that MCAD-deficient cells can indeed utilize C7 through the action of short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase[14][30][36]. Detailed mechanistic studies demonstrated that MCAD-deficient fibroblasts derived from patients carrying the most common MCADD mutation (ACADM G985A/G985A) were able to utilize heptanoic acid as an energy substrate and generate succinyl-CoA intermediates that improved cellular energy production measured by oxygen consumption rate and mitochondrial protein succinylation[14][36]. These findings directly contradicted the prior assumption that triheptanoin was contraindicated in MCADD and opened the therapeutic possibility of repurposing this FDA-approved medication for MCADD treatment.

Animal model evidence provided additional support for triheptanoin efficacy in MCADD. In studies utilizing Acadm-deficient mice, treatment with triheptanoin for seven days resulted in dose-dependent increases in plasma levels of heptanoyl-, valeryl-, and propionylcarnitine, indicating efficient metabolism of the medication[36]. Critically, these metabolic changes correlated with resolution of hepatic pathology, including reversal of hepatic steatosis, increased hepatic glycogen content, and enhanced hepatocyte protein succinylation—all markers indicating improved energy homeostasis[36]. These findings in preclinical models strongly suggested potential therapeutic benefit in human MCADD patients.

Building upon this preclinical evidence, clinical investigation of triheptanoin in MCADD patients has progressed to human trials. A Phase 2 clinical trial of triheptanoin in MCADD (Study Number STUDY23040121, also designated UX007-IST237) was conducted at UPMC Children's Hospital Pittsburgh under the direction of Georgianne Arnold, M.D.[9]. This study enrolled up to eight MCADD patients aged sixteen years and older and assessed the safety and efficacy of triheptanoin by conducting baseline evaluations measuring glucose, acylcarnitines, acylglycines, free fatty acids, and beta-hydroxybutyrate, followed by repeat measurements after a standardized twenty-four-hour fasting protocol[9].

A more recent clinical trial, registered as NCT06067802, represents the most current investigation of triheptanoin in MCADD[2][2]. This study specifically examines triheptanoin for the prevention of hypoglycemia in MCADD patients, enrolling participants aged sixteen years and older with molecular confirmation of MCADD. The study protocol requires three overnight admissions at the Clinical and Translational Research Center at UPMC Children's Hospital Pittsburgh, with a total study duration of ten weeks[2][2]. Study participants undergo fasting up to twenty-four hours in duration, with blood draws collected before, during, and after fasting to assess biochemical markers. Triheptanoin dosing begins at 0.2 grams per kilogram per day, gradually increasing to a maximum dose of 1.0 grams per kilogram per day divided into three or four daily doses to avoid gastric upset[2][2]. Subjects return for repeat overnight admissions at weeks five and nine, with follow-up contact by study staff at week ten to assess for adverse effects[2][2].

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the MCADD triheptanoin trial provide important clinical context regarding which patient populations are considered appropriate for this investigational therapy[2][2]. Inclusion criteria specify molecular confirmation of MCADD, age sixteen years or older, ability to comply with study procedures including overnight admission and IV catheter placement, and negative pregnancy testing for females of childbearing potential with commitment to effective contraception[2][2]. Exclusion criteria carefully eliminate patients with evidence of liver disease (AST or ALT elevation greater than 1.5 times upper limit of normal), active infection, pancreatic insufficiency or concurrent use of pancreatic lipase inhibitors such as orlistat, or diabetes mellitus or concurrent use of medications that can cause hypoglycemia[2][2]. These exclusions reflect concern regarding potential drug interactions and the need to maintain clear metabolic parameters during the investigational trial.

## Nitrogen Scavengers and Repurposed Therapies: Glycerol Phenylbutyrate

Beyond triheptanoin, another investigational approach to MCADD treatment involves the use of nitrogen scavengers—medications traditionally developed and approved for the treatment of urea cycle disorders (UCDs) that may provide secondary benefit in MCADD by addressing hyperammonemia that can develop during acute metabolic decompensation. **Glycerol phenylbutyrate**, marketed under the trade name **Ravicti** and designated as an investigational therapy for MCADD under clinical trial identifier NCT02246218, represents this repurposing approach[1][8].

### Mechanism and Pharmacology of Nitrogen Scavengers

Glycerol phenylbutyrate, also known as glyceryl phenylbutyrate or 3-phenylbutyrate, functions as a nitrogen scavenger that operates through a mechanism distinct from its intended mechanism in urea cycle disorders but potentially beneficial in MCADD[1][8][29]. As a nitrogen scavenger, glycerol phenylbutyrate acts to conjugate with glycine to form hippurate, which is subsequently excreted in the urine, thereby effectively removing nitrogenous waste products from the bloodstream[29]. Each molecule of sodium benzoate or sodium phenylbutyrate effectively eliminates one mole of ammonia through this conjugation mechanism, providing an alternative pathway for nitrogen disposal in patients with impaired urea cycle function[27][29].

The theoretical rationale for applying nitrogen scavenger therapy to MCADD patients derives from clinical observations that some MCADD patients, particularly those with severe enzyme deficiency and liver involvement, develop secondary hyperammonemia during acute metabolic decompensation[1][6][10][6]. Hyperammonemia in MCADD likely arises through multiple mechanisms, including hepatic dysfunction with impaired ammonia metabolism, increased amino acid catabolism during metabolic crisis, and potential mitochondrial dysfunction affecting the urea cycle itself[1]. The presence of hyperammonemia correlates with particularly severe manifestations of MCADD, including hepatomegaly, supraventricular tachycardia, and neurological complications[1].

### Clinical Trial Evidence for Ravicti in MCADD

Glycerol phenylbutyrate has been evaluated as an experimental treatment for MCADD patients specifically to improve MCAD enzyme stability and reduce the accumulation of toxic metabolites[1]. Ravicti had previously received FDA approval for treatment of urea cycle disorders and was selected for MCADD investigation based on preliminary evidence suggesting potential benefit in managing secondary hyperammonemia[1][8][8].

The clinical investigation of Ravicti in MCADD was conducted as an open-label study consisting of a transition period to Ravicti followed by a safety extension period lasting at least six months and up to twenty-four months[8][8]. The study design and enrollment were severely limited by sample size, significantly restricting the researchers' ability to perform many comprehensive analyses evaluating the drug's efficacy in MCADD patients[1]. This sample size limitation represents a common challenge in rare disease drug development, wherein the scarcity of affected individuals makes definitive efficacy demonstrations statistically challenging[1].

Despite these limitations, clinical trials examining Ravicti in MCADD have progressed with structured study protocols. A more comprehensive clinical investigation has examined phenylbutyrate therapy for MCADD in patients with specific genetic backgrounds[9]. The study design included two cohorts with up to twelve subjects per cohort, with subjects randomized to one of three dose regimens within each cohort[9]. Cohort 1 included subjects aged sixteen years or older receiving doses of 3.0 g/m² per day once daily, 3.0 g/m² per day divided twice daily, or 4.0 g/m² per day divided twice daily[9]. Cohort 2 included subjects aged ten to fifteen years with similar dose regimens[9]. Importantly, inclusion criteria specified that subjects must carry at least one copy of the c.985A>G ACADM variant (also designated as the K329E mutation), the most common pathogenic variant among MCADD patients of northern European ancestry[9].

The inclusion criterion specifying the c.985A>G variant reflects an important stratification principle in MCADD research: this particular mutation causes a folding defect in the MCAD enzyme rather than absent protein expression, and protein-misfolding conditions may theoretically respond differently to pharmacological chaperones or related interventions compared to complete loss-of-function mutations[9]. However, at present, Ravicti therapy for MCADD remains investigational with no regulatory approval for this indication, and the clinical utility of this repurposed therapy requires further investigation in adequately powered clinical trials.

## L-Carnitine Supplementation: Controversial Supportive Therapy

Dietary supplementation with **L-carnitine**, an amino acid essential for the transport of long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane, represents one of the oldest and most controversial interventions in MCADD management[1][5][5][23][28]. Carnitine supplementation has been historically prescribed as part of a long-term management strategy for MCADD patients, typically at doses of 100 mg/kg/day, with some patients requiring lower doses of 50 mg/kg/day due to adverse effects including a characteristic fish-smelling body odor from trimethylamine formation[1][5].

### Biochemical Rationale and Current Evidence for L-Carnitine

The theoretical rationale for carnitine supplementation in MCADD derives from biochemical observations that MCADD patients accumulate toxic acylcarnitine metabolites during metabolic stress[1][5][5][23]. In healthy metabolism, carnitine exists primarily in the form of free carnitine in plasma and tissues, with acylcarnitines representing metabolic intermediates that are typically rapidly regenerated to free carnitine through the action of carnitine palmitoyltransferase and related enzymes. However, in MCADD and other fatty acid oxidation disorders, ineffective processing of medium-chain fatty acids results in aberrant accumulation of acylcarnitine species, particularly octanoylcarnitine (C8)[1][5][5][23]. Carnitine supplementation theoretically enhances the esterification of accumulated fatty acid intermediates to carnitine, forming acylcarnitines that can be excreted in urine, thereby reducing the accumulation of potentially toxic fatty acid species within mitochondria and tissues[1][5][5][23].

However, contemporary evidence increasingly questions whether this theoretical benefit translates into meaningful clinical advantage. A landmark study examining L-carnitine supplementation at 50 mg/kg/day during prolonged moderate-intensity exercise in five MCADD patients and three control subjects demonstrated equivocal findings regarding clinical benefit[5][5]. While the study confirmed that octanoylcarnitine levels in plasma were significantly higher in patients receiving carnitine supplementation, suggesting increased clearance of accumulating acylcarnitines, both carnitine-supplemented and non-supplemented patient groups demonstrated elevated free carnitine levels in plasma and urine[5][5]. Critically, the investigators found no significant biochemical advantage for carnitine supplementation in MCADD patients, and all patients were able to complete the exercise test without apparent clinical or biochemical adverse effects even without L-carnitine supplementation[5][5]. The data suggested that MCADD patients possess the capacity to increase carnitine biosynthesis during exercise to compensate for carnitine losses, potentially rendering exogenous supplementation superfluous[5][5].

An earlier pilot study investigating L-carnitine effects on exercise tolerance in four clinically asymptomatic MCADD patients aged eight to twenty years produced more favorable results[23]. These investigators reported that four weeks of L-carnitine supplementation at 100 mg/kg/day resulted in biologically significant improvements in peak oxygen uptake (eighteen to thirty-two percent improvement), VO2 at a heart rate of 170 beats per minute (fifteen to twenty-three percent improvement), VO2 at anaerobic threshold (twenty-seven to forty-two percent improvement), and oxygen pulse (ten to thirty-two percent improvement)[23]. These improvements in exercise tolerance measures suggested that carnitine supplementation might enhance exercise capacity through improved intramitochondrial homeostasis[23]. However, the small sample size and open-label design limited the strength of these findings.

### Secondary Carnitine Deficiency and Questionable Efficacy of Supplementation

A large retrospective observational study examining carnitine supplementation in MCADD patients has raised significant concerns regarding the evidence base supporting routine carnitine therapy[28]. This analysis of 1,228 visits by 93 MCADD patients at a single referral center (University Medical Center Groningen, Netherlands) between October 1994 and October 2019 revealed that secondary carnitine deficiency (SCD), defined as reduced plasma free carnitine concentration, developed in more than sixty percent of the cohort during follow-up[28]. Notably, SCD occurred predominantly in patients with severe MCADD, with highest frequencies observed in preschoolers and adults[28].

Critically, carnitine supplementation and the presence of secondary carnitine deficiency were completely unrelated to important clinical outcomes including the frequency and duration of acute unscheduled preventive hospital visits, exercise tolerance, fatigue, and muscle pain[28]. The relative risk for fatigue, muscle ache, or exercise intolerance was equal between persons with and without SCD (RR 1.6, 95% CI 0.48–5.10, p = 0.4662)[28]. Most striking, among nine MCADD patients who never received carnitine supplementation throughout the entire follow-up period, several (six of nine) developed SCD during multiple visits, yet no episodes of acute severe metabolic crisis were recorded in these non-supplemented individuals[28]. These findings suggest that secondary carnitine deficiency, while biochemically documented in MCADD patients, may not require supplementation to prevent serious clinical complications.

The negative consequences of carnitine supplementation warrant consideration in clinical decision-making[28]. Carnitine supplementation may potentially aggravate tissue accumulation of toxic acylcarnitines in MCADD through mechanisms observed in other fatty acid oxidation disorders such as very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (VLCADD) in animal models[28]. Additionally, carnitine supplementation increases circulating levels of trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a derivative of gut microbiota metabolism of carnitine that has been recently identified as a significant independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease[28]. Given these considerations and the absence of robust evidence demonstrating clinical benefit, routine carnitine supplementation in MCADD patients appears increasingly difficult to justify, and the clinical decision to supplement should be individualized based on specific patient factors rather than universally recommended[1][28].

## Contraindicated Medications and Agents That Worsen MCADD

Understanding medications and substances that are contraindicated or known to precipitate metabolic decompensation in MCADD patients represents an essential component of comprehensive MCADD pharmacotherapy. Several medications and substances are explicitly contraindicated or require careful avoidance in MCADD management due to their capacity to exacerbate metabolic dysfunction and precipitate life-threatening metabolic crises.

### Aspirin and Salicylates: Mechanisms of Metabolic Exacerbation

**Aspirin** (acetylsalicylic acid) represents perhaps the most important contraindicated medication in MCADD and other fatty acid oxidation disorders, with a long-recognized association with severe metabolic decompensation and Reye syndrome-like presentations[10][16][10]. The contraindication against aspirin use in MCADD patients, while clinically well-established, was incompletely understood mechanistically until recent investigations elucidated the biochemical basis for aspirin's harmful effects in these patients[16].

Contemporary research has demonstrated that aspirin increases mitochondrial long-chain fatty acid oxidation through multiple mechanisms[16]. In cell culture experiments using human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells, aspirin at a concentration of 5 millimolar significantly increased the rate of tritiated palmitate oxidation to tritiated water as early as three hours after addition[16]. This accelerated fatty acid oxidation persisted and intensified with continued aspirin exposure for twenty-four hours[16]. The mechanism underlying aspirin's pro-oxidative effects appears distinct from previously described salicylate mechanisms, as salicylic acid alone (aspirin without the acetyl group) produced opposite effects, inhibiting fatty acid oxidation rather than promoting it[16]. The critical difference between these two compounds involves direct protein acetylation by aspirin, wherein the acetyl group of aspirin directly acetylates lysine residues on mitochondrial proteins including fatty acid oxidation enzymes through non-enzymatic acylation[16].

While aspirin directly acetylates fatty acid oxidation enzymes, these acetylation modifications do not directly alter enzyme activity[16]. Instead, aspirin's pro-oxidative effect operates through effects on mitochondrial morphology and respiration[16]. Aspirin treatment causes mitochondrial fragmentation from an interconnected network to a more fragmented state, which associates with reduced utilization of carbohydrate energy sources such as pyruvate and succinate[16]. This metabolic shift toward increased fatty acid oxidation likely occurs as a compensatory response to altered mitochondrial respiration[16]. For MCADD patients with already-impaired capacity to metabolize medium-chain fatty acids, this aspirin-induced increase in overall fatty acid oxidation exacerbates metabolic dysfunction by overwhelming the limited enzymatic capacity available for fatty acid catabolism[16].

The clinical consequence of aspirin ingestion in MCADD patients can be catastrophic. Aspirin has been demonstrated to exacerbate MCAD deficiency by increasing mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, thereby dramatically increasing the metabolic stress on the already-compromised beta-oxidation pathway[10]. In some cases, aspirin exposure has preceded fatal metabolic decompensation in MCADD patients[10]. Therefore, aspirin and aspirin-containing products are explicitly contraindicated in all MCADD patients, and alternative analgesics and antipyretics such as acetaminophen should be universally substituted[6][10][10][10].

### Sodium Valproate and Anticonvulsant Medications

**Sodium valproate**, an antiepileptic drug frequently prescribed for seizure disorder management, has been identified as a medication capable of precipitating severe metabolic decompensation in patients with underlying fatty acid oxidation disorders including MCADD[12]. Case reports describe patients with previously undiagnosed or mild MCADD presenting with severe rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria within days of initiating sodium valproate therapy[12]. A well-documented case involved a twenty-nine-year-old female who presented with rhabdomyolysis shortly after starting sodium valproate, with thorough investigation eventually revealing an underlying mitochondrial disorder characterized by impaired fatty acid oxidation[12].

The mechanism by which sodium valproate precipitates metabolic decompensation in fatty acid oxidation disorders involves impairment of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and potentially direct inhibition of relevant beta-oxidation enzymes[12]. Sodium valproate is a well-known antagonist in patients with underlying mitochondrial diseases and disorders of fatty acid oxidation[12]. Given this risk, sodium valproate should be avoided in MCADD patients, and alternative anticonvulsant agents should be selected when seizure management is necessary[12].

### Medium-Chain Triglycerides as Dietary Sources and Manufactured Products

While medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil supplementation at controlled doses represents an accepted part of standard MCADD management, certain dietary sources and manufactured products containing significant concentrations of medium-chain fatty acids should be explicitly avoided[6][10][26][10]. **Coconut oil** contains approximately fifty-five percent medium-chain fatty acids and is expressly contraindicated in MCADD diets[26][10]. Similarly, **palm kernel oil** and other tropical oils contain high proportions of medium-chain fatty acids and should be avoided[26][10]. Infant formulas that list coconut oil or palm kernel oil as primary fat sources represent particular concerns in infant MCADD patients, and formula selection should explicitly verify the absence of coconut or tropical oil-derived medium-chain fatty acids[10][10].

The rationale for avoiding these dietary sources while permitting controlled MCT oil supplementation reflects dose and specificity considerations. Uncontrolled dietary medium-chain fat intake from coconut oil and similar sources contributes unpredictable loads of medium-chain fatty acids that can precipitate elevation of toxic acylcarnitine species such as octanoylcarnitine (C8)[26][10]. In contrast, controlled MCT oil supplementation provides dietary energy from medium-chain triglycerides at specified, manageable concentrations, and this supplementation actually suppresses fatty acid oxidation from endogenous storage fat by providing dietary medium-chain fat substrate, thereby reducing demand on the impaired beta-oxidation pathway[1][24][10].

## Emerging and Investigational Therapies: Gene Therapy and Anaplerotic Alternatives

Beyond currently available pharmaceutical approaches, several investigational therapeutic strategies are in various stages of preclinical development and early clinical evaluation, potentially representing the future of MCADD treatment. These emerging therapies include gene therapy approaches targeting skeletal muscle beta-oxidation and novel anaplerotic compounds distinct from triheptanoin.

### Gene Therapy Approaches for MCADD

Gene therapy provides a theoretically attractive approach to MCADD treatment, with preclinical evidence demonstrating that even moderate increases in MCAD enzyme expression can substantially improve patient outcomes[1][1]. In situ gene therapies have been explored for MCADD, with particular focus on skeletal muscle tissue as a primary location of beta-oxidation[1][1]. The molecular basis for MCADD—genetic variants in the single ACADM gene encoding MCAD—renders this condition particularly amenable to gene therapy approaches, as correction of this single gene defect could theoretically restore enzymatic function in affected tissues[1][1].

The majority of symptomatic MCADD individuals harbor single point mutations rather than large deletions, making them potentially suitable candidates for gene therapy approaches targeting correction of missense mutations[1][1]. However, preliminary investigations of gene therapy in related metabolic contexts have revealed significant challenges. A study examining a gene therapy approach targeting medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase in the context of diabetes-induced cardiac pathology demonstrated that while cardiac-selective adeno-associated viral vectors encoding MCAD (rAAV6:MCAD) successfully increased MCAD mRNA and protein expression in non-diabetic hearts, translating this approach to the diseased diabetic heart proved more challenging[19][19]. Specifically, rAAV6:MCAD delivery in diabetic hearts increased MCAD mRNA expression but did not significantly increase protein expression, enzyme activity, or improve diabetes-induced cardiac pathology[19][19]. The study concluded that decreased uptake of AAV viral vectors in the diabetic heart, compared to non-diabetic hearts, may have implications for clinical translation of AAV therapies[19][19].

These findings suggest that while gene therapy remains a promising research direction for MCADD, substantial technical challenges remain regarding achieving adequate tissue-specific transduction, particularly in the context of metabolic disease, and ensuring sustained, functional enzyme expression at therapeutically relevant levels. Nevertheless, gene therapy development for MCADD continues to represent an active research area, and multiple research groups worldwide are investigating AAV vectors and related platforms for MCADD treatment[1][1].

### Medium Branched-Chain Fatty Acids as Anaplerotic Alternatives

Beyond triheptanoin, recent preclinical investigations have identified additional anaplerotic fatty acid compounds with potential therapeutic utility in MCADD. These novel compounds, designated as medium branched-chain fatty acids (MBCFAs), including 2,6-dimethylheptanoic acid (dMC7), 6-amino-2,4-dimethylheptanoic acid (AdMC7), and 4,8-dimethylnonanoic acid (dMC9), have been evaluated for their capacity to provide anaplerotic therapy in MCADD cell models and animal studies[14][30][36].

In detailed biochemical investigations using MCADD fibroblasts derived from patients homozygous for the most common ACADM mutation (c.985A>G), these MBCFAs demonstrated the capacity to increase cellular oxygen consumption rates, indicative of enhanced oxidative metabolism[14][30][36]. Treatment with various MBCFA concentrations increased extracellular acidification rates (ECAR) in MCADD cell lines, reflecting enhanced glycolytic activity consistent with improved energy homeostasis[14][30]. Remarkably, treatment with dMC9 proved most consistent at elevating ECAR, suggesting potential superior therapeutic efficacy[14][30][36].

In vivo investigations in Acadm-deficient mice treated with triheptanoin (the C7 form of these compounds) for seven days demonstrated dose-dependent increases in plasma levels of heptanoyl-, valeryl-, and propionylcarnitine, indicating efficient metabolism of the medication[36]. Critically, these metabolic changes correlated with resolution of hepatic pathology, including reversal of hepatic steatosis, increased hepatic glycogen content, and enhanced hepatocyte protein succinylation—all markers indicating improved energy homeostasis[36]. These results strongly support continued investigation of MBCFAs and triheptanoin as potential therapeutic agents for MCADD patients[14][30][36].

The theoretical advantage of MBCFAs such as dMC7, AdMC7, and dMC9 over C7 (triheptanoin) is their capacity to provide two molecules of propionyl-CoA per molecule of compound metabolized, compared to three propionyl-CoA units from triheptanoin[30]. This differential could theoretically allow achievement of therapeutic propionyl-CoA levels at approximately fifty percent of the C7 dose, potentially improving tolerability and clinical feasibility[30]. However, these compounds remain entirely investigational, and clinical trials in human MCADD patients have not yet been initiated.

## Management Principles for Acute Metabolic Decompensation and Emergency Pharmacotherapy

While chronic pharmacotherapy represents an important emerging frontier in MCADD management, the immediate treatment of acute metabolic decompensation episodes remains fundamentally dependent upon rapid glucose administration and supportive care rather than specific disease-modifying pharmaceuticals. Nevertheless, understanding the pharmacological principles underlying acute management is essential for comprehensive consideration of drug therapies in MCADD.

### Intravenous Glucose Administration as Acute Therapy

Acute metabolic decompensation in MCADD constitutes a medical emergency requiring immediate intervention[17][33][17]. When a symptomatic MCADD patient develops acute hypoglycemia, treatment with a bolus of 0.25 milligrams per kilogram of dextrose, not to exceed 25 grams, should be administered immediately[17]. Following hypoglycemia correction, intravenous glucose administration should be initiated immediately with 10% dextrose containing appropriate electrolytes at a rate of 1.5 times maintenance rate or 10-12 mg glucose per kilogram per minute to achieve and maintain a blood glucose level higher than 5 millimol/L (approximately 120-170 mg/dL)[10][17][10][33].

The pharmacological basis for intravenous glucose administration reflects the fundamental pathophysiology of MCADD: the inability to mobilize and oxidize endogenous fat stores during metabolic stress necessitates provision of alternative glucose substrate to prevent severe hypoglycemia and reverse catabolism[1][10][17][10]. Reversal of catabolism and prevention of hypoglycemia represents the immediate priority during acute episodes, and provision of glucose substrates for glycolysis and the citric acid cycle allows sufficient energy production and symptom reversal[1][1]. Importantly, simple carbohydrates administered by mouth may be given during milder episodes, but acute severe hypoglycemia and metabolic crises require parenteral glucose administration to achieve rapid, predictable glycemic correction[1][1].

### Electrolyte Management and Treatment of Secondary Metabolic Derangements

During acute metabolic decompensation, electrolyte and acid-base imbalances frequently develop secondary to metabolic dysfunction and require correction through appropriate intravenous fluid management[10][10]. Electrolyte abnormalities may include hypokalemia (low potassium) requiring supplementation, hyponatremia (low sodium), and other disturbances in electrolyte homeostasis[10][10]. Acid-base disturbances, including metabolic acidosis, may develop during severe metabolic decompensation and require correction through aggressive dextrose administration, which suppresses continued fatty acid mobilization and catabolism[10][10].

Additionally, appropriate treatment should be directed toward any precipitating factors that triggered the metabolic stress, such as prompt treatment of intercurrent infection with appropriate antibiotics when bacterial infection is suspected[10][10][33]. In cases where metabolic decompensation is precipitated by viral illness with fever, antipyretic management with acetaminophen (avoiding aspirin absolutely) and supportive care measures are indicated[10][10].

### Secondary Hyperammonemia and Potential Role for Ammonia-Lowering Strategies

In MCADD patients who develop secondary hyperammonemia during acute metabolic decompensation, potential consideration might be given to nitrogen scavenging approaches, though evidence regarding the utility of acute ammonia-lowering therapy in MCADD specifically remains limited[1][6][6]. While nitrogen scavengers such as sodium benzoate have demonstrated efficacy in rapidly lowering ammonia levels in acute hyperammonemia associated with urea cycle disorders, their application specifically to MCADD-associated hyperammonemia has not been rigorously investigated[27][29].

Hyperammonemia in MCADD likely represents a secondary manifestation of hepatic dysfunction and metabolic derangement rather than a primary target of therapy, and correction of the underlying metabolic decompensation through aggressive glucose administration and reversal of catabolism typically results in normalization of ammonia levels[1][6][6]. However, for MCADD patients who develop severe hyperammonemia with neurological manifestations during acute episodes, consideration of nitrogen scavenging therapy might be reasonable as an adjunctive measure pending the effects of standard glucose therapy, though such use would represent off-label application of medications developed for other indications[1][6].

## Dietary and Nutritional Supplementation Strategies: Adjunctive to Pharmacotherapy

While dietary management remains the cornerstone of MCADD treatment and is not strictly a "drug therapy," contemporary understanding of optimal nutritional management has evolved significantly, incorporating principles that complement and augment any future pharmacological interventions. Comprehensive nutritional management requires frequent meals, high carbohydrate content, fat restriction below thirty percent of total daily caloric intake, and potential supplementation with specific micronutrients and dietary compounds[1][6][10][24][10].

The fundamental dietary principle in MCADD involves avoidance of prolonged fasting through provision of frequent meals (every 2-3 hours in infancy, with less frequent meals as children age), nocturnal feeding or bedtime snacks containing complex carbohydrates, and potentially uncooked cornstarch supplementation (2 grams per kilogram) to maintain blood glucose during sleep periods[10][24][10]. A diet containing no more than thirty percent of total daily energy from fat is recommended, with explicit avoidance of coconut oil, palm kernel oil, and high-fat foods[10][24][10]. Complex carbohydrates from sources such as whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and fruits are preferentially recommended over simple sugars[10][24][10].

Cornstarch specifically deserves comment as a dietary supplement with pharmacological-like properties in MCADD management. Uncooked cornstarch produces a large amount of branched glucose chains that are hydrolyzed and released slowly, allowing normoglycemic levels to be maintained for six to eight hours, proving more effective than equivalent glucose dosing every three hours for preventing nocturnal hypoglycemia[10][24][10]. Cornstarch supplementation can typically be initiated from eight months of age when pancreatic enzymes develop adequate capacity for optimal cornstarch absorption[24].

## Conclusion: Current Landscape and Future Directions in MCADD Pharmacotherapy

The pharmaceutical landscape for medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency remains characterized by the absence of disease-specific approved therapies, with current treatment fundamentally dependent upon dietary intervention and emergency management of acute metabolic crises. However, substantial progress over the past decade has substantially altered the therapeutic trajectory for MCADD, introducing investigational agents and repurposed medications with genuine potential to improve clinical outcomes and reduce disease burden for patients and families affected by this condition.

Triheptanoin, already FDA-approved for long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, represents the most advanced investigational approach for MCADD, with preclinical evidence and early clinical trials demonstrating capability to provide anaplerotic substrates and improve energy production in MCADD patients[14][36]. Should clinical trials currently underway demonstrate safety and efficacy, triheptanoin could potentially receive expanded regulatory approval for MCADD treatment within the coming years, providing the first pharmaceutical therapy specifically targeting disease pathophysiology rather than merely supporting dietary management[2][2].

Beyond triheptanoin, emerging therapeutic approaches including glycerol phenylbutyrate for secondary hyperammonemia, novel medium branched-chain fatty acids providing enhanced anaplerotic potential, and gene therapy vectors targeting skeletal muscle MCAD expression represent future directions that may provide complementary or superior therapeutic options[1][14][19][30][36]. Continued investigation into the mechanistic basis of MCADD pathophysiology, including the role of mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neurological injury during metabolic crises, will likely identify additional therapeutic targets and drug candidates in coming years[1][1][1].

Critically, the evolving pharmaceutical landscape for MCADD underscores the importance of avoiding contraindicated agents, particularly aspirin and sodium valproate, which can precipitate life-threatening metabolic decompensation[10][16][10]. Reconsidering the routine use of L-carnitine supplementation in light of recent evidence questioning its efficacy and potential harms represents another important development in optimizing pharmacological management[28].

As investigational therapies progress through clinical development and potentially achieve regulatory approval, the management of MCADD will likely evolve from a dietary-dependent approach to a multimodal pharmacological strategy combining established dietary principles with novel anaplerotic compounds, genetic therapies, and condition-specific pharmaceutical interventions. This evolution holds substantial promise for improving outcomes in this rare but serious inherited metabolic disorder and demonstrates the value of sustained research efforts and clinical investigation in rare disease pharmacotherapy.
