# Drug therapies for Marfan syndrome (MONDO:0007947): comprehensive research report (focus 2023–2024)

## 1) Key concepts, definitions, and current mechanistic understanding

### Disease definition and therapeutic goal
Marfan syndrome (MFS) is a heritable connective tissue disorder (classically due to pathogenic variants in **FBN1**) where morbidity and mortality are largely driven by **progressive thoracic aortic root dilation leading to dissection/rupture**. Accordingly, pharmacotherapy focuses on **reducing hemodynamic stress** on the aorta and **modulating maladaptive signaling pathways** implicated in aortic wall degeneration.

### Pathobiology relevant to drug therapy
A widely used mechanistic framework links FBN1 defects to dysregulated **TGF-β signaling** and interaction with the **renin–angiotensin system (RAS)**. A 2024 mechanistic study in a Marfan mouse model summarizes that **AT1R (angiotensin II type 1 receptor) signaling**—via G-protein and β-arrestin pathways and inflammatory crosstalk (AT1R–CCR2/MCP-1 axis)—is a druggable contributor to aneurysm progression, and highlights the key translational observation that ARBs prevent aneurysm formation in Marfan mice but in humans generally “only slow down aortic dilation.” (callejon2024investigationofstrategies pages 1-2)

## 2) Approved drug therapies (regulatory status) vs standard-of-care in practice

### Are any drugs approved *specifically for Marfan syndrome*?
No Marfan-syndrome–specific drug approvals were identified in the retrieved regulatory/guideline evidence. Contemporary guidance and trials support the use of several **approved cardiovascular drugs** (approved for hypertension/heart disease indications) **off-label** to slow aortic growth in Marfan syndrome.

### Guideline-supported standard-of-care pharmacotherapy
The **2022 ACC/AHA Aortic Disease Guideline** emphasizes strict blood-pressure control in thoracic aortic aneurysm populations (including Marfan syndrome) with a general goal of **≤130/80 mm Hg** and, if tolerated, **SBP <120 mm Hg**. It describes beta-blockers as supported by prospective Marfan data and characterizes **beta-blockers as reasonable** and **ARBs as a reasonable adjunct to beta-blockers** for thoracic aortic aneurysm/Marfan medical management. (isselbacher20222022accahaguideline pages 46-48)

#### A) Beta-blockers (BBs) – common first-line/anchor therapy (off-label for MFS)
**Relationship type:** Standard-of-care in practice; guideline-supported; typically off-label for the MFS indication.

**Mechanism of action (therapeutic rationale):** Reduce heart rate and myocardial contractility → lower aortic wall stress and dP/dt.

**Evidence:** The 2022 ACC/AHA guideline cites randomized and cohort evidence that beta-blockers slow aortic root growth (e.g., one pediatric cohort estimate of 0.16 mm/y reduction in multivariable analysis). (isselbacher20222022accahaguideline pages 46-48)

**Examples used in practice:** atenolol, propranolol, metoprolol; pediatric use is specifically noted in a 2024 hereditary thoracic aortic disease review. (spaziani2024hereditarythoracicaortic pages 9-10)

#### B) Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) – widely used alternative/adjunct (off-label for MFS)
**Relationship type:** Standard-of-care adjunct or alternative; guideline-supported; off-label for the MFS indication.

**Mechanism of action:** AT1R blockade lowers blood pressure and may attenuate AT1R-driven signaling linked to TGF-β dysregulation and vascular inflammation. (callejon2024investigationofstrategies pages 1-2)

**Highest-quality evidence (key quantitative outcomes):**

*Lancet 2022 individual-patient-data meta-analysis (ARBs and β-blockers in MFS):*
- Across ARB vs control trials, ARB allocation “approximately halved the annual rate of change in the aortic root Z score” (0.07 [SE 0.02] vs 0.13 [SE 0.02]); absolute difference **−0.07 Z-score units/year** (95% CI −0.12 to −0.01; **p=0.012**). (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 1-2)
- In ARB vs β-blocker trials, annual Z-score change was similar (**−0.08 vs −0.11**); difference 0.03 (95% CI −0.05 to 0.10; **p=0.48**). (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 5-6)
- The authors note the ARB effect “seems to be in addition to any effects of β blockers,” supporting an additive (combination) rationale. (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 7-8)

*AIMS trial (Lancet 2019, irbesartan vs placebo):*
- Aortic root diameter progression favored irbesartan: difference in means **−0.21 mm/year** (95% CI −0.40 to −0.02; **p=0.027**). (mullen2019irbesartaninmarfan pages 5-6)
- Annual aortic root Z-score change: **0.05/year vs 0.15/year**; difference **−0.10/year** (95% CI −0.19 to −0.01; **p=0.035**). (mullen2019irbesartaninmarfan pages 5-6)
- BP reductions at 1 year were sustained (SBP −6.3 mm Hg; DBP −3.6 mm Hg). (mullen2019irbesartaninmarfan pages 5-6)

*Pediatric real-world evidence (2023 retrospective cohort, genetic aortopathies predominantly FBN1):*
- Both ARBs and beta-blockers improved aortic root growth, but ARBs showed a more pronounced effect and better tolerance (discontinuation **3% ARB vs 50% BB**). (olfe2023prophylacticeffectof pages 1-2)

#### C) ACE inhibitors (ACEi) – limited evidence base in MFS; sometimes considered
**Relationship type:** Off-label/repurposing candidate; less supported than BB/ARB.

**Mechanism of action:** Reduce angiotensin II production and afterload; may improve arterial stiffness.

**Evidence:** A Phase 4 crossover study compared **perindopril** with atenolol and verapamil, focusing on central pressure and arterial stiffness measures (not hard outcomes). (NCT01295047 chunk 1)

#### D) Direct renin inhibitor (DRI) – investigational as add-on to BB
**Relationship type:** Investigational/repurposing.

**Mechanism:** Upstream RAS blockade.

**Evidence:** A Phase 3 randomized trial studied **aliskiren** (on background atenolol) with MRI-assessed aortic distensibility as primary endpoint. (NCT01715207 chunk 1)

## 3) Investigational and pipeline drugs (clinical trials; NCT numbers)

The current clinical trial landscape remains dominated by **repurposed cardiovascular drugs** (BBs and RAS blockade) and mechanistically motivated matrix/inflammation pathway candidates.

### Key interventional trials (ClinicalTrials.gov)
- **Atenolol vs Losartan** (large pediatric/young adult RCT): **NCT00429364** (Phase 3; completed; 608 enrolled). (Listed in registry search context; trial text not re-extracted here.)
- **Losartan vs Atenolol**: **NCT01145612** (Phase 3; status unknown/last known active-not-recruiting; 140 enrolled). (NCT01145612 chunk 1)
- **Losartan vs Atenolol** (adult vascular stiffness endpoints): **NCT00593710** (Phase 2; completed; 17 enrolled; primary endpoints: pulse wave velocity/distensibility/stiffness/compliance; 12 months). (NCT00593710 chunk 1)
- **Aliskiren add-on** (all patients on atenolol): **NCT01715207** (Phase 3; completed; 32 randomized; primary endpoint: change in central aortic distensibility by cardiac MRI at 24 weeks). (NCT01715207 chunk 1)
- **Oxford Marfan Trial (irbesartan, doxycycline, combination vs placebo)**: **NCT01949233** (Phase 2 pilot; randomized double-blind placebo-controlled; estimated 56 participants; primary endpoint: change in ascending aortic distensibility by CMR at 6 months). (NCT01949233 chunk 1)
- **Comparison of medical therapies (atenolol vs perindopril vs verapamil)**: **NCT01295047** (Phase 4; randomized double-blind crossover; 18 enrolled; primary endpoint: central arterial pressure by applanation tonometry). (NCT01295047 chunk 1)
- **TGF-β ancillary biomarker study**: **NCT01361087** (Phase 3; withdrawn; planned to correlate circulating TGF-β with atenolol vs losartan arms in the main NIH trial). (NCT01361087 chunk 1)

### Translational pipeline directions (2023–2024)
A 2024 Marfan mouse study tested deeper AT1R-pathway modulation beyond classical ARBs (β-arrestin targeting with TRV027, barbadin, CCR2 blocker DMX-200). Only **high-dose losartan** significantly slowed aneurysm progression (1.73 ± 0.12 vs 1.96 ± 0.08 mm; p=0.0033), while the pathway-modulating combinations did not show benefit. This supports a hypothesis that **near-complete AT1R blockade (dose-dependent)** may be necessary in that model. (callejon2024investigationofstrategies pages 1-2)

## 4) Drug repurposing candidates (evidence strength)

### A) Doxycycline (MMP inhibition; ECM stabilization)
**Relationship type:** Investigational repurposing.

**Rationale:** MMP-2/MMP-9 inhibition is proposed to preserve elastic fiber integrity and reduce matrix degradation; a 2024 review notes MMP inhibition (e.g., doxycycline) reduced aortic root growth and delayed rupture in Marfan mouse models. (spaziani2024hereditarythoracicaortic pages 9-10)

**Clinical evidence:** A placebo-controlled pilot trial testing doxycycline alone and in combination with irbesartan is registered (NCT01949233). (NCT01949233 chunk 1)

### B) Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) – *not a repurposing candidate; a caution signal*
Although sometimes used for blood-pressure control, multiple sources flag a cautionary signal in connective tissue disorders. A contemporary Marfan/aortic-dilation review noted “negative impact of fluoroquinolones and calcium channel blockers in patients with connective tissue disorders” and called for clinical caution. (kocyigit2021medicaltherapiesfor pages 7-8)

## 5) Contraindications / drugs to avoid or use with caution

### A) Fluoroquinolone antibiotics (avoid when possible in aortopathy/high-risk connective tissue disease)
**Relationship type:** Safety contraindication/caution in high-risk aortopathy populations.

**Authoritative safety signal and rationale:**
- Reviews cite FDA safety communications warning of an “increased risk of ruptures or tears in the aorta blood vessel with fluoroquinolone antibiotics in certain patients.” (kocyigit2021medicaltherapiesfor pages 9-9)
- Mechanistic rationale summarized in a 2024 umbrella review includes extracellular matrix dysregulation through altered MMP/TIMP balance, increased MMP activity, collagen degradation, and decreased collagen synthesis → aortic wall fragility. (macido2024anumbrellaliterature pages 3-6)

**Quantitative risk estimates (recent syntheses):**
- 2024 umbrella review summarized multiple observational meta-analyses reporting approximately **two-fold** increased risk of aortic aneurysm/dissection with fluoroquinolone exposure (examples pooled estimates: RR ~2.14; adjusted OR ~2.10; OR ~2.26). (macido2024anumbrellaliterature pages 10-14)
- It also cites individual study estimates such as a nested case-control RR 2.43 (95% CI 1.83–3.22) and cohort HRs (e.g., HR 1.20 in a US cohort; and a Swedish cohort estimate consistent with increased risk within 8 weeks). (macido2024anumbrellaliterature pages 3-6)

**Marfan-specific mechanistic plausibility:** The umbrella review reports Marfan mouse model data consistent with increased dissection/rupture and matrix injury signals under ciprofloxacin exposure (e.g., higher dissection and rupture rates). (macido2024anumbrellaliterature pages 6-10)

### B) Calcium channel blockers (use with caution in syndromic/heritable thoracic aortopathy)
A 2024 hereditary thoracic aortic disease review states that “calcium channel antagonists should be used with caution when treating patients with syndromic inherited thoracic aortopathy or congenital heart disease,” reflecting limited/uncertain benefit and potential harm signals. (spaziani2024hereditarythoracicaortic pages 9-10)

## 6) Adverse events of special relevance (drugs that may cause/worsen aortopathy)

### A) Fluoroquinolones: mechanistic injury programs in vascular cells
A 2024 experimental study reports that ciprofloxacin can promote angiotensin-II–induced vascular smooth muscle cell senescence through AMPK/ROS and mitochondrial dysfunction pathways and concludes that ciprofloxacin “might elevate the risk of AAD.” (zeng2024ciprofloxacinacceleratesangiotensiniiinduced pages 1-2)

### B) VEGF pathway inhibitors (TKIs) and aortic aneurysm/dissection risk
**Relationship type:** Adverse drug reaction signal relevant to aneurysm/dissection risk (important in patients with known aortopathy or genetic predisposition).

A March 4, 2024 **JAMA Network Open** nested case-control study in Taiwan examined oral VEGF pathway inhibitors (sorafenib, sunitinib, pazopanib) and found:
- Composite AA/AD: adjusted OR **2.00** (95% CI 1.41–2.84). (wu2024vascularendothelialgrowth pages 4-5)
- Risk was largely driven by aortic dissection: adjusted OR **3.09** (95% CI 1.77–5.39). (wu2024vascularendothelialgrowth pages 4-5)
- Dose/duration dependence: cumulative duration ≥68 days had adjusted OR **2.64** (95% CI 1.66–4.19) and cumulative dose ≥61 defined daily doses had adjusted OR **2.65** (95% CI 1.66–4.23). (wu2024vascularendothelialgrowth pages 7-8)
The authors note important limitations for heritable aortopathy inference, including that **no patients with Marfan syndrome were identified** in the dataset. (wu2024vascularendothelialgrowth pages 7-8)

## 7) Combination therapies and real-world implementation

### Beta-blocker + ARB combination therapy
**Rationale:** additive reduction in hemodynamic stress plus RAS/TGF-β–linked signaling modulation.

**Evidence and expert interpretation:**
- The 2022 Lancet IPD meta-analysis explicitly notes that the ARB effect “seems to be in addition to any effects of β blockers,” and discusses that combination therapy could yield additive reductions in aortic growth if effects are independent/additive. (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 7-8)
- The 2022 ACC/AHA guideline characterizes ARBs as a “reasonable adjunct” to beta-blocker therapy in thoracic aortic aneurysm/Marfan management and cites pooled trial evidence that combination therapy slows aortic growth more than beta-blocker alone. (isselbacher20222022accahaguideline pages 46-48)

### Pediatric implementation (tolerability as a driver)
A 2023 pediatric cohort study (predominantly FBN1) reported much lower discontinuation with ARBs (3%) than beta-blockers (50%), supporting a real-world rationale for ARB-first or ARB-early strategies in children when tolerated. (olfe2023prophylacticeffectof pages 1-2)

## 9) Practical interpretation (expert synthesis based on retrieved evidence)

1. **Core disease-modifying goal:** The most evidence-supported pharmacologic objective in Marfan syndrome is **slowing aortic root growth**, rather than proven reductions in mortality/dissection in short-to-medium follow-up RCTs; clinical event rates are low in trials, so Z-score/diameter slopes remain the dominant endpoints. (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 1-2, mullen2019irbesartaninmarfan pages 5-6)
2. **Best-supported agents:** **ARBs and beta-blockers** have the strongest evidence base and appear to have **similar magnitude effects** when compared head-to-head, with ARBs showing clear benefit vs control in pooled IPD analyses and irbesartan showing benefit vs placebo in AIMS. (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 5-6, pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 1-2, mullen2019irbesartaninmarfan pages 5-6)
3. **Combination therapy is a rational default for many patients:** Guidelines and meta-analytic interpretation support ARB + beta-blocker as a plausible additive regimen to further reduce aortic growth, with practical choice driven by tolerance, blood pressure/heart-rate targets, and patient-specific contraindications. (pitcher2022angiotensinreceptorblockers pages 7-8, isselbacher20222022accahaguideline pages 46-48)
4. **Safety vigilance:** Avoid or minimize exposure to medications with credible aortopathy signals—especially **fluoroquinolones**—and use **calcium channel blockers** cautiously in syndromic heritable thoracic aortopathy unless clearly indicated for other reasons. (macido2024anumbrellaliterature pages 10-14, macido2024anumbrellaliterature pages 3-6, spaziani2024hereditarythoracicaortic pages 9-10)

## Limitations of this report (evidence availability)
- This tool-based evidence set supports robust, high-quality statements for BB/ARB therapy and several safety signals (fluoroquinolones; VEGF inhibitors), but it does **not** include full regulatory label text (FDA/EMA SmPC/USPI) for each drug or a complete, up-to-date listing of all 2023–2024 interventional drug trials beyond those retrieved in the current ClinicalTrials.gov subset. The report therefore distinguishes **guideline-supported standard-of-care/off-label use** from true **disease-specific regulatory approvals**.
