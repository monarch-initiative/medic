# Pharmacological Management of Achalasia: A Comprehensive Review of Drug Therapies, Clinical Applications, and Investigational Approaches

Achalasia represents a primary motility disorder of the esophagus characterized by the failure of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) to relax appropriately during swallowing, accompanied by aperistalsis of the esophageal body[7]. This rare condition profoundly impacts quality of life, resulting in progressive dysphagia, regurgitation, chest pain, and potential serious complications including malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia[4]. While no curative pharmacological therapy currently exists for achalasia, a diverse array of pharmaceutical interventions has been developed and refined over recent decades to provide symptomatic relief and improve esophageal function. This comprehensive report reviews approved drug therapies, investigational agents in clinical trials, drug repurposing opportunities, contraindicated medications, and adverse drug-disease relationships pertinent to achalasia management. The evidence presented draws from regulatory approvals, clinical trial data, and contemporary research examining both traditional and emerging therapeutic approaches to this debilitating condition.

## Approved Pharmaceutical Therapies for Achalasia

### Botulinum Toxin Injection as the Primary Pharmacological Treatment

Botulinum toxin (BT) injection into the lower esophageal sphincter has emerged as the most widely used and most effective pharmacological therapy currently available for achalasia management[1][1]. The FDA has approved two serotypes of botulinum toxin—type A and type B—for various clinical indications, though only type A formulations have gained widespread clinical acceptance for achalasia treatment[1][1]. Specifically, all three formulations of botulinum toxin type A available for clinical use in patients with achalasia are of the A1 subtype, as seven subtypes (A1-A7) have been described, but only the A1 formulation has been developed commercially for this indication[1][1]. The mechanism of action underlying BT's therapeutic efficacy involves a sophisticated process of neuronal interaction and acetylcholine neurotransmission disruption. The BT precursor is synthesized as a 150 kilodalton neurotoxin polypeptide chain with low intrinsic activity along with neuro-toxin associated proteins (NAP), which are believed to protect the neurotoxin from proteases in the gastrointestinal tract[1][1]. Within the injected tissue, the BT precursor undergoes cleavage in vivo into a 100 kilodalton heavy chain and a 50 kilodalton light chain linked by a disulfide bridge, as well as a poorly structured protein segment called the belt[1][1].

The heavy chain comprises two functional domains: the heavy chain carboxy terminus (H-C) and the heavy chain amino terminus (H-N). The H-C domain, which can be further subdivided into two subdomains, serves as the neuronal receptor recognition and binding component, while the H-N facilitates translocation of the light chain into the cytosol[1][1]. Once inside the neuronal cell, the light chain proceeds to cleave one or more of the soluble NSF-attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex proteins, which are essential molecular machinery required for synaptic vesicle fusion with the active zone of the neuronal synapse[1][1]. This cleavage impairs vesicle fusion and inhibits synaptic activity, thereby reducing acetylcholine release at the lower esophageal sphincter and allowing the sphincter muscles to relax[3][4].

The clinical efficacy of botulinum toxin injection in achalasia has been well-established through numerous randomized controlled trials and observational studies. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 uncontrolled studies that reported clinical outcomes in 730 achalasia patients treated with botulinum toxin injection revealed that clinical success, defined by an Eckardt score of 3 or less, was achieved in 77 percent (95 percent confidence interval, 72-81 percent) over the follow-up period[3]. Importantly, when multiple injections are taken into account, BT injection led to a good clinical short-term response (at 4 weeks, with a global symptom score less than 3) in 89.7 percent of patients, demonstrating rapid symptomatic improvement in the majority of recipients[5]. However, the main shortcoming of botulinum toxin therapy is its durability, which is limited to approximately 6 to 12 months, with the effect gradually waning over this timeframe[4][5][7].

Botulinum toxin injection is considered generally very safe, with serious adverse events such as mediastinitis or allergic reactions being exceedingly rare[3]. The most common side effect associated with BT(A) injection into the LES is usually transient chest pain, which is generally mild and self-limited[1][1]. In contrast, botulinum toxin type B (BT-B), by virtue of its stronger anticholinergic effects, has been noted to cause autonomic side effects such as dry mouth and jitteriness even in small doses, and patients who received BT(B) reported higher incidences of constipation and lower saliva production compared to BT(A)[1][1]. These differences have led to the predominant clinical use of type A formulations in achalasia management.

The treatment response to botulinum toxin injection demonstrates important patient-dependent predictors of long-term success. In a prospective follow-up study of 31 patients with achalasia treated with botulinum toxin, the response rate was significantly greater in patients older than 50 years of age (82 percent versus 43 percent in younger patients, P = 0.03) and in patients with vigorous achalasia (100 percent versus 52 percent with classic achalasia, P = 0.03)[32]. The median duration of response averaged 1.3 years, with 19 of the responders eventually experiencing relapse after a median duration of 468 days (range, 153-840 days)[32]. Importantly, 15 of these patients who had relapsed received a second injection with satisfactory results obtained in the majority of patients, suggesting that repeated injections can be effective, though the efficacy of BT with repeat injections decreases and is thought to be secondary to antibody formation[1][32].

The efficacy of botulinum toxin with repeat injections decreases over time, with there being almost universal symptom relapse by two years, although some studies have shown continued efficacy in up to 34 percent of patients at two years[1]. Furthermore, at least a second treatment was required in 46.6 percent of patients[1]. This diminishing returns pattern highlights both the therapeutic value and inherent limitations of BT as a long-term management strategy. Clinical success in achalasia management is often assessed using the Eckardt score, a composite measure that evaluates dysphagia, regurgitation, chest pain, and weight loss, with scores of 3 or less generally considered indicative of therapeutic success[46].

### Nitrate Compounds for Short-Term Symptomatic Relief

Nitrate therapy represents one of the earliest pharmacological approaches investigated for achalasia treatment, with studies examining the effects of short-acting and long-acting nitrates on the lower esophageal sphincter commencing in the 1940s and 1950s[1][1]. Early investigations employed short-acting nitrates such as amyl or acetyl nitrate and sublingual nitroglycerin, but their use was largely abandoned due to the short duration of action (less than 30 minutes) and significant side effects including hypotension and headaches[1]. With the subsequent introduction and widespread use of isosorbide dinitrate, investigators demonstrated significant clinical improvement in dysphagia and decreases in LES pressures on manometry[1]. In a landmark study examining long-acting nitrate effects, a single 5 milligram sublingual dose of isosorbide dinitrate produced a reduction in LES pressure in all 24 patients studied, with the mean LES pressure falling from 46.3 millimeters of mercury to 15.3 millimeters of mercury (P less than 0.01)[13]. The pressure began to drop after several minutes, reaching its lowest levels after 15 minutes, with this measured manometric effect lasting 60 minutes or longer in 10 patients studied[13].

The mechanism of action underlying nitrate efficacy in achalasia involves the release of nitrous oxide, which results in relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter[6]. Nitrates increase nitric oxide concentrations in smooth muscles, causing an increase in cyclic adenosine monophosphate levels, which leads to smooth muscle relaxation[7]. Despite these theoretical advantages, the clinical utility of nitrates in achalasia has been severely limited by multiple factors. The use of nitrates in achalasia has been constrained by its transient or poor efficacy in a significant subset of patients, a high rate of tachyphylaxis leading to tolerance development, and a high incidence of side effects such as headache and hypotension[1][1]. In clinical practice, when 23 patients were followed clinically for 2 to 19 months while receiving isosorbide dinitrate three times daily before meals, 19 reported marked to complete relief of dysphagia[13]. However, side effects, mainly headache, were reported in 8 patients, with 6 of these experiencing alleviation by substituting oral isosorbide dinitrate at 10 milligrams[13]. Notably, 2 patients became refractory to treatment after 2 to 6 months, demonstrating the tachyphylaxis problem that limits long-term utility[13].

Current treatment guidelines recommend nitrates only for patients in early stages of disease, as a temporary regimen immediately prior to another definitive treatment, or in patients who are unable or unwilling to undergo other therapeutic alternatives[23]. They are no longer recommended as standard therapy but rather serve as bridge medications for carefully selected patients awaiting more definitive interventions. The side effect profile remains a significant limitation, with patients frequently experiencing headaches, dizziness, flushing, drowsiness, nausea and vomiting, and hypotension[6].

### Calcium Channel Blockers as Alternative Pharmaceutical Agents

Calcium channel blockers represent another class of pharmaceutical agents investigated for achalasia treatment, with their efficacy first examined in the late 1970s and 1980s[1]. These agents work by inhibiting cellular uptake of calcium, thereby impeding contraction and promoting relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter[1][4]. Nifedipine, the most extensively studied calcium channel blocker in achalasia, has been shown to decrease LES pressures and provide symptomatic relief, although with variable efficacy demonstrating benefit in between 50 and 90 percent of cases across different studies[1][4].

In a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial, nifedipine was titrated to a dose of 10-30 milligrams administered sublingually before meals in ten patients with symptomatic achalasia[19]. Compared with placebo, nifedipine significantly reduced the frequency of dysphagia, though some symptoms of dysphagia, regurgitation, or nocturnal cough were still present most days[19]. Nifedipine significantly reduced lower esophageal sphincter pressure by 28 percent, a value approximately one-half that achieved by successful pneumatic dilation or myotomy[19]. However, esophageal emptying rates, as determined by radionuclide methods, were unchanged by nifedipine, suggesting limitations in its mechanism of action[19].

Additional research examined multiple calcium channel blockers including verapamil. When nifedipine was prospectively compared with verapamil, while verapamil did decrease mean LES pressures, it did not provide effective symptomatic relief compared with nifedipine[1]. A table from recent clinical literature comprehensively summarizes the mechanism of action and common adverse effects of calcium channel blockers used in achalasia management, noting that these agents reduce intracellular calcium uptake, thereby decreasing muscle contractility and relaxing the LES, while commonly producing headache, dizziness, flushing, peripheral edema, nausea and vomiting, and abdominal pain[6].

However, the clinical utility of calcium channel blockers remains significantly constrained by multiple limitations. Up to 30 percent of patients may experience significant side effects from calcium channel blockers such as peripheral edema and headaches, which limit their clinical utility[1][1]. Additionally, tachyphylaxis is a substantial issue with calcium channel blockers, and the use of these drugs often induces tolerance such that patients develop decreased responsiveness to therapy over time[1][1]. Only a handful of studies have examined calcium channel blockers prospectively, with the studies being limited by short follow-up periods and very small sample sizes[1]. Studies comparing nitrates and calcium channel blockers have suggested that nitrates work faster and may be more effective compared with nifedipine, but nitrates have limited clinical use due to more significant side effects[1]. Given the modest efficacy, frequent adverse effects, and development of tolerance, calcium channel blockers are now reserved for patients unable to undergo more definitive treatments or as temporary bridge therapy.

### Phosphodiesterase Type 5 Inhibitors as Investigational Pharmacological Options

Phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE-5) inhibitors represent an emerging class of pharmacological agents proposed as treatment options for esophageal motility disorders including achalasia[14]. These agents induce smooth muscle relaxation through inhibition of the enzyme phosphodiesterase 5, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of 3′,5′-cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)[14]. The mechanism of action is particularly relevant to achalasia because the pathophysiological basis of the disease involves selective loss of inhibitory ganglions in the myenteric plexus resulting in a decrease in nitric oxide (NO), the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter of the gastrointestinal tract[14]. Direct activation of guanylyl cyclase by nitric oxide results in an increase in intracellular cGMP, leading to initiation of a signaling cascade that relaxes smooth muscles or reduces contractions[14]. By inhibiting the breakdown of cGMP, PDE-5 inhibitors theoretically enhance the effects of residual nitric oxide activity in the esophageal sphincter.

Sildenafil, the prototypical PDE-5 inhibitor, was investigated in 14 patients with achalasia in a randomized, double-blind crossover study[12]. After a basal period of 30 minutes, a 50-milligram tablet of sildenafil dissolved in water was infused into the stomach of 7 patients, while the remaining 7 patients received placebo infusion, with recordings continuing for 60 minutes[12]. Lower esophageal sphincter tone, residual pressure, and wave amplitude after sildenafil showed a significant decrease compared with both the basal period and the placebo group, with a marked interpatient variability[12]. The inhibitory effect reached its maximum at approximately 50 percent reduction 15-20 minutes after infusion and lasted less than 1 hour[12]. Importantly, the propagation of pressure waves was not modified by sildenafil, suggesting that the drug does not affect esophageal peristalsis[12]. Another investigation examined sildenafil effects in healthy subjects and patients with various esophageal disorders[14]. Sildenafil lowered LES pressure and propulsive forces in the esophageal body of healthy subjects as well as in patients with nutcracker esophagus, hypertensive LES, and achalasia[14]. However, two patients experienced side effects including headaches, dizziness, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and feeling of tightness in the chest at night, and did not want to continue treatment[14].

Tadalafil, a long-acting PDE-5 inhibitor, was investigated in 12 achalasia patients using a 20-milligram dose administered every two weeks for two months[14]. The study included 6 patients with type 2 achalasia, 3 patients with type 1 achalasia, and 3 patients with type 3 achalasia[14]. Tadalafil administration was found to significantly reduce LES pressure and integrated relaxation pressure in achalasia patients, with patients with type 2 achalasia demonstrating a better response to the drug than other types[14]. Despite these promising mechanistic findings and short-term benefits, PDE-5 inhibitors have not been approved by regulatory agencies specifically for achalasia treatment, and their use remains investigational. Most of the available evidence has been limited to sildenafil, with only 4 studies having evaluated other PDE-5 inhibitors[14]. The transient nature of the drug effect, requiring repeated dosing before meals similar to short-acting nitrates, and questions about long-term efficacy and tolerability have limited advancement of this drug class toward formal approval.

## Investigational and Pipeline Pharmaceuticals in Clinical Development

### Immunomodulatory Therapies Based on Autoimmune Pathophysiology

Recent advances in understanding achalasia pathophysiology have revealed strong evidence for an autoimmune basis of the disease, leading to investigation of immunomodulatory agents as potential disease-modifying treatments[21][23]. The myenteric plexus dysfunction characteristic of achalasia involves the loss of myenteric plexus neurons associated with inflammatory infiltrates and circulating autoantibodies against myenteric nerves[21]. A study examining serum anti-enteric neuronal antibodies (AENA) in patients with achalasia was initiated in February 2019 and completed in November 2025[8]. This prospective observational registry study enrolled 195 patients with achalasia cardia and age-matched healthy subjects without gastrointestinal symptoms to evaluate the association between AENA levels and disease severity[8]. The study specifically aimed to determine whether the intensity of AENA positivity is associated with endoscopic severity (measured by the CARS score), esophageal dilation, and integrated relaxation pressure, and whether AENA status is associated with symptomatic outcomes following peroral endoscopic myotomy[8].

The presence of circulating anti-myenteric antibodies was found in 100 percent of achalasia patients versus 0 percent in controls in one comprehensive immunological study[21]. Circulating helper T cell (Th22/Th17/Th2/Th1) percentages showed a significant increase in achalasia patients versus healthy donors (P less than 0.01), with type III achalasia patients exhibiting the highest inflammatory response compared to types I and II[21]. The interleukin-17 (IL-17A) pathway appears particularly important in achalasia pathogenesis, as IL-17 is defined as a key mediator of inflammatory autoimmune diseases, promoting recruitment of neutrophils, activation of innate immune cells, enhancement of B-cell functions, and induction of proinflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β)[21]. IL-17A positive cell frequency was significantly higher in the myenteric plexus of esophageal achalasia tissue samples compared with control tissue, with higher immunoreactive cell numbers found in types II and III achalasia when compared with type I achalasia[21].

A pilot study investigating corticosteroid treatment for achalasia-variant esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction (EGJOO) recruited adult patients with dysphagia and/or noncardiac chest pain with conclusive esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction defined by high-resolution manometry study (Chicago Classification version 4.0 criteria)[22]. Eligible patients were prescribed prednisone 20 milligrams daily for 14 days[22]. Although integrated relaxation pressure (IRP) decreased significantly with prednisone treatment, it usually did not normalize, and it remains unclear whether the dose or duration of prednisone employed represented optimal therapeutic parameters[22]. Notably, in this pilot study of 10 patients with clinically relevant, conclusive EGJOO, prednisone treatment for only 2 weeks was associated with significant reductions in IRP, and most patients exhibited improvement in symptom scores[22]. While not definitive, these findings strongly suggest that a subset of EGJOO has a component that is both responsive to anti-inflammatory therapy and potentially reversible[22]. The hypothesis proposed that a subset of achalasia can be caused by type 2 inflammation in esophageal muscle (a muscle-predominant form of eosinophilic esophagitis), supporting investigation of corticosteroids and potentially other immunosuppressive agents.

The evidence base for autoimmune mechanisms in achalasia has garnered substantial support from multiple lines of investigation. A genetic association study of achalasia demonstrated that an eight-residue insertion at position 227-234 in the cytoplasmic tail of HLA-DQβ1 confers the most potent known risk factor for achalasia, while two amino acid substitutions in the extracellular region of HLA-DQα1 at position 41 and HLA-DQβ1 at position 45 are independent risk factors for achalasia[7]. These HLA associations strongly support immune-mediated pathophysiology. Furthermore, response of achalasia to immunosuppressive therapy also suggests that achalasia is an autoimmune disorder. To the authors' knowledge, there are only three case reports of corticosteroid use (specifically of prednisolone, methylprednisolone, or beclomethasone) alone or combined with other immunosuppressive therapy (specifically of methotrexate, azathioprine, or cyclophosphamide) for achalasia, and the results show dramatic improvement of the clinical picture, with complete recovery of peristalsis corroborated by high-resolution manometry[23]. These observations suggest that immunomodulatory therapy may represent a future direction for disease modification rather than mere symptomatic management.

### Sclerosing Agents as Alternative Injection Therapies

Alternative agents for the treatment of achalasia have been investigated in clinical research, with particular attention to sclerosing compounds. Ethanolamine oleate (EO) is a sclerosant that is FDA-approved for the treatment of bleeding esophageal varices and is also used for lower extremity varicosities and vascular lesions[1]. Ethanolamine oleate is a synthetic salt comprising ethanolamine and oleic acid and has been investigated as an alternative to botulinum toxin for intrasphincteric injection[1].

In a prospective interventional study, diluted ethanolamine oleate was injected in divided doses into each of four quadrants of the lower esophageal sphincter using a standard sclerotherapy needle in 13 patients with idiopathic achalasia resistant to or considered poor candidates for pneumatic balloon dilation and/or cardiomyotomy[20]. Injection was repeated at 2 and 4 weeks after the first injection[20]. The patients were evaluated with achalasia symptom score (ASS) and timed barium esophagogram (TBE) before and after injections, with good response defined as a decrease in ASS of at least 50 percent of baseline and decrease in height and volume of barium of at least 50 percent of baseline in timed barium esophagogram at 1.5 months after the last injection[20]. All 13 patients (100 percent) had good ASS response (decreased ≥50 percent) and good TBE response (decreased in height and volume of barium ≥50 percent) response rate[20]. The mean ASS decreased from 11.38 (±1.5) to 3.23 (±1.96) at 1.5 months after the last injection (P=0.001)[20]. The mean volume of barium in timed barium esophagogram decreased from 81.38 milliliters (±51.11) to 40.69 milliliters (±61.22) at 1.5 months after the last injection (P=0.016)[20]. The mean duration of follow-up was 17.83 (±1.12) months, with symptoms of 6 patients relapsing, all of whom were treated effectively with reinjection[20]. The investigators concluded that ethanolamine oleate is well tolerated and potentially effective in patients with idiopathic achalasia that might be explained by the local inflammatory properties of ethanolamine oleate[20].

## Drug Repurposing Candidates and Off-Label Uses

### Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide and Related Neuropeptides

Investigation of the underlying pathophysiology of achalasia has identified loss of vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) as a central pathological feature. Parasympathetic excitatory and inhibitory pathways innervate the smooth muscles of the lower esophageal sphincter, with excitatory neurotransmitters such as substance P and acetylcholine, and inhibitory neurotransmitters such as VIP and nitric oxide (the most important inhibitory neurotransmitter of the myenteric plexus) modulating lower esophageal sphincter pressure and relaxation[7]. Individuals with achalasia specifically lack noncholinergic, nonadrenergic inhibitory ganglion cells, but the excitatory neurons remain unaffected, resulting in an imbalance of inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmission[7]. The lack of inhibitory ganglion cells results in a non-relaxed hypertensive esophageal sphincter.

Investigators have examined whether replacement of vasoactive intestinal peptide could restore function in achalasia patients. When intravenous doses of exogenous VIP were administered to patients with achalasia and healthy controls, and lower esophageal sphincter pressure was compared by esophageal manometry, exogenous VIP improved LES relaxation and decreased LES pressure in achalasia patients without affecting LES pressure in healthy volunteers, suggesting the specific relaxation effect of VIP on the lower esophageal sphincter in achalasia[29]. This finding supports the potential for VIP replacement or VIP receptor agonists as disease-modifying therapies, though such agents have not yet advanced into clinical trials specifically for achalasia.

### Antimicrobial Agents and Herpes Simplex Virus Considerations

Evidence suggesting infectious triggers in achalasia pathogenesis has led to investigation of antimicrobial agents. A comprehensive immunological study found that the prevalence of both anti-myenteric antibodies and herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection in achalasia patients was 100 percent versus 0 percent in controls[21]. This striking difference suggests that HSV-1 infection may play a pathogenic role in achalasia development, potentially through molecular mimicry or direct viral-induced neuronal damage.

When strips of esophageal muscle were obtained from patients with achalasia and control subjects (multiorgan transplant donors), and mononuclear cells were purified and cultured with or without ultraviolet-inactivated HSV-1, the proliferative index in mononuclear cells from achalasia patients stimulated with HSV-1 showed a 3.4-fold increase in comparison with control subjects (P less than 0.01)[47]. Additionally, a 1.4-fold increase in interferon gamma release after incubation with HSV-1 was observed in cells from achalasia patients but not control subjects[47]. These findings indicate that HSV-1-reactive immune cells are present in lower esophageal sphincter muscles of patients with achalasia, and the researchers hypothesize that HSV-1-reactive lymphocytes in the lower esophageal sphincter muscles of achalasia patients may contribute to damage of the neurons in the myenteric plexus and lead to the motor dysfunction[47]. However, no specific antiviral agents have been tested therapeutically in achalasia patients based on this putative mechanism.

## Contraindicated Medications and Drug-Disease Associations

### Opioid-Associated Esophageal Dysfunction

Recent clinical evidence has identified chronic opioid therapy as a significant risk factor for esophageal motility disorders including achalasia. In a retrospective study of 395 veteran patients undergoing esophageal manometry, 29 percent had a history of chronic opioid therapy[26]. Notably, patients who were diagnosed with achalasia had a greater proportion of chronic opioid use compared to those who were not diagnosed with achalasia[26]. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant lower average upper esophageal sphincter (UES) resting pressure in opioid patients compared to non-opioid patients[26]. These findings suggest a mechanistic link between opioid exposure and development of esophageal dysmotility, potentially through direct effects on smooth muscle or neural function.

Recent studies have reported an increased frequency of esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction (EGJOO), achalasia, and lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction among opioid users[26]. The mechanisms underlying opioid-induced esophageal dysfunction likely involve direct effects of opioid receptors present on esophageal smooth muscle and myenteric neurons. Opioids are known to impair gastrointestinal motility through multiple mechanisms including reduced acetylcholine release, altered neural signaling, and direct effects on smooth muscle contractility[26]. This association has important clinical implications: patients with achalasia who are on chronic opioid therapy should be counseled regarding this potential worsening of their condition, and conversely, the development of new or worsening dysphagia in patients receiving opioid therapy should prompt consideration of opioid-induced esophageal dysfunction.

### Anticholinergic Medications and Esophageal Motility Impairment

Medications with anticholinergic or antimuscarinic effects are known to cause dysphagia and can worsen esophageal motility disorders[17][27]. A prospective study examined the effects of medications taken within 24 hours of high-resolution esophageal manometry in 502 adult patients with upper gastrointestinal symptoms[27]. Of these patients, 41.2 percent had normal esophageal high-resolution manometry, 41.4 percent had ineffective esophageal motility (IOM), and 7.6 percent had esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction (OGJOO)[27]. Importantly, the odds ratio of being on anticholinergic medications was significantly higher in IOM patients compared to those with normal manometry (odds ratio 3.6, 95 percent confidence interval 1.2-10.8)[27]. For patients with OGJOO, the odds ratios for anticholinergic medications were similarly elevated and greater than 3[27].

Specific anticholinergic medications that cause swallowing problems include benztropine mesylate (given for movement-related effects caused by psychotropic medications), oxybutynin (which improves bladder capacity), propantheline (which inhibits release of stomach acid), and tolterodine (which affects bladder capacity)[17]. Additionally, inhaled beta-agonists, anticonvulsants, selective serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and opioids were all found to have odds ratios greater than 3 for esophageal dysmotility[27]. These medications should be used with caution in patients with pre-existing achalasia and avoided when possible, particularly in patients with symptomatic disease.

### Psychotropic and Neurological Medications

Multiple classes of psychotropic and neurological medications are known to cause dysphagia and impair esophageal function[17]. Antipsychotic and neuroleptic medications including chlorpromazine (Thorazine), olanzapine (Zyprexa), clozapine (Clozaril), quetiapine (Seroquel), fluphenazine (Prolixin), risperidone (Risperdal), haloperidol (Haldol), thioridazine (Mellaril), and lithium can all affect swallowing and esophageal motility[17][27]. The mechanisms involve both anticholinergic effects and direct effects on neural signaling. Benzodiazepines used as anti-anxiety agents, including alprazolam (Xanax), clonazepam (Klonopin), clorazepate (Tranxene), diazepam (Valium), and lorazepam (Ativan), can impair esophageal function[17]. These medications increase the risk of dysphagia and can worsen symptoms in patients with pre-existing achalasia.

Anti-epileptic drugs used for seizure management also carry risk for esophageal dysfunction. Medications including carbamazepine (Tegretol), gabapentin (Neurontin), phenobarbital, phenytoin (Dilantin), and valproic acid (Depakote) have been associated with swallowing difficulties[17]. The mechanisms likely involve effects on neural control of esophageal muscles. High-dose corticosteroids and chemotherapeutic agents may cause muscle wasting or direct damage to the esophagus and can suppress the immune system, making patients susceptible to infection[17].

### Medications That Directly Damage the Esophagus

Certain medications are known to cause direct chemical or mechanical injury to the esophageal mucosa and can secondarily impair motility[17]. Acid-containing products including clindamycin (Cleocin), doxycycline (Vibramycin), erythromycin (Ery-tabs, E-mycin), and tetracycline (Sumycin) can cause esophageal ulceration if not taken with adequate water[17]. Aspirin and other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) including ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) and naproxen (Aleve, Naprosyn) can cause ulceration and bleeding[17]. Bisphosphonates used for osteoporosis treatment such as alendronate (Fosamax) are notorious for causing severe esophageal irritation if not taken with proper precautions[17]. Iron-containing products, potassium chloride supplements, and vitamin C (ascorbic acid) supplements can all cause esophageal injury[17]. These medications should be taken with caution in patients with achalasia, as esophageal stasis may increase the time these caustic agents remain in contact with the esophageal mucosa.

## Adverse Events Associated with Achalasia Treatment and Drug-Disease Complications

### Reflux Disease Following Pharmacological and Procedural Interventions

A critical adverse consequence of achalasia treatment relates to the development of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) following therapeutic interventions. Following peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) procedures, reflux is more likely than with other treatment modalities[16]. Additionally, reflux symptoms are sometimes observed after laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) surgery[16]. The fundoplication component of LHM surgery is specifically designed to reduce reflux complications[16]. By disrupting the barrier function of the lower esophageal sphincter through chemical denervation or surgical division, these treatments necessarily increase the potential for acid reflux. Complications related to balloon dilation include the development of esophageal reflux symptoms in 15-35 percent of patients[9]. However, symptoms of reflux in post-pneumatic dilation patients are usually mild and transient and can be easily controlled by prescribing proton-pump inhibitors[11].

Persistent reflux presents a significant health risk in achalasia patients[16]. Reflux can sometimes reach the lungs (aspiration) which brings a risk of pneumonia[16]. This complication is particularly concerning in elderly patients or those with compromised swallowing and cough reflexes. Adjusting sleeping position to sleep with the head and shoulders raised can help reduce reflux at night[16]. Medication with proton-pump inhibitors can be taken to reduce acid production and reflux symptoms. Recognition of this potential complication is important when counseling patients about treatment options and post-treatment management.

### Esophageal Cancer Risk in Achalasia

An important and concerning adverse consequence of achalasia itself, rather than its treatment, is the substantially elevated risk of esophageal cancer. Patients with achalasia have a 50 times higher risk of presenting with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) compared to the general population, with the disease typically manifesting 20-25 years after achalasia symptom onset[28]. Multiple mechanisms are related to the development of ESCC in achalasia, including bacterial overgrowth, food stasis, genetic alterations, and chronic inflammation[28].

The documented risk of ESCC in achalasia shows considerable variation in the literature, with some authors reporting an increase in esophageal carcinoma up to 50 times higher than in a control population matched by age and sex[28]. A meta-analysis of 40 studies involving 11,978 patients described an incidence of squamous cell carcinoma of 312.4 cases per 100,000 patient-years at risk[28]. The prevalence of ESCC in subjects with esophageal achalasia was 26 cases in 1,000, representing an increase in absolute risk of squamous cell carcinoma of 308.1 cases per 100,000 patients per year[28]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma typically develops 10-15 years after achalasia diagnosis or 20-25 years after the onset of achalasia symptoms, with tumors usually arising in a widely dilated esophagus and, when detected, large and at an advanced stage[28].

Beyond ESCC, achalasia also increases the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma (EA), which typically develops in association with Barrett's esophagus due to uncontrolled chronic acid reflux[28]. A Dutch study found that 8.4 percent of 331 patients with achalasia previously treated with pneumatic dilation developed Barrett's esophagus over a period of up to 25 years, with an annual incidence of Barrett's esophagus of 1.00 percent (95 percent CI: 0.62-1.37)[28]. A hazard ratio of 8.04 for developing Barrett's esophagus was found if a hiatal hernia was present[28]. Although a regular surveillance program for esophageal cancer as standard practice in patients with achalasia remains controversial, clinicians who treat patients with achalasia should be keenly aware of this association to enable detection of cancer in its early stages[28].

### Quality of Life Impairment and Associated Complications

Beyond specific pharmacological adverse effects, achalasia itself imposes substantial quality of life burdens that require recognition and management. Without appropriate treatment, achalasia leads to progressive deterioration. The longer achalasia remains untreated, the more problems patients are likely to have with accumulated food blocking the esophagus[16]. This in time may stretch the esophagus until it becomes dilated, potentially causing loss of its natural shape and ability to funnel food through to the stomach, which would result in any subsequent surgery being more extensive[16]. Loss of the ability to swallow food readily reaching the stomach affects the speed at which patients can eat and the quantity they can take in, potentially resulting in loss of weight and malnutrition in serious cases[16].

Linked to malnutrition, the ability to undertake sports or physical activity may be compromised due to lack of nutritional intake[16]. A loss of quality of life around food and problems of eating with others, for instance, can be psychologically distressing[16]. Stress and tension can make swallowing more difficult and can increase the likelihood of painful spasms[16]. Relaxation techniques and/or anxiolytic medication may help address this psychological component[16]. In severe cases, achalasia may lead to significant chest pain, fatigue, malnutrition, and weight loss[4]. If food particles enter the airways due to coughing and choking while eating, this can lead to pneumonia, inflammation in the airways that can become life-threatening[4].

## Combination Therapies and Multimodal Approaches

### Sequential and Combined Treatment Strategies

In clinical practice, combination therapy approaches are frequently employed to optimize symptom relief and extend duration of response in achalasia patients. Botulinum toxin injection, while effective, requires periodic reinjection that may provide progressively less benefit over time due to antibody formation[10]. Some patients do not adequately respond to balloon dilation or myotomy, which are the first-line treatment modalities in achalasia patients, yet BT injection can be performed in these patients, and symptomatic benefit can be expected in similar percentages as with BT injection in untreated patients[5]. Importantly, the initial response to BT in terms of symptom scores and lower esophageal sphincter pressure appears to be independent of any prior treatment (no previous treatment, balloon dilation, myotomy, or previous BT injection)[5].

For patients with persistent chest pain after Heller myotomy, neuromodulators have emerged as an effective treatment modality. In a retrospective study examining neuromodulators for treatment of spastic chest pain persisting after Heller myotomy in patients with achalasia, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) were found to be safe and effective[38]. Complete resolution of pain was observed in 50 percent of patients treated with neuromodulators including fluoxetine (20 milligrams) and amitriptyline (20 milligrams)[38]. This pharmacological approach addresses the neuropathic component of pain that may persist after mechanical relief of the obstructive component of achalasia.

### Pharmacological Adjuncts to Procedural Interventions

When considering combination therapy, the temporal relationship between pharmacological and procedural interventions is important. Medications may be used as bridge therapy to provide symptomatic relief while patients await more definitive procedures. Medications containing nitrates or calcium channel blockers can help temporarily relax the lower esophageal sphincter[4]. However, they must be taken before every meal, and their side effects usually outweigh the benefits[4]. These agents are best used as temporary bridges to more definitive therapy rather than as long-term monotherapy.

## Comparative Effectiveness and Clinical Trial Evidence

### Systematic Reviews and Network Meta-Analyses

A comprehensive network meta-analysis compared the efficacy of 8 treatment modalities for idiopathic achalasia, including botulinum toxin injection, pneumatic dilation, BT injection plus pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy without fundoplication, LHM with Dor fundoplication, LHM with Toupet fundoplication, POEM with anterior approach, and POEM with posterior approach[30]. Among 24 studies involving 1,987 participants, when compared with pneumatic dilation, POEM with anterior approach, POEM with posterior approach, LHM plus Toupet fundoplication, and LHM plus Dor fundoplication were all significantly superior in short-term efficacy, with POEM with anterior approach and LHM plus Dor showing better improvement in mid-term efficacy[30]. Botulinum toxin injection showed significantly lower efficacy than pneumatic dilation in both short-term and mid-term periods[30]. Regarding safety, only LHM without fundoplication was significantly associated with higher acid reflux than pneumatic dilation, while LHM plus Toupet fundoplication, LHM without fundoplication, and LHM plus Dor fundoplication showed a non-significant increase in moderate-to-severe adverse events[30]. Based on this evidence, the investigators suggested that POEM with anterior or posterior approach and LHM with Dor or Toupet fundoplication be initially recommended for idiopathic achalasia, while both LHM without fundoplication and BT injection are not recommended as definitive therapy[30].

### Achalasia Subtype-Dependent Treatment Response

Important evidence demonstrates that treatment outcomes are dependent on achalasia subtype, and this information can guide the choice of therapy[3][9]. Three achalasia subtypes have been defined based on high-resolution manometry findings in the esophageal body: type I (classic) with minimal contractility in the esophageal body, type II with intermittent periods of pan-esophageal pressurization, and type III (spastic) with premature or spastic distal esophageal contractions[9]. Type II achalasia is characterized by the production of 'pressure columns' due to pan-esophageal pressure through the hollow esophagus, with type III achalasia characterized by the presence of premature and/or spastic contractions[9].

This subtyping has improved clinical understanding of achalasia and influenced the management plan, enabling a more personalized therapeutic approach[9]. Type II achalasia showed the best response to pneumatic dilation, while type III achalasia was a strong negative predictor of response to pneumatic dilation[41]. In one study evaluating treatment outcome implications with either pneumatic dilation or laparoscopic Heller myotomy in 176 patients with achalasia, the Eckardt score was 3 or less post-intervention in 96 percent of patients with type II achalasia, 81 percent with type I achalasia, and 66 percent with type III achalasia[40]. The response to graded pneumatic dilation in type II achalasia was found to be 93 percent compared to type I (81 percent) or III (86 percent), demonstrating the superior response of type II disease to this treatment[40].

For type III achalasia, POEM appears to offer superior outcomes compared to traditional approaches. Kumbhari and colleagues conducted a multicenter comparative study that evaluated treatment outcomes of 75 patients with type III achalasia and showed an excellent treatment success rate of 98 percent following POEM compared to 80 percent after laparoscopic Heller myotomy during both short-term (8.6 months) and long-term (21.5 months) follow-up (P less than 0.01)[9]. These findings demonstrate the importance of achalasia phenotyping in guiding optimal treatment selection.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The pharmacological management of achalasia represents a complex and evolving landscape of therapeutic options, each with distinct advantages, limitations, and appropriate clinical contexts. Botulinum toxin injection remains the most commonly used and most effective pharmacological therapy for achalasia, demonstrating excellent short-term safety and efficacy profiles[1][1]. However, its limited durability, typically lasting 6 to 12 months, and the gradual diminishment of effectiveness with repeated injections due to antibody formation necessitate ongoing investigation into alternative and adjunctive approaches[1][1]. Nitrates and calcium channel blockers, while providing transient symptomatic benefit, are limited by modest efficacy, frequent adverse effects including hypotension and headache, and the development of tachyphylaxis in substantial proportions of patients[1][1].

The accumulating evidence for autoimmune pathophysiology in achalasia has opened new therapeutic avenues through investigation of immunomodulatory agents including corticosteroids and potential development of more targeted immunosuppressive therapies[21][23]. Preliminary data supporting clinical benefit of short-term corticosteroid therapy in select achalasia patients warrants further investigation with larger, well-controlled trials[22]. The role of potential infectious triggers, particularly herpes simplex virus type 1, in initiating or perpetuating achalasia pathophysiology requires further clarification and may lead to novel therapeutic interventions combining immunomodulation with antimicrobial approaches[21][47].

Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors represent a pharmacological approach with sound theoretical basis grounded in achalasia pathophysiology, yet limited clinical advancement reflects concerns about short duration of action, modest efficacy, and questions about long-term tolerability[14]. Replacement or potentiation of vasoactive intestinal peptide, given the documented loss of VIP-containing neurons in achalasia, remains an attractive future direction, though therapeutic development in this area has been limited[29].

Critical to optimal patient management is recognition of contraindicated medications that may worsen esophageal function. Chronic opioid therapy represents a particularly important risk factor for development or exacerbation of achalasia that warrants clinical attention and patient counseling[26]. Anticholinergic medications, certain psychotropic agents, and medications that cause direct esophageal injury should be used with caution in achalasia patients and avoided when therapeutically feasible alternatives exist[17][27].

The substantial cancer risk associated with achalasia, with 50-fold elevation in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma incidence, remains a sobering reminder of the serious long-term consequences of this disease[28]. Although controversial, consideration of surveillance strategies in high-risk achalasia populations warrants ongoing discussion among clinicians managing these patients.

Finally, the demonstrated importance of achalasia phenotyping through high-resolution manometry in predicting treatment response supports personalized medicine approaches to therapy selection[9][41]. Future pharmaceutical development should ideally focus on disease-modifying agents capable of restoring myenteric plexus function and esophageal peristalsis rather than merely providing symptomatic relief through destruction or denervation of the lower esophageal sphincter. The convergence of improved pathophysiological understanding, emerging immunomodulatory approaches, and refinement of endoscopic and surgical techniques offers hope for transformative advances in achalasia management in the coming years.
