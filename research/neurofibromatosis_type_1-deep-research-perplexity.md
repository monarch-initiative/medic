# Comprehensive Review of Drug Therapies for Neurofibromatosis Type 1: Approved Treatments, Investigational Agents, and Therapeutic Opportunities

This comprehensive research report synthesizes current evidence on pharmacological interventions for neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), an autosomal-dominant genetic disorder affecting approximately one in 2,500 to 3,500 individuals worldwide[5]. The landscape of NF1 treatment has undergone transformative change in recent years, with two MEK inhibitors recently receiving FDA approval specifically for symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas—selumetinib (KOSELUGO) in November 2025 for adults and mirdametinib (GOMEKLI) in February 2025 for children as young as two years old and adults[1][3][5]. These approvals represent the culmination of decades of research into the pathophysiological mechanisms driving NF1-associated tumorigenesis, particularly the understanding that loss of neurofibromin function leads to constitutive activation of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway[5][32]. Beyond these landmark approvals, numerous investigational agents are in various stages of clinical development, drug repurposing candidates are being explored, and emerging evidence supports combination therapeutic strategies that may further improve outcomes for this medically complex population. This report provides a detailed analysis of approved medications, pipeline agents, evidence-based off-label uses, and emerging therapeutic opportunities while emphasizing the importance of comprehensive safety monitoring and individualized treatment planning.

## Pathophysiological Basis for Pharmacological Intervention in Neurofibromatosis Type 1

Understanding the molecular underpinnings of NF1 is essential for comprehending why specific pharmacological agents have been selected for therapeutic development. Neurofibromatosis type 1 results from pathogenic variants in the NF1 gene located on chromosome 17q11.2, which encodes neurofibromin, a critical negative regulator of RAS signaling[5][32]. Neurofibromin functions as a RAS GTPase-activating protein (RAS-GAP) that accelerates the conversion of active GTP-bound RAS to its inactive GDP-bound state[14][32]. When mutations cause loss of neurofibromin function, this regulatory mechanism fails, resulting in constitutive RAS activation and downstream hyperactivation of multiple signaling cascades[5][14][32]. Chief among these is the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK (MAPK/ERK) signaling cascade, which regulates cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and migration[5][8]. Additionally, loss of neurofibromin leads to activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, which promotes cellular growth and survival[14][14][32]. This multilayered dysregulation of signaling pathways drives the development of benign and malignant tumors characteristic of NF1[14][32].

The discovery that NF1-associated tumors are driven primarily by hyperactivation of the RAS–MAPK pathway provided a rational basis for targeted drug development[25]. Advances in cancer biology had previously demonstrated that inhibition of MEK, the kinase immediately upstream of ERK in this cascade, could produce substantial tumor growth inhibition with acceptable tolerability in patients with other malignancies such as BRAF-mutated melanoma[8]. This mechanistic similarity, coupled with preclinical data demonstrating that MEK inhibition could reduce neurofibroma volume and proliferation in animal models, laid the groundwork for clinical trials of MEK inhibitors in NF1 patients[8][11][25]. The clinical success of these agents has validated the RAS–MAPK pathway as a primary therapeutic target in NF1, though emerging research suggests that optimal outcomes may require combination approaches targeting multiple downstream effectors simultaneously[16].

## Approved Pharmacological Therapies for Neurofibromatosis Type 1

### Selumetinib: Pioneering MEK Inhibitor for Inoperable Plexiform Neurofibromas

Selumetinib (AstraZeneca Pharmaceuticals LP, marketed as KOSELUGO) represents the first FDA-approved medication specifically indicated for treating neurofibromatosis type 1-associated tumors. This oral selective inhibitor of MEK1 and MEK2 locks these kinases into an inactive conformation while still permitting ATP binding and substrate binding, thereby disrupting the molecular interactions required for proper ERK phosphorylation and activation[14][22]. The drug's development and approval pathway was guided by the landmark SPRINT trial, a phase II study that enrolled 50 children with NF1 and symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas (PNs)—tumors that grow along nerve sheaths in a diffuse, infiltrative pattern that typically makes surgical resection impossible or prohibitively morbid[34]. The SPRINT trial demonstrated that selumetinib produced partial responses (defined as ≥20% volumetric reduction) in 68% of pediatric patients, with 82% of responders maintaining their response for at least one year[11][34]. Beyond tumor shrinkage, children treated with selumetinib experienced clinically meaningful improvements in pain (85% of patients with baseline pain), quality of life (70% of patients), and functional outcomes related to their plexiform neurofibromas[22][26]. Based on these compelling results, the FDA granted accelerated approval to selumetinib for pediatric patients one year of age and older in April 2020[10][26].

The success in pediatric populations prompted evaluation of selumetinib in adult patients with NF1 and symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas through the KOMET trial, a pivotal international, randomized, placebo-controlled phase III study that was recently completed[1][1]. The KOMET trial enrolled 145 adults (aged 18 years or older) with symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas, defined as tumors that could not be completely removed without substantial risk for morbidity due to encasement or proximity to vital structures, invasiveness, or high vascularity[1][1]. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive selumetinib 25 mg/m² orally twice daily or placebo for 12 cycles, with the primary efficacy endpoint being confirmed overall response rate (ORR) by cycle 16 assessed by independent central review using Response Evaluation in Neurofibromatosis and Schwannomatosis (REiNS) criteria[1][1]. Selumetinib demonstrated a confirmed ORR of 20% (95% CI: 11, 31) compared to 5% (95% CI: 2, 13) for placebo (p-value 0.011), with a median time to response of 3.7 months[1][35]. Among responders, 86% maintained their response for at least six months[1][1]. Notably, among patients with baseline chronic pain intensity scores of at least 3, selumetinib produced a greater reduction in pain scores and clinically meaningful improvement, though these differences did not reach statistical significance[35]. On November 19, 2025, the FDA expanded selumetinib approval to include adult patients based on KOMET trial results, extending the indication to adults with symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas[1][1].

The recommended dosing for selumetinib in both pediatric and adult populations is 25 mg/m² orally twice daily until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity[1][1]. The drug has been assigned orphan drug designation, reflecting the unmet medical need in this rare disease population[1]. Selumetinib is available in multiple formulations to accommodate different patient populations: capsules for patients able to swallow them, and oral granules or liquid formulations for younger children or those with swallowing difficulties[2][26].

### Mirdametinib: Second-Generation MEK Inhibitor with Dual Efficacy in Pediatric and Adult Populations

Mirdametinib (SpringWorks Therapeutics, marketed as GOMEKLI), a potent selective non-ATP-competitive inhibitor of MEK1 and MEK2, was approved by the FDA on February 11, 2025, marking the second FDA approval for an agent treating NF1-associated plexiform neurofibromas[3][5]. This approval was supported by the pivotal ReNeu phase IIb trial, which was the largest multicenter NF1-PN trial reported to date and enrolled both adult (n=58) and pediatric (n=56) patients with symptomatic, unresectable plexiform neurofibromas causing significant morbidities[5][33]. The trial design differed notably from SPRINT and KOMET by using a 21-days-on, 7-days-off dosing schedule within 28-day cycles at a dose of 2 mg/m² orally twice daily (maximum 4 mg twice daily)[5][33]. This intermittent dosing approach contrasts with the continuous dosing schedules studied for other MEK inhibitors.

The ReNeu trial demonstrated confirmed objective response rates by blinded independent central review of 41% in adults (24 of 58 patients) and 52% in children (29 of 56 patients), with median target plexiform neurofibroma volumetric reductions of -41% in adults and -42% in children[5][33]. Importantly, the trial included a long-term follow-up phase, during which two additional adults and one additional child achieved confirmed responses, bringing the total response rates to approximately 45% in adults and 54% in children when accounting for delayed responses[5][33]. The depth of tumor shrinkage was notable, with best volumetric responses ranging from -90% to 13% in adults and -91% to 48% in children[33]. Both adult and pediatric cohorts reported significant and clinically meaningful improvements beginning early in treatment and sustained throughout the study period in patient-reported or parent proxy-reported outcome measures including worst tumor pain severity, pain interference, and health-related quality of life[5][33]. The trial demonstrated that mirdametinib was well-tolerated across both age groups, with the most commonly reported treatment-related adverse events being dermatitis acneiform, diarrhea, and nausea in adults, and dermatitis acneiform, diarrhea, and paronychia in children[33].

The FDA approval of mirdametinib represents a significant expansion of treatment options for NF1 patients, as it is approved for use in patients as young as two years of age—younger than selumetinib's minimum approved age—through adults[3][5]. Like selumetinib, mirdametinib has been included in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines as a category 2A recommendation for treatment of symptomatic plexiform neurofibromas in adult and pediatric patients (≥2 years) not amenable to complete resection[17]. Mirdametinib is available in both capsule and oral suspension (tablet for oral suspension) formulations to accommodate patients of different ages and those with swallowing difficulties[5].

### Comparative Efficacy and Safety Between Approved MEK Inhibitors

In the absence of head-to-head clinical trials directly comparing selumetinib and mirdametinib, indirect treatment comparisons have been conducted to assess their relative efficacy and safety profiles. An indirect treatment comparison analyzing efficacy and safety data from pediatric patients with NF1-PN demonstrated that mirdametinib showed a significantly greater mean best percent reduction from baseline in target plexiform neurofibroma volume compared with selumetinib (difference in mean of approximately 15-20%, p<0.05)[23]. Additionally, the indirect treatment comparison revealed that all-grade treatment-related adverse event odds ratios were significantly lower with mirdametinib versus selumetinib for dermatitis acneiform, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, blood creatine phosphokinase elevation, dry skin, pruritus, constipation, abdominal pain, stomatitis, hair color change, headache, and neutrophil count decrease (all p<0.05)[23]. Importantly, the odds ratio of dose reductions due to treatment-related adverse events was significantly lower with mirdametinib compared to selumetinib[23]. These findings suggest potential advantages of mirdametinib in terms of both efficacy and tolerability, though the differences observed in indirect comparisons do not necessarily establish superiority and may reflect differences in trial design, patient populations, and outcome measurement approaches.

## Investigational and Pipeline Pharmacological Agents for Neurofibromatosis Type 1

### MEK Inhibitor Pipeline: Trametinib and FCN-159

Beyond selumetinib and mirdametinib, other MEK inhibitors are under investigation for NF1-associated tumors. Trametinib, a MEK inhibitor approved for melanoma and other malignancies, has been employed off-label in NF1 patients and is the subject of ongoing clinical trials[20]. A phase II trial of trametinib in children and adults with NF1 and plexiform neurofibromas (the NF108-BINI study, NCT03231306) has been evaluating the drug in patients one year of age and older with progressive neurofibromas or those causing significant morbidity[7]. The trial utilized a dosing approach with careful volumetric MRI assessment, with patients able to receive additional courses beyond an initial 8-cycle treatment period if achieving at least a 15% reduction in tumor volume[7]. Results from this trial indicated that at least 50% of patients met the partial response target of ≥20% plexiform neurofibroma volume reduction[8].

FCN-159, a novel MEK inhibitor currently under investigation, is being studied in a phase I/IIb trial (NCT04954001) enrolling both adult and pediatric participants (age not specified in available materials) with neurofibromatosis type 1 and inoperable plexiform neurofibromas[29]. This trial is evaluating the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetic characteristics, and anti-tumor activity of FCN-159, with the goal of establishing an optimal dose for future clinical development[29]. FCN-159 represents an important addition to the pipeline given the potential for additional mechanistic insights or improved tolerability profiles compared to currently available agents.

### Combination Therapies and Rational Drug Combinations

Emerging evidence supports the hypothesis that combining MEK inhibitors with agents targeting complementary pathways may enhance therapeutic efficacy while potentially allowing dose reduction of individual agents to mitigate adverse effects. Recent research has demonstrated synergistic effects of combining selumetinib with heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) inhibitors including SNX-2112 and retaspimycin in plexiform neurofibroma cell lines and in vivo xenograft models[16]. The rationale for this combination stems from the observation that HSP90 inhibition may overcome treatment resistance mediated by stem-like tumor populations within neurofibromas[16]. A study combining selumetinib at a dose 20% lower than standard monotherapy with HSP90 inhibitors demonstrated better tumor inhibition compared to selumetinib monotherapy, while delaying progression in mouse models of plexiform neurofibromas[16]. These findings provide compelling rationale for future clinical trials of combination MEK-HSP90 inhibition in NF1.

Additionally, combined MEK and SHP2 inhibition has shown promise in preclinical studies for treating NF1-associated bone complications, particularly tibial pseudarthrosis—a serious skeletal manifestation of NF1[41]. Research demonstrated that while MEK inhibition alone or SHP2 inhibition alone showed limited effects on bone healing in NF1-deficient models, the combination of selumetinib and the SHP2 inhibitor SHP099 was uniquely effective at promoting bone and cartilage formation while reducing fibrosis accumulation in fracture calluses[41]. This combination approach corrected cell fate impairment in NF1-deficient progenitor cells and Schwann cells, suggesting potential for future clinical translation[41]. The mechanistic basis for this synergy involves complementary inhibition of RAS-MAPK signaling at different pathway nodes—MEK acts downstream of RAS, while SHP2 is a master positive regulator of RAS-MAPK signaling upstream of RAS[41]. Several clinical trials are now exploring combination therapies in early phases, with ongoing research likely to yield additional combination regimens worthy of clinical evaluation[26][26].

### Investigational Agents Targeting Non-MAPK Pathways

While most investigational agents for NF1 target the RAS-MAPK pathway, emerging research suggests that alternative mechanisms may offer additional benefit. The mTOR inhibitor everolimus has been evaluated in a phase II trial for recurrent, radiographically progressive NF1-associated low-grade glioma, given the activation of the PI3K/mTOR pathway observed in these tumors[36]. The trial demonstrated significant disease stability or shrinkage during everolimus treatment, with 10 of 15 evaluable patients remaining free of progression at median follow-up of 33 months[36]. Treatment was generally well-tolerated, with no patients discontinuing due to toxicity[36]. These findings establish everolimus as a potential option for progressive NF1-associated low-grade glioma, particularly in patients who have failed or are intolerant of conventional chemotherapy.

Sorafenib, a multi-targeted kinase inhibitor with activity against RAF, VEGFR, and other tyrosine kinases, has been evaluated in preclinical models and early clinical trials for NF1-associated neurofibromas[13][19]. A phase I trial in pediatric patients evaluated the combination of sorafenib and topotecan (a topoisomerase I inhibitor), determining a maximum tolerated dose of sorafenib 150 mg/m² twice daily combined with topotecan 1.4 mg/m² daily[19]. While the combination was tolerable, activity in the studied population was limited[19]. A separate preclinical study in an NF1 mouse model demonstrated that sorafenib significantly decreased neurofibroma growth rate, with volumetric measurements showing significant reductions compared to vehicle controls[13]. These findings support continued investigation of sorafenib in appropriately designed clinical trials.

### Topical MEK Inhibitors for Cutaneous Manifestations

A significant unmet need exists for effective treatments of cutaneous neurofibromas in NF1, which can cause cosmetic concerns, itching, and discomfort[31][38]. NFX-179, a metabolically labile topical MEK inhibitor applied as a gel formulation, has been investigated for this indication. A phase IIa trial enrolled 48 participants with NF1 and cutaneous neurofibromas randomized to four treatment arms: NFX-179 Topical Gel at concentrations of 0.05%, 0.15%, 0.50%, or vehicle control, applied once daily to five target cutaneous neurofibromas for 28 days[38]. The trial demonstrated dose-dependent reduction in phosphorylated ERK levels in cutaneous neurofibromas at day 28, with a 47% decrease in phosphorylated ERK in the 0.5% NFX-179 group compared to vehicle (p=0.0001)[38]. Critically, no local or systemic toxicities were observed during the treatment period, and systemic concentrations of NFX-179 remained below 1 ng/mL, suggesting minimal systemic exposure[38]. With regard to efficacy, 20% of cutaneous neurofibromas treated with 0.5% NFX-179 Topical Gel demonstrated a ≥50% reduction in volume compared to 6% in the vehicle group by ruler measurement with calculated volume (p=0.021)[38]. These preliminary findings provide proof of concept for topical MEK inhibition in cutaneous neurofibromas and support advancement to larger, longer-duration trials. A subsequent clinical trial testing higher concentrations of NFX-179 (0.50% and 1.5%) against vehicle is ongoing (NCT05005845), with preliminary results likely to further elucidate the therapeutic potential of this approach[31].

## Drug Repurposing Candidates and Off-Label Uses in Neurofibromatosis Type 1

### Lovastatin: Restoration of Synaptic Plasticity and Cognitive Function

An innovative approach to NF1 treatment involves drug repurposing—the application of medications approved for other indications to treat NF1 based on mechanistic rationale or emerging clinical evidence. Lovastatin, a 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor approved for hypercholesterolemia treatment, has emerged as a promising candidate based on its potent inhibition of RAS/MAPK pathway activity[12]. Computational and preclinical studies demonstrated that lovastatin downregulation of hyperactive RAS pathway activity leads to improvement of synaptic plasticity and restoration of learning deficits and attention in mouse models of NF1[12]. This mechanistic finding prompted a placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blind crossover trial in patients with NF1 assessing the effects of a four-day course of lovastatin on markers of synaptic plasticity and neuropsychological function[12]. Using transcranial magnetic stimulation paired associative stimulation (PAS)—a technique measuring long-term potentiation-like plasticity—researchers found that lovastatin normalized increased intracortical inhibition, improved long-term potentiation (LTP)-like plasticity, and improved phasic alertness compared to placebo[12]. Specifically, short intracortical facilitation and short intracortical inhibition were significantly improved following lovastatin treatment, and these improvements correlated with enhanced attention performance[12]. These proof-of-principle findings raise the possibility that lovastatin could be investigated in longer-duration trials to assess potential benefits on cognitive function and learning disabilities, which affect approximately 65% of individuals with NF1[10]. However, it should be noted that despite promising preliminary data, lovastatin has not yet been systematically studied in large clinical trials for NF1-related cognitive impairment, and clinical translation remains exploratory at this stage.

### mTOR Inhibitors and Growth Factor Signaling

Beyond everolimus, the mTOR pathway represents a potential therapeutic target in NF1 given that activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling contributes to cell growth and survival downstream of RAS hyperactivation[14][14][32]. Rapamycin (sirolimus) and its derivatives have been investigated in NF1-associated tumors. Preclinical studies demonstrated that the mTOR inhibitor RAD001 (everolimus) could inhibit the proliferation of neurofibromin-deficient cells in vitro, providing mechanistic support for clinical investigation[31]. While mTOR inhibitor monotherapy has shown modest activity in some NF1 contexts, emerging evidence suggests that optimal benefit may require combination with MEK inhibitors to comprehensively suppress RAS-MAPK pathway hyperactivation[14][36].

### Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors and Immunomodulatory Approaches

Imatinib mesylate, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor with activity against c-KIT that is approved for chronic myeloid leukemia and gastrointestinal stromal tumors, has been investigated in NF1 based on the rationale that c-KIT-expressing mast cells within the neurofibroma microenvironment contribute to tumor progression through paracrine signaling[14][31]. A phase II trial with imatinib reported a 17% response rate (≥20% tumor reduction), though study limitations including small sample size and population heterogeneity may have underestimated its therapeutic effect[14]. Sunitinib malate, a powerful selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor with activity against c-Kit, PDGFR, and VEGFR, demonstrated preclinical activity with potential to reduce plexiform neurofibroma number and size and decrease mast cell infiltration[14]. However, a phase II trial with sunitinib (NCT01402817) was prematurely terminated following one patient death of uncertain but possibly drug-related causes[14].

Emerging evidence also suggests potential roles for immunomodulatory approaches in NF1. The immune microenvironment profoundly influences neurofibroma development and malignant transformation, with mast cells, macrophages, and T cells all contributing to the pathological milieu[43]. A preclinical study investigating PLX3397, a selective c-Fms and c-Kit inhibitor combined with rapamycin (TORC1 inhibitor) to deplete tumor-promoting macrophages, showed efficacy with overall reduction in malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor cell proliferation[43]. These findings provide rationale for future clinical investigation of immune-targeting approaches in NF1-associated malignancies.

## Safety Considerations, Adverse Event Management, and Long-Term Monitoring

### Comprehensive Adverse Event Profile of Approved MEK Inhibitors

The clinical utility of MEK inhibitors in NF1 is substantially influenced by their adverse event profiles and the feasibility of managing associated toxicities. Both selumetinib and mirdametinib carry labeling warnings and precautions for several serious potential adverse effects, necessitating comprehensive baseline and ongoing monitoring protocols. The prescribing information for selumetinib includes warnings for left ventricular dysfunction, ocular toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, skin toxicity, increased creatine phosphokinase, increased vitamin E levels, increased bleeding risk, and embryo-fetal toxicity[1][1]. The most common adverse events reported in clinical trials include elevated creatine phosphokinase levels, rash, acneiform dermatitis, paronychia, alopecia, abdominal pain, and deep vein thrombosis[15].

A comprehensive pharmacovigilance analysis using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database identified 48 preferred terms from 15 organ systems as having disproportionate adverse event signals associated with selumetinib[15]. Among all adverse event reports, the most relatively frequent included elevated blood creatine phosphokinase (11.40%), rash (9.76%), acneiform dermatitis (6.27%), paronychia (6.04%), and alopecia (4.88%)[15]. Notably, several serious adverse events not documented in the selumetinib package insert were identified through FAERS analysis, including rhabdomyolysis, serous retinopathy, urinary frequency, poor wound healing, ingrown nails, and proteinuria[15]. The study findings underscored the importance of early detection and management of adverse reactions associated with selumetinib, particularly within the initial month of treatment[15].

### Cardiac Toxicity and Monitoring Requirements

Cardiac toxicity represents one of the most serious potential adverse effects of MEK inhibitor therapy, though it remains relatively rare. In early trials of trametinib in adults with melanoma, 7% of patients experienced a decrease in left ventricular ejection fraction of >10%[8]. While the majority of reported cases are asymptomatic, grade 3 (or symptomatic) cardiac toxicity has been documented[8]. Patients with preexisting cardiac disease, including QTc prolongation, arrhythmias, or uncontrolled hypertension, appear to be at higher risk for serious cardiac toxicities[8]. However, in reported trials, cardiac function generally normalized after discontinuation of therapy, suggesting reversibility of the adverse effect[8]. In NF1 pediatric patients treated with selumetinib, an asymptomatic decrease in ejection fraction was noted in one child in the highest dose range (above the current recommended dose)[8]. Given the potential for serious cardiac complications and the reversibility of cardiac dysfunction, most providers recommend routine cardiac monitoring while on MEK inhibitor therapy[8][8]. Standard of care typically includes baseline echocardiography or multigated acquisition scan, repeat assessment after one month of therapy initiation, and then every three to six months during continued treatment[8][8]. Providers should maintain vigilance for common cardiac symptoms such as peripheral edema, murmurs, and dyspnea. Left ventricular dysfunction can develop as early as two weeks into therapy or as late as two years after initiation[8]. For changes in left ventricular ejection fraction of >10% from baseline, MEK inhibitor therapy should typically be held for up to four weeks, with resumption at a lower dose if cardiac function recovers to baseline, and permanent discontinuation if recovery does not occur within four weeks or if ongoing cardiac symptoms develop[8].

### Ocular Toxicity and Ophthalmologic Monitoring

Ocular complications represent another significant safety concern with MEK inhibitor therapy. Multiple ocular adverse effects have been documented, including reduction in visual acuity, dry eye syndrome, visual field abnormalities, panuveitis, MEK-associated retinopathy (MEKAR), and retinal vein occlusion[28]. The most common side effect reported in phase I and phase II clinical trials is serous detachment of the neurosensory retina, also known as MEK-associated retinopathy, observed in as many as 65% of study participants[28]. Retinal vein occlusion represents a potentially sight-threatening complication, and retinal pigment epithelial detachment has also been observed[1][5]. Based on these findings, the standard of care involves baseline retinal examinations with optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging before initiating MEK inhibitor therapy, with multiple close follow-up appointments after drug initiation[28]. The mirdametinib label specifically recommends baseline and periodic ophthalmologic examinations because of the risk of retinal vein occlusion and retinal pigment epithelial detachment[5]. A retrospective analysis of 26 patients with NF1 treated with different MEK inhibitors (selumetinib 77%, trametinib 23%, mirdametinib 4%) with an average follow-up of 413 days found that none of the patients experienced retinopathy during the study period, suggesting that with appropriate monitoring and management, serious retinal complications may be preventable[28]. Four patients (15%) exhibited symptoms of dry eye, all of which were effectively managed with topical lubrication[28]. Notably, among patients with pre-existing optic neuropathies (27% of the cohort), no instances of nerve changes occurred after commencing MEK inhibitor therapy[28].

### Dermatologic Adverse Effects

Skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders represent the most common adverse effects of MEK inhibitors, with particular prominence of acneiform dermatitis, paronychia, and xerosis[15][8]. In a prospective study of 20 patients treated with selumetinib for NF1, skin side effects were nearly universal, including xerosis, paronychia, and acneiform rash[15]. While these effects are generally manageable and rarely life-threatening, they can significantly impact patient quality of life and may necessitate dose modifications or treatment interruptions[8]. A retrospective study found that 76% of patients using selumetinib experienced an increase in creatine phosphokinase (CPK), with 9% experiencing grade 3 or higher elevations, and 7% requiring dose reduction due to CPK elevation[15]. Clinical guidelines recommend careful skin monitoring, proactive management with topical agents and oral antibiotics for acneiform dermatitis, and consideration of dose reduction for intolerable dermatologic toxicity[8][8].

### Embryo-Fetal Toxicity and Reproductive Considerations

Mirdametinib is teratogenic; accordingly, the product label mandates pregnancy testing and appropriate contraception counseling[5]. Similarly, selumetinib carries warnings regarding embryo-fetal toxicity[1][1]. These warnings reflect preclinical findings of developmental toxicity and necessitate careful counseling of reproductive-age patients and implementation of appropriate contraceptive measures during treatment. Patients of childbearing potential should undergo baseline pregnancy testing before initiating therapy and regular pregnancy testing during treatment. The teratogenic potential of MEK inhibitors must be carefully weighed against the therapeutic benefits when considering these agents in reproductive-age women, and alternative therapies should be explored when feasible for pregnant patients or those planning pregnancy.

### Gastrointestinal Toxicity

Gastrointestinal side effects, including diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, are commonly reported with MEK inhibitors[15][8]. These effects are generally manageable with standard antiemetic and antidiarrheal medications, but severe cases may necessitate dose modification or treatment interruption. The indirect treatment comparison between selumetinib and mirdametinib demonstrated significantly lower odds ratios for all-grade diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting with mirdametinib compared to selumetinib, suggesting potential differences in tolerability between these agents[23].

## Therapeutic Approaches for NF1-Associated Pain and Symptom Management

### Pain Management in Neurofibromatosis Type 1

Pain represents one of the most burdensome symptoms affecting quality of life in individuals with NF1, with limited approved pharmacological options historically available. A retrospective study of adults with NF1 found that a substantial proportion (55%, n=139) had been prescribed opioid medication at some point in their lifetime to manage pain symptoms[27]. Importantly, among patients currently prescribed opioid medication, 85% indicated "little to no effectiveness" when qualitatively asked to describe their medication's efficacy[27]. Furthermore, frequent somnolent effects were commonly reported, with many patients describing feeling "too sleepy" or "lazy" from opioid therapy[27]. Additional medications commonly prescribed for NF1-related pain included GABA analogs (gabapentin, pregabalin) in 27% of patients and benzodiazepines in 10%, though these were noted to be equally ineffective in many patients[27]. Notably, 51% of adults actively used over-the-counter medications for pain management[27]. These findings underscore the urgent need for effective pain management strategies in this population.

The emergence of approved MEK inhibitors has provided the first pharmacological agents with documented efficacy in reducing NF1-related pain. In the KOMET trial evaluating selumetinib in adults with symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas, among patients with baseline chronic pain intensity scores of at least 3, selumetinib produced a greater reduction in pain scores and clinically meaningful improvement compared to placebo[35]. Similarly, the ReNeu trial of mirdametinib demonstrated that both adult and pediatric cohorts reported significant and clinically meaningful improvements in worst tumor pain severity, pain interference, and health-related quality of life beginning early in treatment and sustained throughout the study period[33]. These improvements in pain with MEK inhibitor therapy appear to result from tumor shrinkage and associated reduction in mechanical compression and irritation of involved nerves, rather than from primary analgesic effects of the drugs themselves.

### Psychological and Behavioral Interventions for Pain

In recognition of the limitations of pharmacological pain management in NF1, research has increasingly focused on psychological and behavioral interventions. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), a form of cognitive-behavioral therapy emphasizing acceptance of pain and commitment to valued living, has been studied in NF1 patients with plexiform-related pain[26][26]. A randomized controlled trial enrolling people with NF1 and plexiform-related pain (ages 16-56) demonstrated that those randomized to immediate ACT had significantly decreased pain interference compared to a wait-list control group[26][26]. The data revealed that patients' willingness to experience pain to connect with what matters most to them (their values) was a key factor driving improvements in pain interference[26][26]. In 2023, researchers at Massachusetts General Hospital published findings from a study testing a mind-body intervention delivered through weekly online modules for eight weeks in mostly adults with NF1[26][26]. Results showed that the intervention was helpful in increasing gratitude, coping, and mindfulness[26][26]. These findings establish psychological and behavioral interventions as valuable adjunctive approaches to pain management in NF1 patients.

### Complementary and Physical Therapies

Evidence suggests that complementary treatments including yoga, massage therapy, and physical therapy have demonstrated effectiveness in improving pain thresholds in NF1 populations[27]. A significant relationship was shown between increased usage of complementary treatments and decreased pain severity and interference scores[27]. In other populations with similar prevalence and co-occurring pain symptoms, such as cystic fibrosis and fibromyalgia, complementary treatments have been shown to reduce effects of chronic and acute pain symptoms[27]. These findings suggest that consideration of alternative treatments and careful monitoring of more conservative or lower-risk therapeutic approaches may improve pain management and reduce the risk of developing medication dependence.

### Emerging Interventional Approaches

A novel case report described the successful use of ultrasound-guided perineural steroid injection for treatment of NF1 neurofibroma-related pain[30]. The case involved a young female patient with intermittent buttock pain radiating down the leg diagnosed with neurofibroma sciatic nerve compression, who was successfully managed with perineural steroid injection and experienced significant symptomatic improvement within one week and complete pain resolution at one month follow-up[30]. Although this represents a single case and sparse literature exists regarding the efficacy of this approach in NF1 patients, it provides a proof-of-concept example of an intervention that may be considered when neurofibroma-related pain fails to respond to conservative treatment[30]. Perineural steroid injections offer an alternative to oral medical management while avoiding the often substantial morbidity associated with surgical intervention[30].

## Treatment of NF1-Associated Psychiatric Comorbidities

### Dopaminergic Dysfunction and Psychiatric Symptoms in NF1

Psychiatric comorbidities, including depression, anxiety, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), are frequently observed in patients with NF1, with estimated prevalence rates of depression (76.8%), anxiety (53.7%), and ADHD (23.2%) in affected individuals[21][21]. Evidence suggests that these psychiatric manifestations may have a neurobiological basis related to dysregulation of dopaminergic neurotransmission[21][21]. Animal models of NF1 have demonstrated decreased dopamine levels, though post-synapric dopamine receptor expression remains unchanged[21][21]. Based on this mechanistic understanding, the administration of methylphenidate or L-dopa has been demonstrated to normalize dopamine levels and lead to increased attention-related and exploratory behavior in mice with NF1[21][21]. A year-long clinical study of children with NF1 found that methylphenidate administration significantly improved cognitive and academic performance as well as social deficits[21][21]. Double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trials have demonstrated the efficacy of methylphenidate in reducing ADHD symptoms at four weeks and cognitive symptoms at six weeks[21][21].

### Psychopharmacological Management Strategies

A real-world retrospective study examining psychiatric medication use in 82 NF1 patients with comorbid psychiatric diagnoses revealed important insights regarding optimal pharmacological management[21][21]. The use of second-generation antipsychotic agent augmentation therapy or hydroxyzine monotherapy was associated with significantly more behavioral health-related emergency department visits, admissions, and inpatient days in the study population[21][21]. Conversely, the use of bupropion augmentation therapy, buspirone augmentation therapy, and stimulant medications was associated with improved clinical outcomes, though these results did not reach statistical significance[21][21]. These findings suggest that dopamine-blocking therapy with antipsychotic medications may be associated with worse clinical outcomes in NF1 patients, while dopamine-enhancing therapy appears beneficial[21][21]. The use of antidepressants with serotoninergic activity, including imipramine and fluoxetine, has been shown to increase neurogenesis and improve behavioral symptoms in NF1 mice and may merit further clinical investigation[21].

## Other Emerging and Investigational Therapeutic Approaches

### Treatment of NF1-Associated Low-Grade Gliomas

Low-grade gliomas, particularly optic pathway gliomas, represent a common and potentially vision-threatening complication of NF1, occurring in 15-20% of children[6][44]. Historically, treatment approaches for progressive NF1-associated low-grade gliomas have been limited to chemotherapy (most commonly carboplatin and vincristine), surgery when feasible, or observation[14][44]. Recent studies have demonstrated compelling activity of MEK inhibitors in this indication. A phase II trial of selumetinib in children with NF1-associated low-grade glioma demonstrated at least partial response (≥50% tumor reduction on MRI) in 40% of patients[14]. These preliminary results suggest comparable efficacy to conventional chemotherapy with potentially superior tolerability and safety profile[14]. A phase 2 trial of selumetinib in children with recurrent optic pathway and hypothalamic low-grade glioma without NF1 showed very promising activity with significantly improved response rates compared to historical studies with single-agent vinblastine or bevacizumab plus irinotecan[20]. Importantly, functional outcomes improved in some patients, with 4 of 19 evaluable patients having improvement in visual acuity and 5 of 19 showing improvement in visual fields[20]. The progression-free survival was encouraging compared to historical studies, prompting many clinicians to consider MEK inhibition in patients with progressive visual loss[20]. The Children's Oncology Group has recently opened a phase III trial (NCT04166409) randomizing between carboplatin/vincristine and selumetinib monotherapy specifically in NF1 patients with progressive low-grade glioma, which will provide head-to-head efficacy and safety data comparing these approaches.

### Treatment of NF1-Associated Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST) represent one of the most serious oncological complications of NF1, with patients carrying an 8-13% lifetime risk of developing these aggressive tumors[40]. Unless complete surgical resection with wide margins is feasible, the five-year survival prospects are poor[40]. Approximately 91% of NF1-related MPNST develop from pre-existing distinct nodular lesions identifiable on MRI, with a median time from detection to malignant transformation of 5.1 years[40]. This finding suggests potential value of surveillance strategies and surgical resection of pre-malignant lesions when feasible to prevent malignant transformation. Standard chemotherapy for MPNST typically includes combinations of doxorubicin, ifosfamide, and etoposide, but response rates in NF1-associated MPNST are generally poor (17.9%) compared to sporadic MPNST (44.4%)[14]. Emerging research has identified PLK1 inhibitors and gemcitabine as potentially promising therapeutic options through high-throughput drug screening approaches[11]. Analysis of patient data revealed a correlation between high PLK1 expression and poor prognosis, providing mechanistic support for this therapeutic approach[11]. While investigational agents targeting MPNST are under development, no specific approved pharmacological therapies exist for this indication, underscoring the urgent need for novel treatment strategies.

### Drug Repurposing Approaches and Systematic Screening

Systematic drug repurposing efforts have identified additional candidates worthy of clinical investigation in NF1. A comprehensive drug repurposing screen examining 299 clinical and investigational drugs identified PLK1 inhibitors and gemcitabine as emerging therapeutic options for NF1-associated MPNST[11]. This approach leverages existing FDA-approved drugs and clinical trial candidates that have already undergone safety and dosing studies in humans, thereby reducing the risk, cost, and time associated with traditional drug development[11]. Such systematic screening approaches hold promise for identifying additional therapeutic opportunities in NF1.

## Special Considerations and Future Directions

### Bone and Skeletal Manifestations of NF1

Beyond tumors, NF1 affects multiple organ systems, with skeletal complications being particularly significant. Short stature, scoliosis, kyphosis, sphenoid wing dysplasia, and tibial pseudarthrosis represent serious orthopedic manifestations of NF1[10]. The recent findings that combined MEK and SHP2 inhibition effectively prevents tibial pseudarthrosis in preclinical models represent an important advance in addressing this serious complication[41]. These combination approaches are likely to transition into clinical trials, potentially offering the first pharmacological therapy for NF1-related pseudarthrosis.

### Secondary Malignancies and Long-Term Treatment Implications

An important consideration in the long-term management of NF1 is the elevated risk of secondary malignancies in treated patients, particularly those exposed to genotoxic agents. A systematic analysis demonstrated that NF1-affected children with a primary tumor are at significantly higher risk of developing subsequent neoplasms compared to their non-NF1 counterparts[37]. Among NF1-affected children with primary tumors, those exposed to therapeutic radiation face an approximately 2.8-fold increased risk of subsequent neoplasms[37]. These findings underscore the importance of developing targeted therapies like MEK inhibitors that avoid genotoxicity. While long-term safety data for MEK inhibitors remain limited, the absence of known genotoxic potential represents a significant advantage over traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, particularly for pediatric patients who may receive these agents for extended periods.

### Age-Specific Treatment Considerations and Quality of Life

Management of NF1-associated plexiform neurofibromas presents unique challenges across the lifespan, with disease presentation, treatment response, and comorbidities differing substantially between pediatric and adult populations[17]. Selumetinib is now approved for children as young as one year of age, while mirdametinib is approved starting at age two years, enabling treatment of symptomatic young children with serious plexiform neurofibromas[1][2][3][5]. Long-term follow-up data demonstrate durable tumor shrinkage in children remaining on selumetinib, with a median progression-free survival of approximately seven years (88 cycles), supporting continued long-term treatment[17]. Quality of life improvements observed in both pediatric and adult populations underscore the importance of these therapeutic advances for affected individuals. Notably, study data from NIH trials of selumetinib demonstrated that both children and adults achieved high adherence to their medication regimens, with factors facilitating adherence including increased tumor pain when medication was stopped or temporarily paused[26][26].

## Conclusion

The landscape of pharmacological therapy for neurofibromatosis type 1 has undergone remarkable transformation in recent years, transitioning from limited options focused primarily on surgical management and symptom control to an expanding armamentarium of targeted agents with evidence-based efficacy. The FDA approvals of selumetinib in November 2025 for adults and mirdametinib in February 2025 for patients as young as two years old represent watershed moments in NF1 treatment, providing the first medications demonstrated to produce meaningful tumor shrinkage and associated symptomatic and functional improvements in both pediatric and adult populations with inoperable plexiform neurofibromas[1][3][5][1]. These approvals were supported by rigorous clinical trials—the SPRINT trial for pediatric selumetinib, the KOMET trial for adult selumetinib, and the ReNeu trial for mirdametinib—demonstrating consistent benefits across diverse patient populations[1][1][33][34].

Beyond these landmark approvals, a robust pipeline of investigational agents is advancing through clinical development, offering promise for expanded treatment options, improved efficacy, reduced toxicity, and potential applications to additional NF1-associated complications. Combination therapeutic approaches targeting complementary signaling pathways represent an important emerging strategy that may enhance efficacy while reducing individual drug doses and associated toxicity[16][41]. Investigational agents including FCN-159, topical MEK inhibitors, and mTOR inhibitors are expanding the therapeutic arsenal. Drug repurposing efforts, exemplified by the investigation of lovastatin for cognitive improvements and systematic screening identifying PLK1 inhibitors and gemcitabine for MPNST treatment, demonstrate the value of identifying existing medications with mechanistic relevance to NF1 pathophysiology[11][12].

Comprehensive management of NF1 requires attention not only to tumor-directed therapy but also to supportive care addressing pain, psychiatric comorbidities, and quality of life. The emergence of MEK inhibitors with documented pain-reducing effects represents a major advance, yet psychological and behavioral interventions, complementary therapies, and emerging interventional approaches remain important adjunctive components of comprehensive pain management[26][27][30][26]. Recognition of dopaminergic dysfunction in NF1-related psychiatric conditions has led to improved approaches to managing comorbid depression, anxiety, and ADHD through targeting dopamine signaling[21][21].

The safety profile of MEK inhibitors, while generally manageable, requires vigilant monitoring and comprehensive management strategies to mitigate dermatologic, cardiac, ocular, and gastrointestinal toxicities[1][5][8][15][28][8]. Careful attention to baseline and ongoing monitoring protocols—including cardiac assessments, ophthalmologic examinations, laboratory monitoring, and clinical assessment for adverse effects—is essential to optimizing outcomes while minimizing harm[8][28][8]. The reversibility of many MEK inhibitor-associated adverse effects when drugs are discontinued or doses are reduced provides reassurance that most toxicities can be managed effectively[8].

Looking forward, several key priorities merit emphasis. First, continued research into optimal dosing schedules, treatment duration, and long-term safety of MEK inhibitors is essential, particularly given that pediatric patients may receive these agents for many years. Second, head-to-head comparative trials of selumetinib and mirdametinib would provide valuable evidence to guide clinical decision-making. Third, research into mechanisms of treatment resistance and strategies to overcome limited response in a subset of patients remains critical. Fourth, expansion of therapeutic options to other NF1-associated tumor types and manifestations, including low-grade gliomas, cutaneous neurofibromas, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors, represents an important frontier. Fifth, investigation of combination therapies and rational drug combinations as outlined above shows considerable promise and merits prioritization. Finally, continued attention to quality of life, functional outcomes, and comprehensive supportive care ensures that treatment goals align with patient values and priorities.

In conclusion, the recent FDA approvals of selumetinib and mirdametinib for NF1-associated plexiform neurofibromas represent transformative advances that have fundamentally altered the therapeutic landscape for this serious genetic disorder. These approvals validate decades of research into NF1 pathophysiology, demonstrate the clinical relevance of targeting the RAS-MAPK pathway, and provide proof of concept that precision medicine approaches can yield meaningful benefits in rare genetic diseases. As the pipeline of investigational agents advances, combination strategies are refined, and long-term outcomes data accumulate, it is anticipated that the coming years will yield additional therapeutic options with potential to improve outcomes across the diverse manifestations of neurofibromatosis type 1. Clinicians caring for patients with NF1 should remain informed of these rapid developments, implement appropriate monitoring protocols, and consider referral to specialized NF1 centers of excellence to ensure access to the most current evidence-based therapeutic approaches.
