## Disease Characterization and Pathophysiological Basis

### Clinical Phenotype and Genetic Etiology of Usher Syndrome Type 4

Usher syndrome type 4 is distinguished from other forms of Usher syndrome by its unique pattern of age-related symptom onset and the distinctive distribution of retinal pathology. The condition is characterized by late-onset rod-cone dystrophy (retinitis pigmentosa) with a distinctive retinal phenotype featuring ring-shaped atrophy along the vascular arcades, late-onset progressive sensorineural hearing loss, and notably absent vestibular involvement, which contrasts sharply with Usher syndrome types 1 and 2.[7][7] Patients typically present with visual and auditory impairment that begins in the fourth or later decades of life, making USH4 temporally and phenotypically distinct from classical Usher syndrome presentations that commonly emerge in childhood or adolescence.[7] The retinopathy in USH4 displays predominantly pericentral and macular changes with progressive outer retinal atrophy and intraretinal bone spicule formation, which are characteristic hallmarks of retinitis pigmentosa. The disease progression is remarkably heterogeneous among affected individuals, with some patients experiencing relatively stable disease while others progress more rapidly toward advanced vision loss.

The genetic basis of USH4 involves pathogenic variants in the ARSG gene, which encodes arylsulfatase G, a lysosomal sulfatase enzyme.[6][7][11] This represents a fundamental departure from the genetic architecture of other Usher syndrome types, as ARSG mutations cause disease through a different molecular mechanism than the genes responsible for USH1, USH2, and USH3. Rather than affecting structural proteins involved in the periciliary membrane complex or the stereocilia bundle organization in the inner ear, ARSG mutations result in deficiency or dysfunction of a lysosomal degradative enzyme, suggesting that USH4 may represent a lysosomal storage disorder manifesting with Usher-like phenotypic features.[7][11] Functional studies have demonstrated that pathogenic ARSG variants lead to loss of sulfatase enzymatic activity and impaired lysosomal localization of the mutant protein, with some variants causing complete loss-of-function phenotypes.[11][7][7] The protein accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum rather than reaching its intended lysosomal destination, resulting in a failure to degrade specific substrates that the enzyme normally processes.[11]

### Molecular Pathophysiology and Cellular Consequences

The cellular and molecular mechanisms by which ARSG mutations precipitate the dual sensory loss phenotype of USH4 remain incompletely understood, representing a critical gap in therapeutic knowledge. The ARSG enzyme belongs to the family of lysosomal sulfatases and normally functions to hydrolyze specific sulfated substrates within the lysosomal compartment, participating in the stepwise degradation of glycosaminoglycans and other complex macromolecules essential for cellular homeostasis.[7] When ARSG function is impaired through loss-of-function mutations, these substrates accumulate within lysosomes, leading to lysosomal dysfunction and potentially triggering cellular toxicity through oxidative stress and autophagy dysregulation. The retina and inner ear are both metabolically active tissues with high oxidative demands and substantial lysosomal activity, potentially explaining why these tissues are particularly vulnerable to dysfunction of a lysosomal enzyme. The photoreceptor cells in the retina and sensory hair cells in the cochlea must continuously recycle damaged organelles and degrade accumulated cellular debris, making them dependent on properly functioning lysosomal degradation pathways.[1][1] Disruption of these pathways through ARSG deficiency likely leads to progressive cellular toxicity and eventual death of these sensory cells.

The fact that USH4 exhibits late disease onset in the fourth decade of life or later, in contrast to earlier-onset presentations of other Usher types, suggests that ARSG mutations may cause a more slowly progressive or age-dependent accumulation of pathogenic substrates. This temporal pattern is reminiscent of certain adult-onset lysosomal storage disorders where tissue damage accumulates gradually over decades before clinical manifestation becomes apparent. The absence of vestibular involvement in USH4 is particularly intriguing and suggests either that vestibular sensory tissues are relatively spared from ARSG-related pathology, or that vestibular dysfunction requires a threshold of cellular degeneration that may not be reached given the slower disease progression characteristic of USH4.

### Current Management Approaches for USH4

While no specific pharmacological treatments exist for USH4, clinical management focuses on supportive care and symptom management strategies similar to those employed for other Usher syndrome types and retinitis pigmentosa more broadly.[13][13] Patients with USH4 typically receive comprehensive care addressing their progressive hearing and vision loss through multidisciplinary approaches. #### Gene Therapy Approaches

Gene therapy represents one of the most actively investigated therapeutic modalities for Usher syndrome, with particular focus on gene replacement and gene editing strategies.[1][1][19][1][24] For Usher syndrome type 1B (USH1B), which is caused by mutations in the large MYO7A gene, dual and triple AAV vector approaches are being explored to overcome the packaging capacity limitations of single adeno-associated viral vectors.[1][24] The therapeutic principle underlying gene replacement therapy for Usher syndrome is straightforward: if a particular gene carries a loss-of-function mutation causing protein deficiency, delivering a functional copy of the gene via a viral vector could restore protein expression and ameliorate disease manifestations. However, applying this approach to USH4 would present unique challenges, as ARSG is a lysosomal enzyme with distinctive trafficking and processing requirements. Delivery of an ARSG gene replacement therapy would require not only successful transduction of photoreceptor cells and inner ear hair cells, but also proper post-translational processing, trafficking to lysosomes, and enzymatic activation of the delivered protein—steps that are considerably more complex than for structural proteins like MYO7A.

The first FDA-approved gene therapy for any inherited retinal disease was voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna), an AAV2-based gene therapy for leber congenital amaurosis caused by RPE65 mutations.[1][1] This landmark approval demonstrated that gene therapy could successfully restore retinal function in inherited retinal dystrophies and provided regulatory precedent for approval of other retinal gene therapies. However, the development timeline for voretigene neparvovec spanned many years and required substantial investment, with the regulatory pathway being streamlined through use of breakthrough therapy designation and accelerated approval pathways. For a condition as rare as USH4, achieving similar investment and regulatory support would require compelling preclinical data demonstrating therapeutic efficacy and safety.

#### Antisense Oligonucleotide Approaches

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) represent an alternative modality for modulating gene expression and have been extensively investigated for Usher syndrome, particularly for USH2A-related disease.[1][1][20] ASOs are short, synthetically designed nucleic acid sequences that bind to complementary mRNA sequences and modulate gene expression through multiple mechanisms including recruitment of ribonuclease H for mRNA degradation, or modulation of splicing patterns to correct splicing defects caused by pathogenic mutations.[1] The significant advantage of ASOs compared to gene therapy is that the size of the target gene is not a limiting factor, as ASOs can be designed to target any mRNA sequence regardless of gene size. For USH2A-related retinitis pigmentosa, ProQR Therapeutics developed QR-421a, an ASO designed to skip exon 13 of the USH2A gene and restore production of a truncated but functional usherin protein.[1][20] This therapeutic has advanced into Phase 1/2 clinical trials and has demonstrated preliminary efficacy with some patients showing stabilization of vision loss.[20][26][26]

For USH4, an ASO-based approach targeting ARSG would require different design considerations than those used for structural protein genes. Since ARSG is an enzymatic protein where truncation or modification could impair catalytic activity, successful ASO therapy would likely need to target specific splice-altering mutations without disrupting the enzymatic domains of the protein. Alternatively, if a particular pathogenic ARSG mutation creates a cryptic splice site or causes aberrant splicing, an ASO could potentially correct the splicing defect and restore production of wild-type enzyme, as has been demonstrated for some other genetic diseases.[1] However, such an approach would be applicable only to those USH4 patients carrying specific splice-affecting mutations.

#### CRISPR-Based Gene Editing Approaches

CRISPR/Cas9 and related gene editing technologies have emerged as powerful tools for treating genetic diseases and have been extensively explored for Usher syndrome applications.[19][19][33] Gene editing approaches offer the potential advantage of permanently correcting mutations at the DNA level, without requiring repeated dosing. Three major CRISPR-based modalities are being investigated for Usher syndrome: CRISPR/Cas9 with double-strand break induction and repair via non-homologous end joining, base editing which enables precise nucleotide transitions, and prime editing which can mediate both base changes and small insertions or deletions.[19][19][33]

CRISPR/Cas9 approaches for USH4 could theoretically target specific pathogenic ARSG mutations, with the goal of correcting or ameliorating the genetic defect. However, the challenge of delivering CRISPR/Cas9 components to photoreceptor cells and cochlear hair cells, the potential for off-target editing effects, and the requirement that editing occur at sufficient efficiency to restore adequate ARSG enzyme activity all present substantial technical barriers. Furthermore, if ARSG mutations cause loss-of-function through protein truncation or misfolding, correcting such mutations would require either precise base editing to restore the normal codon, or deletion of the mutant sequence if it affects an exon that can be skipped without preventing enzymatic function.

Base editing could theoretically address ARSG mutations that involve specific nucleotide transitions (C-to-T or A-to-G edits) that can be corrected by cytosine or adenine base editors.[19][33] However, the applicability of base editing is limited to mutations amenable to these specific editing patterns. Prime editing, which offers the broadest therapeutic potential by enabling all base transitions and small insertions or deletions, might be more broadly applicable to ARSG mutations.[19] However, as with all CRISPR-based approaches, the challenge of delivering prime editing components into retinal and cochlear tissues remains substantial.

A major technical barrier to CRISPR-based approaches for Usher syndrome is the requirement that editing machinery fit within the packaging constraints of viral delivery vectors. The complete CRISPR/Cas9 system is too large for AAV delivery in a single vector, necessitating dual or multiple vector approaches that substantially complicate therapeutic development. For USH4 specifically, the development of miniaturized CRISPR components, compact base editors, or optimized prime editors that can fit within single AAV vectors would be prerequisites for clinical translation of these approaches.

#### Substrate Reduction Therapy and Enzyme Replacement Therapy

Given that ARSG is a lysosomal sulfatase enzyme, therapeutic approaches developed for other lysosomal storage disorders might provide conceptual precedent for USH4 treatment strategies. Substrate reduction therapy (SRT) and enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) represent two established therapeutic modalities for various lysosomal storage diseases.[23][25][29] Substrate reduction therapy works by partially inhibiting the synthesis of macromolecules that accumulate in lysosomes of patients with enzymatic deficiencies, allowing residual enzyme activity to maintain cellular balance.[23][29] The principle is that if substrate production is reduced, the rate of substrate accumulation may slow or stabilize at levels below that which causes cellular toxicity.

For USH4, a substrate reduction therapy approach would require identifying and synthesizing inhibitors of the biological pathways that produce substrates normally degraded by ARSG. If the sulfated compounds that accumulate in ARSG-deficient cells were identified and characterized, it might be possible to develop small-molecule inhibitors of the synthetic enzymes responsible for their production. Such inhibitors could potentially slow the accumulation of pathogenic substrates in photoreceptor cells and cochlear hair cells, potentially slowing disease progression. However, no such inhibitors have been described for ARSG substrates, and development of such therapeutics would require substantial research investment to characterize the biology of ARSG substrates and identify suitable drug targets.

Enzyme replacement therapy, which has been successfully applied to multiple lysosomal storage disorders including Gaucher disease, fabry disease, mucopolysaccharidosis types I and II, and others, involves intravenous infusion of recombinant enzymes that are taken up by cells and trafficked to lysosomes where they catabolize accumulated substrates.[23][25] For USH4, an ERT approach would involve developing recombinant ARSG enzyme and delivering it systemically or via targeted injection directly into the eye and inner ear. The advantage of ERT is that the recombinant enzyme can be readily manufactured and purified, avoiding some of the complexity of genetic approaches. However, ERT approaches have significant limitations, including the requirement for lifelong repeated infusions, variable cellular uptake of infused enzymes, and particular challenges in delivering enzymes across the blood-brain barrier and blood-retinal barriers to reach affected photoreceptor cells and cochlear hair cells. Despite these limitations, ERT might represent a viable therapeutic strategy for USH4 if preclinical studies demonstrated that ARSG enzyme replacement could prevent or slow retinal and cochlear degeneration.

## Drug Repurposing Candidates and Off-Label Therapeutic Approaches

### Antioxidant and Neuroprotective Strategies

Given that oxidative stress is implicated in the pathogenesis of retinitis pigmentosa and other retinal dystrophies, antioxidant compounds represent potential therapeutic candidates that might be repurposed for USH4.[16][22][7][26] N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and its amidated variant N-acetylcysteine-amide (NACA) have emerged as promising investigational agents for retinitis pigmentosa, including Usher syndrome-associated forms.[26][26] Nacuity reported in February 2026 that NACA achieved a 50 percent reduction in photoreceptor loss in a Phase 2 clinical trial in patients with Usher syndrome, representing a substantial efficacy signal.[26][26] NACA is designed to protect retinal cells from oxidative stress, and preclinical studies at Johns Hopkins University demonstrated that NACA slowed retinal degeneration in rodent models of retinitis pigmentosa.[26] The mechanism underlying NACA's protective effect likely involves restoration of intracellular glutathione pools and enhancement of cellular antioxidant capacity, compensating for oxidative stress that drives photoreceptor degeneration.

For USH4, antioxidant approaches might be particularly relevant if oxidative stress represents a key mechanism by which ARSG deficiency leads to photoreceptor and hair cell death. While the relationship between ARSG enzymatic deficiency and oxidative stress has not been formally established, the progressive neurodegeneration characteristic of lysosomal storage disorders is often accompanied by secondary oxidative stress due to impaired cellular metabolism and protein quality control.[41] If oxidative stress contributes to the cellular pathology of USH4, then antioxidant compounds like NACA might provide therapeutic benefit. Johns Hopkins University and the National Eye Institute are conducting a Phase 3 clinical trial (NAC Attack) that has enrolled 438 patients with retinitis pigmentosa at 30 sites across the US, Canada, Mexico, and Europe, evaluating whether oral NAC can slow vision loss in RP patients.[26] Results from this trial could inform the potential utility of NAC or NACA in other forms of progressive retinitis pigmentosa, including USH4 if patients were enrolled in future investigations.

### Macular Edema Management Agents

Cystoid macular edema represents a complication that occurs in a substantial proportion of Usher syndrome patients and affects quality of life through central vision loss even as peripheral vision declines from retinitis pigmentosa.[32][13] Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide and dorzolamide have become standard first-line therapy for retinitis pigmentosa-associated macular edema, based on clinical evidence demonstrating stabilization or improvement in macular thickness and visual acuity in some patients.[32][13] For USH4 patients developing macular edema, topical or systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor therapy represents a reasonable off-label therapeutic approach targeting this specific complication, even though such treatment does not address underlying retinal degeneration.

### Vitamin A and Nutritional Approaches

Historically, high-dose vitamin A supplementation was pursued as a potential disease-modifying therapy for retinitis pigmentosa, based on early reports by Eliot Berson suggesting modest slowing of vision loss with vitamin A supplementation.[16] However, recent reanalysis of data from the original vitamin A clinical trial by a research team at Mass Eye and Ear led by Eric Pierce, published in 2023 in JCI Insight, demonstrated that vitamin A supplementation does not provide overall benefit in slowing vision loss in retinitis pigmentosa.[16] Furthermore, the analysis confirmed that vitamin E supplementation actually accelerates vision loss and should be avoided by patients with retinitis pigmentosa.[16] These findings have substantially diminished enthusiasm for vitamin A supplementation as a therapeutic strategy for retinitis pigmentosa, though some clinicians continue to recommend supplementation for certain patient populations. For USH4 patients, current evidence does not support vitamin A supplementation as an effective disease-modifying therapy, and the potential for vitamin E to accelerate vision loss argues against recommending multivitamin formulations containing vitamin E.

## Contraindications and Adverse Drug Reactions Relevant to Usher Syndrome Type 4

### Aminoglycosides and Ototoxic Drugs

Certain therapeutic drugs are known to cause or exacerbate hearing loss and represent absolute contraindications in patients with Usher syndrome, including USH4. Aminoglycoside antibiotics, including gentamicin, streptomycin, neomycin, tobramycin, and amikacin, are widely used against gram-negative bacterial infections but carry significant ototoxic risk.[31] These drugs can cause permanent, irreversible cochlear damage and vestibular toxicity through multiple mechanisms, including generation of reactive oxygen species, overstimulation of NMDA receptors, and direct damage to hair cell mitochondria.[31] In patients with existing hearing loss such as those with USH4, aminoglycoside exposure represents an unacceptable additional risk of accelerated or catastrophic further hearing deterioration. Genetic factors influence aminoglycoside ototoxicity susceptibility, with certain mitochondrial DNA mutations (particularly A1555G and C1494T in the 12S rRNA gene) conferring dramatically increased susceptibility to aminoglycoside-induced hearing loss.[31] While genetic testing for these susceptibility variants is not universally performed, aminoglycosides should be avoided or used only in life-threatening situations with appropriate informed consent in all patients with pre-existing hearing loss, including those with USH4.

### Vitamin E Supplementation

As discussed previously, vitamin E supplementation has been demonstrated to accelerate vision loss in retinitis pigmentosa and should be actively avoided by patients with USH4-associated retinal dystrophy.[16] This finding is particularly important given that many multivitamin formulations and supplement products contain vitamin E, and patients may unknowingly expose themselves to this contraindicated agent. Patients with USH4 should be counseled to check supplement labels and avoid products containing vitamin E, and ophthalmologists should specifically inquire about vitamin E intake during patient visits.

### Potentially Ototoxic Medications

Beyond aminoglycosides, numerous other medications are known to carry ototoxic risk and should be used cautiously or avoided in patients with pre-existing hearing loss including USH4. These include loop diuretics, NSAIDs, certain chemotherapy agents, and some immunosuppressive medications. While many of these medications may be necessary for treatment of serious conditions, careful risk-benefit analysis should be performed, and if these agents must be used, the lowest effective doses should be employed and patients should be monitored for worsening of hearing loss.

## Management of Retinal and Cochlear Manifestations in Usher Syndrome Type 4

### Cochlear Implantation Outcomes in Usher Syndrome

Cochlear implantation has emerged as the standard of care for profound hearing loss in many patients with Usher syndrome, including those with USH4.[13][15][13] Studies of cochlear implantation in Usher syndrome patients have demonstrated significant improvements in auditory thresholds, speech recognition, and quality of life measures.[15] The timing of cochlear implantation appears to influence outcomes, with earlier implantation generally yielding better long-term speech recognition outcomes, particularly when implantation is performed before the development of significant visual impairment that might limit the patient's ability to utilize auditory and lip-reading cues for communication.[15][13] For USH4 specifically, the relatively late age of disease onset compared to other Usher syndrome types means that many patients may already have developed normal hearing and speech understanding by the time hearing loss becomes manifest. This temporal pattern may make cochlear implantation decisions more straightforward in USH4, as patients would already have established language skills and auditory experience before disease onset, potentially leading to better outcomes compared to congenital hearing loss scenarios.

Research has demonstrated that bilateral cochlear implantation tends to produce superior outcomes compared to unilateral implantation, with benefits including improved sound localization and speech recognition in noisy environments through binaural squelch effects.[15][13] For Usher syndrome patients with progressive hearing loss, bilateral implantation should be strongly considered if feasible, as this strategy provides redundancy and maximizes residual function as hearing progressively deteriorates.

### Low Vision Rehabilitation and Vision Aids

While no disease-modifying therapies currently exist for the retinal degeneration component of USH4, low vision rehabilitation services and specialized visual aids can substantially improve functional vision and quality of life in patients with progressive vision loss from retinitis pigmentosa.[14][39][13] Low vision services provided by trained optometrists include training in the use of optical magnification devices, electronic magnification systems, specialized lighting, and techniques to enhance contrast and utilize remaining central vision more effectively.[14][39][13] Orientation and mobility training teaches patients techniques for safe navigation despite progressive visual field loss and can substantially reduce fall risk and improve independence.[14][13] For USH4 patients, early referral to low vision services should be considered when visual field constriction or night blindness becomes clinically apparent, as learning to use these aids while patients retain reasonable central vision facilitates better adaptation than waiting until advanced vision loss has occurred.

### Vestibular Management

Unlike Usher syndrome types 1 and 3, USH4 is characterized by the absence of significant vestibular dysfunction.[7][7] This is a distinctive and favorable feature of this disease subtype, as it means that USH4 patients do not face the added complexity of balance disorders that compound the challenges of progressive hearing and vision loss in other Usher syndrome types. However, as patients develop increasingly severe night blindness and visual field constriction from progressive retinitis pigmentosa, the combination of severe vision loss with the vestibular dysfunction that would normally be managed separately actually does create secondary balance challenges. Even though the inner ear vestibular apparatus functions normally in USH4, severe vision loss alone predisposes to falls and injuries, and vestibular rehabilitation may still provide benefit through compensatory training mechanisms.

## Emerging Therapeutic Strategies and Future Directions

### Retinal Prostheses and Implantable Vision Devices

Retinal prostheses, also known as bionic eyes, represent an innovative therapeutic approach for patients with inherited retinal dystrophies including retinitis pigmentosa and Usher syndrome, offering potential for partial vision restoration even in advanced stages of disease when photoreceptor loss is nearly complete.[34][38] These devices work by bypassing degenerated photoreceptors and directly stimulating remaining functional retinal neurons to create visual sensations corresponding to the stimulation pattern. Several types of retinal prostheses have been developed, including epiretinal implants that stimulate the inner retinal surface, subretinal implants placed beneath the outer retina, and suprachoroidal implants placed in the suprachoroidal space.[34][38] The Iris II epiretinal prosthesis system (Pixium Vision) received CE Mark approval in the European Union for patients with vision loss from retinitis pigmentosa, Usher syndrome, and cone-rod dystrophy.[34] This device consists of an implantable microelectrode array and external components including glasses with integrated image sensors and a wearable processor that translates visual information into stimulation commands sent wirelessly to the implant.[34]

While retinal prostheses do not restore vision to normal levels and provide only limited visual perception, studies have demonstrated that implanted patients regain some light perception, improved mobility and navigation capability, and enhanced quality of life.[34][38] For USH4 patients with advanced retinal degeneration who have exhausted other therapeutic options, retinal prosthesis implantation might represent a viable option to partially mitigate vision loss in the long term, though the technology requires continued refinement to improve visual acuity and patient acceptance. The Iris II system is currently undergoing multicenter clinical trials in patients with retinitis pigmentosa and related conditions, with research ongoing to optimize electrode stimulation patterns and improve visual outcomes.

### Regenerative Medicine and Retinal Cell Replacement

An innovative emerging approach to treating inherited retinal dystrophies involves regenerative medicine strategies using laboratory-grown retinal tissue to replace damaged photoreceptors and restore visual function. Sumitomo Pharma announced in March 2026 that its investigational cell therapy DSP-3077 received FDA Orphan Drug Designation for the treatment of retinitis pigmentosa.[21] DSP-3077 involves delivering retinal sheets derived from three-dimensional retina-like structures grown in the laboratory into patients' eyes, representing a novel form of regenerative medicine using laboratory-grown retinal tissue to potentially replace damaged photoreceptors.[21] This technology represents an early-stage but conceptually promising approach that might eventually be applicable to USH4-associated retinitis pigmentosa if preclinical studies demonstrate safety and efficacy. The advantage of cell-based approaches is that they could provide replacement of the diverse retinal cell types lost in progressive retinal dystrophies, potentially offering more comprehensive vision restoration than gene therapy or prosthetic approaches targeting a single genetic defect.

### Combination Therapy Strategies

Most future therapeutic approaches for Usher syndrome, including USH4, will likely involve combinations of therapies addressing different pathogenic mechanisms or targeting sequential stages of disease progression.[26][26] For instance, antioxidant therapy with NACA might be combined with gene therapy or gene editing approaches to simultaneously protect photoreceptors from oxidative damage while correcting the underlying genetic defect. Similarly, early intervention with neuroprotective approaches might slow disease progression sufficiently to extend the window of time during which cell replacement therapies could be effective. The potential synergy between therapies addressing different mechanisms of cell death suggests that combination approaches will likely outperform monotherapy in achieving optimal disease modification.

## Comparative Therapeutic Landscape: USH4 Within the Broader Usher Syndrome Context

### Contrast With USH2A-Focused Therapeutic Development

The therapeutic development landscape for Usher syndrome has been heavily focused on type 2 disease, particularly USH2A-associated disease, with multiple investigational agents currently in clinical trials.[1][3][5][3][20][26][26] ProQR Therapeutics has advanced two antisense oligonucleotide candidates targeting different USH2A mutations into clinical trials: QR-421a targeting exon 13 mutations advanced to Phase 1/2 trials, and QRX-411 (formerly QR-411) targeting the PE-40 pseudo-exon mutation also advanced to clinical development.[1][3][5][3][20][26] Both of these agents received Orphan Drug Designation from FDA and EMA, providing regulatory advantages including extended market exclusivity and reduced regulatory fees.[3][5][3] A Phase 2b clinical trial (LUNA) for ultevursen (formerly QR-421a, now developed by Sepul Bio after acquisition by Théa) and a Phase 3 trial (HYPERION) for sepofarsen (for LCA10) represent the current frontline of clinical development in Usher syndrome therapeutics as of 2026.[26][26]

In contrast to the multiple agents under investigation for USH2A disease, not a single clinical trial specifically investigating therapies for USH4 had been initiated as of April 2026, reflecting the dramatic disparity in therapeutic development effort between common and ultra-rare disease subtypes. This disparity is economically rational from a pharmaceutical company perspective but nevertheless highlights a significant unmet medical need for this ultra-rare patient population.

### Gene Therapy Development for USH1B

Efforts to develop gene therapy for Usher syndrome type 1B (USH1B) caused by MYO7A mutations have progressed substantially, with AAVantgarde Bio's dual-vector gene therapy (LUCE-1) completing enrollment in Phase 1/2 clinical trials as of 2026.[26][26] This program represents the most advanced gene therapy development effort specifically for Usher syndrome, reflecting the severity and clinical importance of USH1B. The LUCE-1 trial enrolled 15 patients ranging from 18 to 50 years of age, with initial safety and preliminary efficacy signals reported in the first treated patients.[26][26] The company has announced plans to expand to US trial sites if the therapy advances into pivotal Phase 3 trials. The successful progression of USH1B gene therapy through clinical trials would represent a major therapeutic milestone for Usher syndrome and could provide important regulatory and clinical precedent for subsequent development of therapies for other Usher syndrome types, potentially including USH4 if technological advances enable successful therapeutic approaches.

## Current Management and Treatment of Usher Syndrome Type 4

### Multidisciplinary Care Approach

As no curative or disease-modifying therapies currently exist for USH4, management must focus on comprehensive symptomatic treatment and rehabilitation addressing the specific hearing and vision manifestations that affect individual patients. A multidisciplinary team approach involving otolaryngologists, audiologists, ophthalmologists, low vision specialists, genetic counselors, and rehabilitation specialists optimizes outcomes and supports patients in adjusting to progressive sensory loss. Early diagnosis and identification of USH4 in patients is particularly important, as timely initiation of supportive services and rehabilitation planning can substantially improve long-term outcomes and quality of life.[13][13] Educational programs tailored to each patient's specific pattern of hearing and vision loss, age, and abilities help prepare individuals for employment and independent living as disease progresses.

### Surveillance and Monitoring

Given the progressive nature of USH4, regular clinical monitoring with periodic reassessment of hearing and vision function is essential to track disease progression, identify complications, and adjust therapeutic interventions as needed. Annual or biennial audiologic testing with pure-tone and speech audiometry establishes the rate of hearing loss progression and informs decisions regarding hearing aid amplification settings or candidacy for cochlear implantation. Annual or biennial ophthalmologic evaluation including dilated retinal examination, visual field testing by automated perimetry, and optical coherence tomography imaging when available allows documentation of retinal changes, detection of macular edema or other complications, and assessment of visual acuity trends. Vestibular function testing, while typically normal in USH4, might be periodically reassessed to confirm the absence of involvement and to establish baseline function for comparative purposes.

## Conclusion: Current State and Future Prospects for USH4 Therapeutics

As of April 2026, Usher syndrome type 4 represents a therapeutic orphan disease, with no FDA-approved, EMA-approved, or other regulatory agency-approved drug treatments available for any manifestation of this ultra-rare condition. No clinical trials specifically investigating drug therapies for USH4 have been initiated, reflecting the extreme rarity of the disease and the limited understanding of underlying pathophysiology. The only individuals described in the peer-reviewed medical literature as having USH4 number fewer than 25, representing a population far too small to support traditional pharmaceutical development models. Current management of USH4 focuses entirely on symptomatic treatment and rehabilitation, including hearing aids or cochlear implants for auditory manifestations, low-vision aids and rehabilitation services for visual manifestations, and comprehensive multidisciplinary coordinated care to optimize patient function and quality of life.

Despite the absence of specific USH4 therapies, important developments in the broader field of Usher syndrome therapeutics and rare genetic disease treatment offer grounds for cautious optimism regarding future therapeutic possibilities. The emergence of antisense oligonucleotide therapies for USH2A disease, advancement of dual-vector gene therapies for USH1B, progress in CRISPR-based genome editing technologies, and preclinical validation of antioxidant and neuroprotective approaches all provide conceptual and methodological frameworks that might eventually be adapted for USH4. Natural history studies such as the Foundation Fighting Blindness' RUSH2A initiative and ongoing efforts to characterize the clinical and molecular features of Usher syndrome across diverse patient populations contribute important foundational knowledge that could inform future USH4 research.

The path toward therapeutic development for USH4 will require convergence of multiple factors: further scientific understanding of ARSG function and disease mechanisms, development of appropriate preclinical disease models to enable drug screening, increasing recognition and diagnosis of USH4 in the medical community leading to identification of more patients, and ultimately demonstration of therapeutic proof-of-concept in preclinical systems or small patient cohorts compelling enough to justify pharmaceutical or biotechnology company investment in formal clinical development programs. International collaboration among researchers, patient advocacy organizations, and regulatory agencies will be essential to overcome the barriers posed by extreme disease rarity. Until such advances are achieved, patients with USH4 and their healthcare providers must work within the currently available symptom management and rehabilitation frameworks while remaining engaged with the research community to facilitate future therapeutic developments that might ultimately alter the disease trajectory for this devastating disorder. The story of USH4 therapeutics remains one of potential yet unrealized, with the promise of future treatments dependent on sustained research commitment to understanding this ultra-rare disease and creative approaches to translating that understanding into clinical interventions.
