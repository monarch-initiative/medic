# Comprehensive Analysis of Drug Therapies for Phenylketonuria: Approved, Investigational, and Emerging Treatment Approaches

Phenylketonuria (PKU) represents one of the most significant advances in inherited metabolic disease management over the past several decades, evolving from a universally devastating condition to one amenable to multiple therapeutic interventions. This comprehensive research report examines the current and emerging pharmacological landscape for PKU management, encompassing FDA-approved medications, European Union-approved therapies, investigational compounds in clinical development, combination treatment strategies, and relevant safety considerations for healthcare providers and patients. The therapeutic options available today represent a paradigm shift from dietary restriction alone to a multifaceted approach incorporating enzyme replacement, cofactor optimization, metabolic pathway modulation, and potentially curative gene therapy approaches. This report synthesizes evidence from clinical trials, regulatory documents, and published literature to provide an integrated understanding of PKU pharmacotherapy as of April 2026.

## Approved Pharmacological Therapies for Phenylketonuria

### Sepiapterin (Sephience™) as a Natural BH4 Precursor

Sepiapterin represents the most recent major advancement in PKU therapeutics, having received FDA approval on July 28, 2025, and European Commission marketing authorization on June 23, 2025[1][35]. This medication addresses a critical gap in the therapeutic arsenal by offering efficacy across the entire spectrum of PKU disease severity, including patients with non-BH4-responsive genotypes who previously had limited pharmacological options beyond dietary restriction. Sephience is indicated for the treatment of hyperphenylalaninemia (HPA) in adult and pediatric patients one month of age and older with sepiapterin-responsive PKU[1]. The mechanism of action distinguishes sepiapterin from sapropterin through its function as a dual pharmacological chaperone that works both as a precursor to the enzymatic cofactor BH4 and through direct binding to variant PAH enzymes[35].

The clinical efficacy of sepiapterin was established through the Phase 3 APHENITY trial, which demonstrated substantial superiority over existing therapies[21][46]. In the APHENITY trial, 156 participants were assessed in Part 1, with 114 participants (73%) demonstrating sepiapterin responsiveness defined as at least 15 percent reduction in blood phenylalanine from baseline[46]. Among those with at least 30 percent reduction in Part 1, the primary analysis set included 98 participants who proceeded to Part 2, wherein participants received six weeks of forced-dose escalation to a maximum of 60 milligrams per kilogram daily or placebo[46]. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in blood phenylalanine concentration after six weeks of sepiapterin treatment of 63 percent (standard deviation 20) compared with placebo showing only 1 percent change (standard deviation 29), with a least squares mean difference of negative 395.9 micromoles per liter (standard error 33.8; p<0.0001)[46]. Critically, the AMPLIPHY trial, published in 2026, provided direct head-to-head comparison between sepiapterin and sapropterin, demonstrating that sepiapterin achieved a 70 percent greater reduction in blood phenylalanine compared with the maximum licensed dose of sapropterin[21]. The least-squares mean reduction in blood phenylalanine from baseline was negative 437.0 micromoles per liter (standard error 28.0) with sepiapterin versus negative 256.6 micromoles per liter (standard error 28.2) with sapropterin, representing a least-squares mean difference of negative 180.4 micromoles per liter (95 percent confidence interval: negative 229.5, negative 131.4; p<0.0001)[21].

The safety profile of sepiapterin proved favorable across the APHENITY trial population[46]. Treatment-emergent adverse events were reported in 33 (59 percent) of 56 participants who received sepiapterin and 18 (33 percent) of 54 participants who received placebo[46]. Most treatment-emergent adverse events were mild gastrointestinal events, with 11 (20 percent) of 56 participants receiving sepiapterin and 10 (19 percent) of 54 participants receiving placebo experiencing such events, which resolved quickly[46]. There were no deaths and no serious or severe adverse events reported[46]. The product prescribing information indicates that the most common adverse reactions observed with sepiapterin at greater than or equal to 2 percent and greater than placebo were diarrhea, headache, abdominal pain, hypophenylalaninemia, feces discoloration, and oropharyngeal pain[1]. Importantly, sepiapterin carries no formal contraindications, though careful monitoring of blood phenylalanine is necessary to avoid hypophenylalaninemia[1].

A significant consideration for sepiapterin prescribing involves potential drug interactions. The prescribing information specifically advises avoiding concomitant use of drugs known to inhibit folate synthesis via dihydrofolate reductase inhibition, including trimethoprim, methotrexate, trimetrexate, pemetrexed, pralatrexate, raltitrexed, and piritrexim, as such concurrent administration may reduce sepiapterin metabolism to BH4[1]. If such concomitant use is unavoidable, blood phenylalanine levels should be monitored carefully[1]. The labeling expansion in the European Union included a broad indication for all ages and disease severities, with the authorization applicable to all 27 European Union member states as well as Iceland, Norway, and Liechtenstein[35].

### Sapropterin Dihydrochloride (Kuvan®) as Established BH4 Cofactor Therapy

Sapropterin dihydrochloride, marketed as Kuvan and subsequently available in generic formulations and under alternative brand names such as Javygtor, has served as the standard pharmacological adjunct to dietary therapy since FDA approval in 2007[2][4][27]. This synthetic formulation of tetrahydrobiopterin functions as a cofactor for phenylalanine hydroxylase, working by stabilizing and enhancing the activity of residual PAH enzyme in individuals with responsive mutations[2][27]. The mechanism operates through provision of exogenous BH4, which acts as an essential cofactor necessary for the PAH enzyme's ability to convert phenylalanine to tyrosine, thereby reducing blood phenylalanine levels in responsive individuals[27].

The therapeutic utility of sapropterin is constrained by genotype-dependent responsiveness, with approximately one-third of all patients with PKU demonstrating clinical response to this therapy[32]. Cochrane systematic review evidence indicates short-term benefit from sapropterin in responsive individuals, with two placebo-controlled trials demonstrating significant reductions in blood phenylalanine concentration and improved protein tolerance[2]. One trial administered 10 milligrams per kilogram daily sapropterin in 89 children and adults with PKU whose diets were not restricted and who had previously responded to sapropterin, measuring change in blood phenylalanine concentration[2]. The second trial screened 90 children aged 4 to 12 years with PKU whose diet was restricted for responsiveness to sapropterin, with 46 responders entering the placebo-controlled portion receiving 20 milligrams per kilogram daily sapropterin[2]. Both trials reported adverse events with overall low risk of bias[2].

The specific efficacy outcomes from these trials demonstrated a mean difference in blood phenylalanine concentration between sapropterin and control groups at three weeks of negative 135.20 micromoles per liter (95 percent confidence interval negative 187.92 to negative 82.48)[2]. Significantly, sapropterin showed no serious adverse events associated with short-term use[2]. Long-term extension studies have provided additional evidence for sustained benefit; the SPARK extension trial followed 51 pediatric patients for 36 additional months beyond the initial 26-week study period[16]. Patients in the "sapropterin continuous" group, who had received sapropterin plus phenylalanine-restricted diet throughout, demonstrated dietary phenylalanine tolerance increase of 38.7 milligrams per kilogram daily (95 percent confidence interval 28.9, 48.6; p<0.0001) versus baseline[16]. The "sapropterin extension" group, who initiated sapropterin after dietary-only treatment, showed less pronounced effect with dietary phenylalanine tolerance increase of 5.5 milligrams per kilogram daily versus baseline (95 percent confidence interval negative 2.8, 13.8; p=0.1929), though this difference only reached statistical significance between months 9 and 21[16]. Patients in both groups maintained normal neuromotor development and growth parameters[16].

### Pegvaliase-pqpz (Palynziq®) as Enzyme Substitution Therapy

Pegvaliase-pqpz, marketed as Palynziq, represents a fundamentally distinct therapeutic approach compared to cofactor optimization strategies[5][8][28]. This medication functions as an enzyme substitution therapy utilizing a pegylated recombinant phenylalanine ammonia lyase derived from Anabaena variabilis, which catalyzes the direct degradation of phenylalanine to ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid, thereby bypassing the deficient PAH enzyme entirely[5][8][28][30]. This genotype-independent mechanism permits efficacy in patients regardless of residual PAH activity or BH4 responsiveness status[8]. Pegvaliase was FDA-approved on May 24, 2018, for reduction of blood phenylalanine concentrations in adult patients with PKU who have uncontrolled blood phenylalanine concentrations greater than 600 micromoles per liter on existing management[28][30].

The clinical development program comprised two pivotal Phase 3 randomized controlled trials designated PRISM-1 and PRISM-2, followed by an ongoing open-label extension study[30]. In PRISM-1, pegvaliase-naïve participants with blood phenylalanine greater than 600 micromoles per liter were randomized 1:1 to a maintenance dose of 20 milligrams daily or 40 milligrams daily of pegvaliase[30]. Participants in PRISM-1 continued pegvaliase treatment in PRISM-2, a multi-part clinical trial that included an ongoing open-label long-term extension study evaluating pegvaliase doses ranging from 5 milligrams daily to 60 milligrams daily[30]. Of 261 participants receiving pegvaliase treatment, 72.0 percent and 32.6 percent reached at least 12 months and at least 24 months of study treatment respectively, with 65 percent still actively receiving treatment[30]. Mean blood phenylalanine was 1232.7 micromoles per liter (standard deviation 386.4) at baseline, 564.5 micromoles per liter (standard deviation 531.2) at 12 months, and 311.4 micromoles per liter (standard deviation 427) at 24 months, representing a decrease from baseline of 51.1 percent and 68.7 percent respectively[30]. Within 24 months, 68.4 percent of participants achieved blood phenylalanine ≤600 micromoles per liter, 60.7 percent achieved blood phenylalanine ≤360 micromoles per liter, and 51.2 percent achieved blood phenylalanine ≤120 micromoles per liter[30].

The prescribing information for pegvaliase was expanded in October 2020 to increase the maximum allowable dose from 40 milligrams to 60 milligrams daily, based on longer-term efficacy data demonstrating sustained phenylalanine lowering out to three years and over six years of safety data[5]. Of the 77 participants treated for at least three years, 58 (75 percent), 51 (66 percent), and 37 (48 percent) had blood phenylalanine concentration less than or equal to 600, 360, and 120 micromoles per liter respectively at three years of treatment[5]. More recently, in October 2025, the FDA agreed to review BioMarin Pharmaceutical's supplemental biologics license application seeking approval to extend pegvaliase use to adolescents aged 12 to 17 years with PKU[31], expanding the previously adult-only indication.

The safety profile of pegvaliase, however, represents a significant clinical consideration requiring careful patient selection and monitoring. A comprehensive analysis of data from the PRISM-1 and PRISM-2 trials and the extension study 165-304 involving 261 adult participants with mean 36.6 months of pegvaliase treatment documented the incidence and characteristics of adverse events[22]. In the overall population, 71.3 percent (n=186), 65.1 percent (n=170), and 59.4 percent (n=155) achieved clinically significant blood phenylalanine reduction to ≤600, ≤360, and ≤120 micromoles per liter respectively[22]. However, the most common adverse events included arthralgia, injection site reactions, headache, and injection site erythema[22]. Importantly, acute systemic hypersensitivity reactions occurred in 7 (2.7 percent) of 261 participants early in treatment[22]. Severe acute systemic hypersensitivity reactions were documented in 3 (1.1 percent) of participants, and anaphylaxis per FDA criteria occurred in 14 (5.4 percent) of 261 participants overall[22]. Critically, the incidence of most adverse events, including hypersensitivity adverse events, was higher during the early treatment phase (≤6 months) than later during treatment[22], with safety profiles improving from early to sustained treatment phases[22].

The immunogenicity of pegvaliase significantly contributes to its adverse event profile and clinical pharmacodynamics. All patients treated with pegvaliase developed anti-drug antibodies against phenylalanine ammonia lyase, peaking by 6 months and then stabilizing[38]. Most patients developed transient antibody responses against the polyethylene glycol component, peaking by 3 months then returning to baseline by 9 months[38]. Binding of these antibodies to pegvaliase led to circulating immune complex formation and complement activation, which were highest during early treatment[38]. Blood phenylalanine decreased over time as circulating immune complex levels and complement activation declined and pegvaliase dosage increased[38]. The magnitude of the neutralizing antibody immune response was inversely associated with the degree of blood phenylalanine reduction, with patients in the lowest antibody titer quartile experiencing the largest reductions in blood phenylalanine during early treatment[38]. However, following stabilization of antibody titers and dose adjustment, mean blood phenylalanine concentrations continued to decrease even in patients within higher antibody titer quartiles[38].

## Investigational Drug Candidates in Clinical Development

### Repinatrabit (JNT-517): Novel Phenylalanine Transporter Inhibitor

Repinatrabit (JNT-517), developed by Jnana Therapeutics in collaboration with Otsuka Pharmaceutical, represents an innovative investigational approach targeting the kidney reabsorption of phenylalanine through selective inhibition of the solute carrier family 6 member 19 transporter[11][23][11]. This mechanism represents a conceptually novel strategy distinct from existing therapeutic approaches, as it blocks the kidney's ability to reabsorb filtered phenylalanine, thereby increasing urinary phenylalanine excretion and reducing blood phenylalanine levels[11]. Repinatrabit acts at a cryptic allosteric site on the SLC6A19 transporter to achieve this effect[11][11].

Repinatrabit received orphan drug designation and rare pediatric disease designation from the U.S. FDA for the treatment of PKU[11][11]. Interim analysis data from the Phase 1b/2 study demonstrated robust reduction in blood phenylalanine levels within one week of dosing, regardless of disease severity or non-responsiveness to existing treatments[11][11]. The 150-milligram twice-daily dose of repinatrabit led to a statistically significant (p=<0.0002 versus placebo) mean blood phenylalanine reduction from baseline of 60 percent on average across days 14, 21, and 28, and a 58 percent reduction from baseline to day 28[11]. Critically, no serious adverse events were observed in any dosing group, with no clinically significant changes in laboratory parameters and no clinically significant changes in plasma amino acids other than phenylalanine observed, confirming safety and tolerability[11].

In December 2025, Otsuka announced the initiation of the global Phase 3 PheORD clinical trial evaluating repinatrabit, a randomized study assessing the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of oral repinatrabit administered twice daily in participants with PKU[11][11]. The Phase 3 trial design includes approximately 120 patients randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of two repinatrabit dose groups (75 milligrams or 150 milligrams twice daily) or placebo for six weeks in the first treatment period, with the primary endpoint assessing short-term mean reduction in blood phenylalanine levels at weeks 2, 4, and 6[23]. During the secondary study period, participants originally assigned to repinatrabit will continue on their respective dose for an additional 46 weeks, while those originally assigned to placebo will transition to repinatrabit 150 milligrams twice daily, with continued safety and efficacy follow-up through 52 weeks[23].

Most recently, in March 2026, Otsuka unveiled new open-label extension study data for repinatrabit demonstrating early and clinically meaningful reductions in blood phenylalanine levels[23]. Adolescents receiving repinatrabit 75 milligrams twice daily achieved sustained phenylalanine reductions at the first evaluable timepoint in the open-label extension (Day 56 / open-label extension Month 1), with an average reduction of 67 percent from baseline[23]. All participants in the cohort demonstrated clinical response, including adolescents with prior sapropterin experience (both responders and non-responders) as well as one sapropterin-naïve participant[23]. Repinatrabit was well tolerated, with a safety profile consistent with prior adult Phase 2 findings, with no new treatment-related adverse events identified and no serious adverse events reported[23]. The scale and consistency of phenylalanine reduction observed in adolescents were comparable to those reported in adult Phase 2 studies, reinforcing the strong potential of repinatrabit across diverse age groups[23].

### NGGT002: Adeno-Associated Virus Gene Therapy

NGGT002, developed by Next Generation Gene Therapy (NGGT), represents a groundbreaking approach utilizing recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 8 as a delivery vector carrying a functional copy of the human PAH gene[12][17]. This gene therapy represents an attempt to achieve a potentially curative or long-lasting correction of the underlying genetic defect responsible for PKU. NGGT002 is a Phase 1/2, open-label, multiple-center, dose escalation and cohort expansion study designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of NGGT002 in adult subjects with classic phenylketonuria[12][17]. Study participants receive a single administration of NGGT002 and will be followed for safety and efficacy for five years[12][17]. Male and female subjects aged 18-55 years with a diagnosis of classic PKU (characterized by severe PAH deficiency with confirmed PAH mutations predicted with no residual enzyme activity) are eligible[12][17].

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for NGGT002 trials carefully delineate the patient population likely to benefit from this approach. Subjects must be intolerant or unresponsive to available medical therapies such as Kuvan (sapropterin) or Palynziq (pegvaliase), or subjects who have been on such medications but discontinued them for medical reasons or personal decision at least 28 days prior to signing informed consent[12][17]. Subjects who have good disease control on existing therapies are explicitly excluded[12][17]. Multiple laboratory and clinical parameters establish exclusion criteria, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal and/or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal, alkaline phosphatase greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal, total bilirubin greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal, or direct bilirubin greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal[12][17]. Additional exclusions involve subjects with elevated circulating serum alpha-fetoprotein, subjects with prior investigational treatments received within 3 months before screening (or 5 half-lives, if longer), and other conditions the investigators deem inappropriate for enrollment, such as severe comorbidities including renal insufficiency or kidney failure, osteoporosis, anemia, acid reflux or gastroesophageal ulcer, major depression, and epilepsy[12][17].

Critically, patients who are presently on available medications for PKU treatment such as Kuvan or Palynziq are excluded from NGGT002 trials[12][17]. Additional exclusion criteria specify that subjects with weight over 120 kilograms or those consuming too much natural protein (>2 grams per kilogram body weight per day) in their daily diet cannot participate[12][17]. The early clinical evaluations of NGGT002 demonstrated substantial promise, with five of six patients receiving the high dose maintaining plasma phenylalanine concentrations near 120 micromoles per liter for up to 32 weeks after a single administration[31]. Long-term safety and efficacy outcomes are being assessed in ongoing trials[31].

The mechanisms being investigated with NGGT002 include direct gene addition to hepatocytes via rAAV8 vector-mediated transduction, which would establish a pool of functional PAH protein capable of metabolizing phenylalanine more effectively. Preclinical models have demonstrated the feasibility and potential efficacy of this approach, though the clinical translation remains in early phases of investigation.

### LUNAR-hPAH mRNA Replacement Therapy

Researchers have developed an mRNA replacement therapeutic approach to treat the underlying cause of phenylketonuria through delivery of the human phenylalanine hydroxylase gene sequence as an mRNA payload[13]. This innovative modality, designated LUNAR-hPAH mRNA, represents a distinct alternative to DNA-based gene therapy approaches. The technology involves lipid nanoparticle delivery vehicles optimized for hepatocyte targeting, with modifications including the LUNAR ionizable lipid formulation, which has been shown to be five times more efficient in delivering mRNA than formulations carrying MC3, the ionizable lipid of the FDA-approved drug Onpattro[13].

Intravenous infusion of LUNAR-hPAH mRNA has been shown to generate high levels of human phenylalanine hydroxylase protein in hepatocytes and restore phenylalanine metabolism in the Pah enu2 mouse model of PKU[13]. Treatment with LUNAR-hPAH mRNA was well tolerated with no apparent clinical observations and nothing significant was observed in clinical chemistry analysis of serum samples[13]. By 24 hours, treatment with LUNAR-hPAH mRNA decreased plasma phenylalanine levels in Pah enu2 homozygotes by approximately 20-fold post-dose, similar to the reduction of phenylalanine levels obtained with the low-phenylalanine diet regimen[13]. Phenylalanine levels gradually returned to pre-dose levels by 72 hours post-dose[13].

The mRNA approach offers several potential advantages over DNA-based or enzymatic approaches to treatment[13]. Modulating lipid composition of LUNAR nanoparticles allowed enrichment toward hepatocytes, as demonstrated by immunohistochemistry and fluorescence-activated cell sorting data[13]. Direct mRNA delivery to the cytoplasm of hepatocytes leads to immediate production of human phenylalanine hydroxylase protein, bypassing the risk of potential unintended genomic integration and allowing rapid efficacious responses[13]. The use of chemically modified mRNAs with uridine derivatives enhances translation, reduces immunogenicity driven by sensors such as Toll-Like Receptors and nuclease recognition, making the mRNA therapy more suitable for repeat dosing[13]. These characteristics suggest that mRNA replacement therapy could potentially provide a longer and effective solution to PKU patients compared with current approved standard of care approaches[13].

## Combination Therapy Approaches and Synergistic Treatment Regimens

### Dietary Phenylalanine Restriction Combined with Pharmacological Therapy

The foundational treatment for PKU remains dietary phenylalanine restriction, which prevents the accumulation of excessive phenylalanine in blood and brain through strict control of natural protein and phenylalanine intake[10][10]. Modern PKU management recognizes that dietary restriction must typically be maintained throughout life, contrary to earlier recommendations that suggested discontinuation after childhood[4]. A strict, lifelong low-phenylalanine diet remains the principal treatment in PKU and may be the sole treatment or used in combination with drug treatments[10]. All approved pharmacological therapies for PKU—sapropterin, pegvaliase, and sepiapterin—are designed to be used in conjunction with continued dietary phenylalanine restriction rather than as replacements for dietary management[1][2][4][10].

The specific implementation of PKU dietary management involves replacement of natural protein that has been removed from the diet with phenylalanine-free protein through synthetic amino acid mixtures or protein substitutes[10][10]. All protein substitutes are phenylalanine-free or contain very low phenylalanine concentrations[10][10]. The amount of dietary phenylalanine tolerated varies between individuals depending upon the severity of each person's PKU, with people with mild or moderate PKU tolerating more protein than those with classic PKU[10][10]. The daily phenylalanine allowance should be individualized according to each patient's tolerance to enable them to maintain consistent phenylalanine and protein intake[10][10].

When pharmacological therapies are added to the dietary foundation, they function to allow increased dietary liberalization. For example, in the APHENITY trial, participants on sepiapterin demonstrated an average 97 percent increase in dietary phenylalanine intake, with study participants increasing their dietary phenylalanine intake by an average of 126 percent[31]. Similarly, pegvaliase was designed with the goal of allowing patients to achieve life-long, reliable control of blood phenylalanine concentration while consuming diets that are unrestricted in protein[45].

### Large Neutral Amino Acid Supplementation

Large neutral amino acids supplementation represents a distinct therapeutic approach based on the understanding that large neutral amino acids share the same transport system to the brain, designated LAT-1[9][24]. By providing high concentrations of all large neutral amino acids except phenylalanine, competitive inhibition can reduce phenylalanine transport from the gut into the blood and then from the blood into the brain, thereby resulting in reduced cerebral phenylalanine concentrations[24]. The large neutral amino acids included in supplementation therapy are histidine, isoleucine, leucine, methionine, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, valine, and phenylalanine[9]. Large neutral amino acid supplements containing these amino acids (with phenylalanine removed) are marketed as an option for treating PKU[9].

Research indicates that large neutral amino acid supplementation reduces the amount of phenylalanine reaching the brain, contributes to patients receiving supplementation rich in tyrosine and threonine, and contributes to production of dopamine and serotonin[9]. The large neutral amino acid treatment also provides dietary liberalization described as increased dietary phenylalanine or natural protein intake[9]. This treatment approach could be considered an option in adolescents or adults with low adherence to phenylalanine-restricted diet[9]. One retrospective observational study of 29 PKU patients treated with large neutral amino acid supplementation between 2013-2022 found that LNAA treatment could be a treatment option for adolescents or adults who are not adhering to a phenylalanine-restricted diet[9].

Research on mice with PKU revealed that supplementing with tyrosine and tryptophan produced effects comparable to a high-protein diet in those given specific isoleucine, leucine, and threonine[9]. The group receiving tyrosine and tryptophan supplements exhibited significantly elevated serotonin levels[9]. Another experiment on animals demonstrated that large neutral amino acid supplementation increased brain serotonin and norepinephrine levels in mice while dopamine levels remained unchanged[9].

### Tyrosine Supplementation

Tyrosine represents an amino acid that is downstream of phenylalanine hydroxylase in the metabolic pathway and becomes deficient in patients with PKU due to reduced conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine[40]. A Cochrane systematic review examining tyrosine supplementation alongside or instead of a phenylalanine-restricted diet noted that although the amount of tyrosine measured in the blood of those taking the supplement was higher, there were no differences noted in other outcome measures including intelligence, neuropsychological performance, growth, nutritional status, mortality rate, or quality of life[40]. The review concluded that there is no evidence to suggest that tyrosine should be routinely added to the diet of people with phenylketonuria, though further randomized controlled trials would be needed to provide more definitive evidence[40].

## Mechanisms of Drug Action and Therapeutic Target Pathways

### Phenylalanine Hydroxylase as Primary Therapeutic Target

The fundamental pathophysiology of PKU involves deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase enzyme, which normally catalyzes the irreversible conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine in the presence of the essential cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin[14][28]. Without adequate PAH enzyme activity or in the absence of sufficient BH4 cofactor, dietary and endogenous phenylalanine cannot be adequately metabolized, leading to accumulation of phenylalanine in blood and brain[14][28]. The spectrum of severity in untreated PKU ranges from complete enzyme deficiency (classic PKU with phenylalanine concentration >1200 micromoles per liter), to moderate PKU (phenylalanine concentration 900-1200 micromoles per liter), mild PKU (phenylalanine concentration 600-900 micromoles per liter), and mild hyperphenylalaninemia (phenylalanine concentration 360-600 micromoles per liter)[28].

The PAH gene contains numerous pathogenic variants that determine responsiveness to different therapeutic modalities. Approximately 70 percent of patients who underwent BH4 testing demonstrated responsiveness to BH4 therapy, with phenotypes responding to BH4 treatment being mostly mild phenotypes[37]. Conversely, classic PKU phenotypes were associated with specific alleles such as c.1066-11G>A (splicing) variants, whereas the p.Arg408Trp allele was linked to severe symptoms[37]. This genotype-phenotype correlation has direct implications for treatment selection, with BH4-responsive patients potentially benefiting from sapropterin or sepiapterin, while those with non-responsive genotypes may require enzyme substitution therapy or novel approaches such as gene therapy.

### Sapropterin and Sepiapterin Mechanisms in Cofactor Optimization

Sapropterin functions as an exogenous source of synthetic BH4, providing the cofactor necessary for PAH enzyme to process phenylalanine[27]. The mechanism operates through stabilization and activation of residual PAH enzyme present in individuals with responsiveness-determining genetic variants[27]. Sepiapterin, by contrast, functions as a dual pharmacological chaperone, working both as a natural precursor to BH4 and through direct binding to variant PAH enzymes[1][35]. Sepiapterin acts as a dual pharmacological chaperone through mechanisms including enhancement of conformational stability of misfolded PAH enzyme and increasing intracellular concentrations of BH4, thereby enabling effective reduction of blood phenylalanine levels[35]. This dual mechanism may explain sepiapterin's superior efficacy compared with sapropterin across a broader patient population, including those with PAH variants typically considered non-BH4-responsive.

### Pegvaliase as Enzyme Substitution Therapy

Pegvaliase functions through a fundamentally distinct mechanism from cofactor optimization strategies, directly metabolizing phenylalanine through enzymatic degradation rather than enhancing the activity of deficient endogenous enzyme[28][30]. The pegylated recombinant phenylalanine ammonia lyase enzyme converts phenylalanine to ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid[28]. Ammonia is subsequently metabolized by the liver[28]. This mechanism permits efficacy independent of residual PAH activity or BH4 responsiveness, making pegvaliase a genotype-independent therapy suitable for any patient with PKU regardless of underlying genetic variation[8]. The PEGylation of the PAL enzyme reduces immune recognition and improves pharmacodynamic stability, though patients still develop anti-drug antibodies as discussed above[38].

### Repinatrabit Mechanism at SLC6A19 Transporter

Repinatrabit operates through selective inhibition of the solute carrier family 6 member 19 transporter, which mediates kidney reabsorption of filtered phenylalanine[11][11]. By blocking this transporter at a cryptic allosteric site, repinatrabit prevents renal reabsorption of phenylalanine, thereby increasing urinary phenylalanine excretion and reducing blood phenylalanine levels[11][11]. This mechanism represents a novel approach distinct from enzyme optimization or enzyme substitution, instead targeting a physiological process (renal reabsorption) rather than hepatic metabolism. The rapid onset of action observed with repinatrabit (phenylalanine reduction within one week of dosing) may reflect the pharmacological potency of this mechanism[11].

## Contraindications and Important Safety Considerations

### Contraindications and Drug Interactions with Sepiapterin

Sepiapterin carries no formal contraindications in its prescribing information[1]. However, specific drug interactions warrant careful attention. The prescribing information specifically advises avoiding concomitant use of drugs known to inhibit folate synthesis via dihydrofolate reductase inhibition[1]. These include trimethoprim, methotrexate, trimetrexate, pemetrexed, pralatrexate, raltitrexed, and piritrexim[1]. The mechanistic basis for this contraindication involves the fact that concomitant administration of such drugs may reduce sepiapterin metabolism to BH4[1]. If concomitant use is not avoidable, blood phenylalanine levels require careful monitoring[1]. The interaction with methotrexate and trimethoprim is particularly notable given the clinical use of these medications in cancer therapy and infectious disease treatment respectively; clinicians prescribing these agents to patients with PKU on sepiapterin must carefully weigh risks and benefits.

### Aspartame-Containing Medications as Contraindicated Substances

Aspartame represents a critical medication contraindication for all PKU patients, whether or not they are receiving pharmacological therapy[4][20]. Aspartame is a phenylalanine-containing sweetener added to foods, drinks, and medications[4][20][44]. Aspartame is metabolized in the gastrointestinal tract into phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and methanol[36]. The amount of phenylalanine provided by aspartame is often not identifiable from food and drinks labels[20]. Importantly, the amount of phenylalanine in aspartame-containing foods and drinks is not declared on ingredient labels, and its impact on metabolic control in patients with PKU is not well established[20].

Aspartame can be safely consumed by healthy individuals, but it has long been recognized as a hazard to individuals with phenylketonuria and should be avoided[20]. However, a recent equality risk assessment conducted by the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency examining the soft drinks industry levy stated that they were unaware of any evidence to suggest that existing warnings on food labels about the presence of aspartame in soft drinks were inadequate for people with PKU[20]. Aspartame is commonly added to a wide variety of foods including low calorie sweeteners, soft drinks, iced tea, flavored mineral water, energy drinks, dessert mixes, frozen desserts, syrups/dessert sauces, mints, jelly, chewing gum, fruit yogurt, ice lollies, and ice creams[20].

Critically, aspartame is commonly used as a sugar replacement in antibiotics and chewable tablets[20]. A comprehensive analysis identified 401 medications containing phenylalanine or aspartame, with the main medications containing significant amounts of aspartame and phenylalanine intake being amoxicillin, the combination of amoxicillin plus clavulanic acid, and paracetamol/acetaminophen[36]. The European PKU guidelines recommend that for immediate and short-term treatment of infections, if only aspartame-containing medicines are available, it may be better to use these medications until aspartame-free medication is sourced rather than leave a person with PKU without treatment for a concurrent illness, as blood phenylalanine levels will rise with infection[20]. This pragmatic approach recognizes the competing risks of untreated infection versus temporary elevation in blood phenylalanine from aspartame-containing medications.

### Anesthetic Considerations and Perioperative Management

Specific anesthetic medications warrant avoidance or cautious use in patients with PKU. Certain anesthetic medications should be avoided or used with extreme caution, including muscle relaxants metabolized by ester hydrolysis into odd-chain organic acids such as succinylcholine, cisatracurium, and mivacurium[25]. Propofol, while not absolutely contraindicated, requires caution as it uncouples oxidative phosphorylation and inhibits mitochondrial complex I, the main mechanism of propofol infusion syndrome[25]. In vitro, elevated phenylalanine concentrations can inhibit the activity of mitochondrial complex I as well by competing with nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate[25]. Combined inhibition of the mitochondrial respiratory chain can manifest as hyperthermia and acidosis[25]. In a review of 14 cases of patients with methylmalonic acidemia undergoing liver transplantation, propofol was used for induction and maintenance in the first 7 of 14 patients; after the seventh patient, propofol was no longer used out of concern that it contributed to metabolic decompensation, although the first five patients had no evidence of decompensation[25].

Nitrous oxide should be used with extreme caution in patients with PKU who are vitamin B12-deficient, as nitrous oxide inactivates B12-dependent methionine synthase[25]. Additionally, analgesics derived from propionic acid such as ibuprofen and naproxen should be avoided in patients with PKU, whereas ketorolac, derived from acetic acid, does not present the same risk[25]. Oral intake should not be stopped for an extended period preoperatively, as this can cause a catabolic state leading to increased serum phenylalanine levels[25]. For major or emergency surgery, intravenous dextrose should be given before and after surgery to avoid a catabolic state[25].

### Adverse Events Associated with Pegvaliase

The comprehensive pharmacovigilance analysis utilizing the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System identified 6,264 reports related to pegvaliase[19]. Arthralgia emerged as the most frequently reported adverse event, with additional unexpected signals identified including lymphadenopathy, serum sickness, and tunnel vision[19]. Weibull distribution analysis revealed that the incidence of adverse events decreased over time[19]. Common adverse reactions encompass anaphylaxis, tachycardia, angioedema, rash, urticaria, pruritus, and gastrointestinal symptoms such as vomiting, nausea, and diarrhea[19]. Most adverse events such as injection site erythema, rash, heart rate increase, and oropharyngeal pain were consistent with findings from clinical trials[19].

Volcano plot analysis revealed that injection site reaction, injection site erythema, injection site pruritus, arthralgia, and injection site swelling were more prevalent among self-reported consumers[19]. In the healthcare professionals reporting group, the top three symptoms were arthralgia, anaphylactic reaction, and urticaria[19]. Early clinical trials identified major adverse reactions including allergic reactions that can occur at any stage of treatment[19]. Additional major adverse reaction symptoms included injection site reactions, arthralgia, gastrointestinal symptoms, headache, systemic skin reactions, nausea, cough, sore throat, itching, nasal congestion, fatigue, and anxiety[19].

## Maternal Phenylketonuria and Pregnancy Management

A particularly critical consideration for PKU management involves maternal phenylketonuria syndrome, which arises when women with PKU have elevated phenylalanine levels during pregnancy, leading to teratogenic effects in the fetus[29][39]. During pregnancy, phenylalanine crosses the placenta, and phenylalanine levels in the developing fetus can be higher than what is measured in the pregnant woman[39]. High levels of phenylalanine during pregnancy can cause severe complications for the fetus[29][39]. These complications include microcephaly, congenital heart defects, intrauterine growth restriction, and intellectual disabilities[29][39]. Ensuring adequate metabolic control through a strict low-phenylalanine diet before and during pregnancy is essential to avoid these risks[29].

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics recommends that women with PKU have phenylalanine levels ≤360 micromoles per liter before getting pregnant and maintain phenylalanine levels ≤360 micromoles per liter throughout pregnancy and in the postpartum period for the best maternal and infant outcomes[39]. However, recent data indicate that up to 40 percent of women with PKU may experience difficulty adhering to the necessary dietary restrictions during pregnancy despite clear evidence of the detrimental effects of non-adherence[29]. It is important not to allow phenylalanine levels to become too low, as some studies show that very low phenylalanine levels (below 100 micromoles per liter) can cause growth problems, especially during the second and third trimesters[39].

Recent case reports underscore the severe consequences of inadequate prenatal care and poor dietary adherence in women with PKU[29]. In one documented case, prenatal care was initiated in the 23rd week of gestation, delaying dietary intervention[29]. Prenatal exposure to elevated maternal phenylalanine levels due to untreated PKU was identified as the cause of observed anomalies, with the maternal phenylalanine level on the day of delivery being 22 milligrams per deciliter, more than 3-4 times higher than the upper recommended limit for pregnant women of 6 milligrams per deciliter or 360 micromoles per liter[29]. The findings were consistent with maternal PKU syndrome, presenting as a constellation of teratogenic effects arising from inadequate metabolic control during pregnancy[29]. This case emphasizes the pivotal role of maintaining strict metabolic control in women with PKU both prior to conception and throughout pregnancy to avert maternal PKU syndrome in offspring[29].

## Screening, Diagnosis, and Treatment Initiation Considerations

### Newborn Screening and Early Diagnosis

Phenylketonuria is detected through standardized newborn screening programs available for two generations in many developed nations[14][44]. A PKU screening test measures the amount of phenylalanine in a sample of a newborn's blood[44]. All newborns in the United States are required to be tested for PKU[44]. The test is easy and has almost no health risks, and early detection of PKU allows for prevention of lifelong brain damage and other serious health problems through implementation of a special diet low in protein and phenylalanine[44]. The screening is usually performed as part of newborn screening between 24 and 72 hours after birth[44]. If a baby was not born in the hospital or if the family left the hospital early, the screening should be scheduled as soon as possible[44].

If a newborn's screening test shows abnormal results, additional confirmatory testing may be necessary. If both parents know they carry a gene for PKU and the baby's test is normal, the baby should have genetic testing to definitively confirm or rule out PKU[44]. Genetic testing involves checking the baby's genes for pathogenic variants[44]. Some older infants and children may need testing if they were born in another country or if the results of newborn screening were negative for PKU, especially if they have symptoms of PKU[44].

### Treatment Initiation and Target Phenylalanine Levels

Current guidelines recommend that treatment should be initiated before 10 days of age, which in many countries requires changes to national newborn screening timing, logistics, and diagnostic procedures[41]. There is unanimity in the literature among professionals that patients with untreated blood phenylalanine concentrations >600 micromoles per liter should be treated[41]. However, evidence regarding initiation of treatment with blood phenylalanine concentrations between 360 and 600 micromoles per liter is inconsistent[41]. Costello and colleagues found a trend towards lower intelligence quotient in those with higher phenylalanine levels when comparing three groups (<400, 400-500, and >500 micromoles per liter) and recommended treatment to maintain phenylalanine <400 micromoles per liter throughout childhood in all forms of PKU[41].

The evidence supporting treatment is of suboptimal quality, while evidence supporting no treatment is of better quality[41]. However, the number of patients with blood phenylalanine levels just below 600 micromoles per liter is considered too low and a different statistical analysis would be more informative[41]. Recommendations indicate that patients with an untreated phenylalanine concentration between 360 and 600 micromoles per liter should be treated during the first 12 years of age, particularly as good metabolic control during childhood appears essential to prevent cognitive function impairment in PKU[41].

For patients aged 12 years or older with untreated phenylalanine levels <600 micromoles per liter, follow-up at a lower frequency is recommended, but remains particularly important in women due to the risks associated with maternal PKU when blood phenylalanine levels are >360 micromoles per liter[41]. Women need to be advised at each clinic that dietary treatment or BH4 therapy (or both) is essential pre-conception and during pregnancy[41].

The phenylketonuria treatment landscape has undergone substantial transformation between 2025 and 2026 with multiple regulatory approvals and expanded indications[31]. In July 2025, PTC Therapeutics announced FDA approval of sepiapterin with broad labeling inclusive of all disease subtypes for individuals one month of age and older[1]. In October 2025, the FDA agreed to review BioMarin Pharmaceutical's supplemental biologics license application for pegvaliase-pqpz seeking approval to extend its use to adolescents aged 12-17 years with phenylketonuria[31].

In December 2025, Otsuka announced the initiation of the global Phase 3 PheORD clinical trial evaluating repinatrabit (JNT-517) for PKU treatment[11][11]. This phase 3 trial design included approximately 120 patients randomized to one of two repinatrabit dose groups (75 milligrams or 150 milligrams twice daily) or placebo for six weeks, with additional study extension through 52 weeks[23]. In March 2025, PTC Therapeutics presented Phase 3 APHENITY trial data showing that sepiapterin enabled 97 percent of PKU patients to increase dietary phenylalanine intake by an average of 126 percent[31].

## Conclusion and Synthesis of Current Treatment Landscape

The pharmacological management of phenylketonuria has undergone dramatic evolution from a disease managed exclusively through lifelong dietary restriction to one amenable to multiple therapeutic interventions targeting distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. The approval of sepiapterin (Sephience) in 2025 represents a watershed moment in PKU therapeutics, providing superior efficacy to the long-established sapropterin (Kuvan) with applicability across the entire spectrum of disease severity, including previously difficult-to-treat non-BH4-responsive genotypes. The enzyme substitution therapy pegvaliase (Palynziq), approved initially for adults with uncontrolled phenylalanine despite existing management, has demonstrated sustained blood phenylalanine reduction with a manageable though notable safety profile requiring careful patient selection and monitoring. The investigational pipeline includes compelling candidates such as repinatrabit (JNT-517), which employs a novel mechanism targeting renal phenylalanine reabsorption with rapid onset of action and promising safety data from Phase 2 trials, and advanced gene therapy approaches including AAV-based NGGT002 and mRNA-based therapeutics that potentially offer more durable or curative correction of the underlying genetic defect[1][21][22][30][31][11].

The evidence supporting contemporary pharmacological treatment of PKU emphasizes the importance of individualized therapy selection based on patient age, disease severity, genotype-phenotype correlations, comorbidities, tolerability considerations, and treatment goals. Combination strategies employing dietary phenylalanine restriction as the foundational approach, supplemented with emerging pharmacological options tailored to individual responsiveness and clinical circumstances, represent the current standard of care. Particular attention remains necessary to critical clinical considerations including maternal PKU syndrome prevention through strict metabolic control in women of childbearing years, careful monitoring for drug interactions particularly involving medications containing aspartame or inhibitors of folate synthesis, and recognition of the immunological considerations associated with enzyme replacement therapy. The continuing expansion of approved and investigational treatment options through 2026 and beyond promises to further transform the therapeutic landscape, potentially offering unprecedented opportunities for improved quality of life and metabolic control across the full spectrum of PKU disease severity and genotypic diversity[31].
