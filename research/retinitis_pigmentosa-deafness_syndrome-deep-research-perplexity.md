# Comprehensive Review of Drug Therapies for Retinitis Pigmentosa-Deafness Syndrome (Usher Syndrome)

Retinitis pigmentosa-deafness syndrome, commonly known as Usher syndrome, represents one of the most severe forms of inherited retinal disease, affecting approximately one in 10,000 individuals globally, with an estimated 400,000 people affected worldwide[7]. This comprehensive research report examines the current landscape of approved therapeutics, investigational drugs, drug repurposing opportunities, contraindicated medications, and relevant adverse drug effects in the treatment of Usher syndrome and related forms of retinitis pigmentosa. As of April 2026, the therapeutic landscape for this condition has expanded significantly, with multiple promising investigational approaches now advancing through late-stage clinical development, representing a major shift from the historical absence of disease-modifying treatments.

## Disease Overview and Clinical Context

Usher syndrome is characterized by the combination of congenital neurosensory hearing loss and progressive retinal degeneration manifesting as retinitis pigmentosa, with variable vestibular dysfunction[7]. The syndrome is classified into three distinct clinical subtypes based on the severity and timing of symptoms. Type 1 Usher syndrome represents the most severe form, characterized by profound congenital bilateral hearing loss, vestibular dysfunction causing developmental motor delays, and prepubertal onset of progressive vision loss due to retinitis pigmentosa[1][7]. Type 2 patients typically present with mild to moderate congenital hearing loss with sloping audiograms and later-onset retinitis pigmentosa, usually diagnosed during puberty or later, while maintaining normal vestibular function[7]. Type 3 patients experience progressive post-lingual hearing loss with delayed and variable onset of retinitis pigmentosa symptoms, potentially manifesting in mid-adulthood[1]. The pathophysiology involves mutations in genes encoding proteins that form critical interactomes in the sensory cells of both the inner ear and retina, including motor proteins, scaffold proteins, transmembrane proteins, adhesion proteins, and G protein-coupled receptors[7].

The progression of vision loss in Usher syndrome typically begins with night blindness caused by rod photoreceptor degeneration, followed by progressive constriction of the visual field resulting in tunnel vision, and ultimately complete blindness if the condition progresses without intervention[1][1]. Historically, adaptive techniques such as braille, digital and optical magnifiers, mobility training, cochlear implants, and assistive listening devices have been the only available options for reducing disability[1]. However, this treatment landscape has undergone substantial transformation, particularly over the past five years, with multiple investigational therapies now demonstrating clinical efficacy and advancing toward regulatory approval.

## Approved Drug Therapies

### LUXTURNA (Voretigene Neparvovec-rzyl)

LUXTURNA represents the only currently FDA-approved gene therapy for inherited retinal disease, having received marketing approval from the United States Food and Drug Administration in December 2017[9][13][8]. However, the application of this therapy to Usher syndrome patients is severely limited by its specificity to mutations in the *RPE65* gene. LUXTURNA is indicated exclusively for patients with inherited retinal disease (IRD) due to confirmed biallelic *RPE65* gene mutations[13]. The mechanism of action involves subretinal injection of a recombinant adeno-associated viral vector serotype 2 (AAV2) carrying a functional copy of the human *RPE65* complementary DNA into the retinal pigment epithelium cells[35]. The transduced RPE cells produce functional RPE65 protein, which is essential for regenerating 11-*cis*-retinal, a critical visual pigment component necessary for maintaining the visual cycle[35][39]. This mechanism restores the biochemical pathway leading to production of 11-*cis*-retinal and improves photoreceptor function.

Clinical trial results demonstrated profound benefits for eligible patients. Study participants with severe vision loss reported substantive functional improvements, including the ability to place away navigational canes, seeing stars in the night sky, reading printed material, and recognizing faces of loved ones[8]. Vision restoration has been documented to persist for at least three years following a single subretinal injection[8][39]. The treatment protocol involves vitrectomy surgery followed by a single subretinal injection of the vector, and the procedure is performed in an operating room under controlled surgical conditions[35]. While *RPE65*-associated retinal dystrophy represents a distinct genetic etiology separate from the nine genes primarily associated with Usher syndrome (*MYO7A, USH1C, PCDH15, CDH23, USH1G, USH2A, ADGRV1, WHRN, CLRN1*), some patients with Usher syndrome who carry biallelic *RPE65* mutations represent a small subset that could theoretically benefit from LUXTURNA[39]. However, the vast majority of Usher syndrome patients carry mutations in other genes and therefore remain ineligible for this therapy.

The development and approval of LUXTURNA has catalyzed innovation across the retinitis pigmentosa treatment market, serving as proof-of-concept that gene therapy can effectively treat inherited retinal diseases[9]. The Foundation Fighting Blindness invested approximately $10 million in more than a decade of laboratory research that enabled the LUXTURNA clinical trial at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia[8]. This landmark achievement has inspired development of numerous additional gene therapy approaches targeting other genetic forms of retinal degeneration.

## Investigational and Pipeline Drugs in Clinical Development

### Antisense Oligonucleotide Therapies

#### QR-421a for USH2A Exon 13 Mutations

QR-421a represents one of the most advanced investigational therapies specifically targeting Usher syndrome, developed by ProQR Therapeutics. This antisense oligonucleotide (AON) drug is designed to induce skipping of exon 13 in the *USH2A* gene, one of the most frequent causes of both syndromic and non-syndromic retinitis pigmentosa[26][10]. Mutations in *USH2A* are among the most common causes of Usher syndrome type 2 and non-syndromic retinitis pigmentosa. The mechanism of action involves masking the disease mutation in the RNA transcript, allowing cells to produce functional usherin protein that lacks the amino acids encoded by exon 13 but retains sufficient residual function to prevent progressive loss of retinal function[26].

Preclinical evidence established the foundation for clinical development. Studies in zebrafish models bearing *ush2a* mutations demonstrated that usherin lacking the amino acids encoded by exon 13 possesses sufficient residual function to prevent loss of retinal function[26]. Cellular modeling identified QR-421a as the lead candidate molecule. Reverse transcription PCR analysis of patient-derived fibroblasts and photoreceptor precursor cells (PPCs) homozygous for the c.2299delG mutation revealed that QR-421a induced significant levels of exon 13 skipping at all tested concentrations (1–10 μM), with exons 12 and 14 retained within the *USH2A* Δexon 13 transcript[26]. At a 1-μM concentration, exon 13 skipping was observed in 42% ± 11% of *USH2A* transcripts. Critically, exon 13 skipping with QR-421a restored electroretinogram (ERG) b-wave amplitudes in treated cells, indicating improved retinal function[26]. The corresponding mouse AON demonstrated long *in vivo* duration of action in mouse retina following a single intravitreal treatment.

The clinical development program for QR-421a was originally designated the Stellar trial (NCT03780257), evaluating the safety and tolerability of QR-421a administered via intravitreal injection in subjects with retinitis pigmentosa due to mutations in exon 13 of the *USH2A* gene[10]. Three dose levels were evaluated: 50, 100, and 200 μg, with additional dose levels potentially evaluated based on safety and efficacy data. The study protocol included a 24-month follow-up period assessing safety, tolerability, and efficacy endpoints including changes in dark-adapted chromatic (DAC) perimetry[10]. Preliminary findings were reported on April 20, 2020, representing the first treatment to the eyes of an Usher 2A patient using QR-421a. These positive interim results led to advancement of the program and garnered significant interest from the field.

However, ProQR's antisense oligonucleotide program for USH2A was subsequently transferred to Théa and its newly established business unit Sepul Bio, which has now launched the Phase 2b LUNA clinical trial for ultevursen (formerly known as QR-421a), specifically designed for people with retinitis pigmentosa or Usher syndrome caused by mutations in exon 13 of the *USH2A* gene[33][34]. The two-year LUNA trial enrolled 81 patients (adults and children over eight years of age) at multiple global sites, with the first patient dosed in December 2024. In ProQR's previous Phase 1/2 clinical trial, ultevursen demonstrated benefits in best corrected visual acuity, retinal sensitivity, and retinal structure in patients with USH2A exon 13 mutations[34].

#### Sepofarsen for Leber Congenital Amaurosis 10

While primarily developed for Leber congenital amaurosis type 10 (LCA10) caused by *CEP290* mutations, sepofarsen merits discussion in the context of Usher syndrome research as it represents a successful proof-of-concept for antisense oligonucleotide therapy in photoreceptor disorders. Sepofarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide designed to mask the intronic IVS26 mutation in the *CEP290* gene, allowing cells to produce functional CEP290 protein[33]. The mechanism involves restoration of normal splicing of the *CEP290* transcript. Sepul Bio, a business unit of Laboratories Théa, dosed the first participants in the Phase 3 HYPERION clinical trial for sepofarsen in October 2025[33]. The two-year randomized, placebo-controlled HYPERION trial enrolled 32 patients, including adults and children six years of age or older, at multiple sites across the US, Canada, and Europe. At the start of the study, the two eyes of each patient were randomized so that one eye received sepofarsen and the other eye received placebo for the first year. Sepofarsen and placebo were injected into the vitreous every six months[33].

### Gene Therapy Approaches

#### MCO-010 for Advanced Retinitis Pigmentosa

MCO-010 represents a paradigm shift in therapeutic approach for advanced retinitis pigmentosa, including late-stage Usher syndrome. Developed by Nanoscope Therapeutics, MCO-010 is an optogenetic gene therapy designed to restore vision in patients who have experienced substantial photoreceptor degeneration. Unlike traditional gene replacement therapies that target specific genetic mutations, MCO-010 takes a disease-agnostic approach by utilizing the remaining visual circuitry following photoreceptor death[29][32]. The mechanism involves intravitreal injection of the MCO platform, which activates highly dense bipolar retinal cells to become light sensitive through expression of a multi-characteristic opsin (MCO) protein. By leveraging the intact inner retinal circuitry, this approach can theoretically benefit patients regardless of their specific genetic mutation, making it applicable to the broad spectrum of Usher syndrome subtypes.

MCO-010 has demonstrated durable and clinically meaningful vision improvements in long-term follow-up studies. The Phase 2b/3 RESTORE trial and its long-term extension study (REMAIN) demonstrated sustained efficacy through 152 weeks of follow-up. Patients maintained an average best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) gain from baseline of approximately 0.3 LogMAR, equivalent to three lines or 15 letters on a standard ETDRS vision chart, through Week 152[29]. The BCVA area-under-the-curve profiles across both RESTORE and REMAIN showed fivefold greater vision gains compared to sham treatment[29]. From a safety perspective, no serious ocular adverse events were reported among treated patients, and only one mild case of inflammation required topical steroids, with 14 of 15 treated patients requiring no ongoing inflammation management at Week 152[29]. The favorable safety and tolerability profile has been maintained through the three-year follow-up period.

MCO-010 received FDA Fast Track and Orphan Drug designations for both retinitis pigmentosa and Stargardt disease, along with RMAT designation for Stargardt disease[29]. The European Medicines Agency granted MCO-010 five Orphan designations across a wide spectrum of retinal degenerations involving photoreceptor loss. A rolling Biologics License Application (BLA) submission to the FDA was initiated in June 2025, with the potential for MCO-010 to become the first FDA-approved optogenetic therapy[29]. The treatment is administered as a one-time, in-office intravitreal injection without the need for genetic testing, enabling broad patient applicability within existing retina office workflows[29][32]. Nanoscope Therapeutics is also conducting a Phase 2 program for MCO in geographic atrophy with plans to initiate therapy by the end of 2025, and other IND-ready programs include Leber congenital amaurosis.

Post-hoc clinical analyses from the three-year randomized Phase 2b/3 data are being presented to provide clinicians with a richer framework for understanding the effects of one-time MCO-010 treatment and identifying patient and disease characteristics associated with best outcomes[32]. This additional analysis is expected to inform clinical decision-making regarding patient selection for optogenetic therapy.

#### Laru-zova for X-Linked Retinitis Pigmentosa

Beacon Therapeutics has developed laru-zova, a gene therapy targeting X-linked retinitis pigmentosa (XLRP) caused by mutations in the *RPGR* gene. Although XLRP represents a distinct genetic form separate from Usher syndrome, the development of laru-zova demonstrates important advances in gene therapy delivery and efficacy that have implications for the broader retinitis pigmentosa field. Laru-zova consists of a full-length *RPGR* gene delivered via a highly efficient AAV capsid with a stabilized gene cassette, administered through subretinal injection at two dose levels[27].

The Phase 2 DAWN trial demonstrated vision improvements in patients who previously received treatment. Specifically, 15 male patients with XLRP who received the treatment in one eye in a previous trial had their second eyes dosed with laru-zova. The trial showed greater 2- and 3-line improvements in study eyes compared to previously treated fellow eyes, with a safety profile consistent with previous studies[27]. The pivotal Phase 2/3 VISTA trial completed enrollment across sites in North America, the United Kingdom, and Australia in July 2025, exceeding initial enrollment projections and reflecting strong interest from both patients and investigators[27]. The study enrolled male patients aged 12 to 50 and was designed to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of laru-zova at two dose levels compared to an untreated control group. Twelve-month topline data from the VISTA trial were expected in the second half of 2026[27].

Laru-zova has received multiple regulatory designations supporting accelerated development. The therapy received Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy (RMAT) and Fast Track designations from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Priority Medicines (PRIME) designation from the European Medicines Agency, and Innovative Licensing and Access Pathway (ILAP) from the UK's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency[27]. Additionally, laru-zova received Orphan Drug Designation from both the FDA and EMA[27]. Beacon planned to use data from the VISTA trial, alongside long-term data from the DAWN trial and other Phase 1/2 studies (HORIZON and SKYLINE) to support regulatory submissions in the United States and Europe.

#### SPVN20 for Advanced Retinitis Pigmentosa

SparingVision's SPVN20 represents another innovative gene-agnostic approach to restoring vision in advanced and late-stage retinitis pigmentosa patients with "dormant cones." The therapy's unique mechanism of action allows restoration of visual acuity and color vision independently of the patient's genetic mutation[14]. In retinitis pigmentosa, the outer segment of cone photoreceptors atrophies as the disease progresses, losing the ability to respond to light. Critically, cone photoreceptors remain present but become non-functional or "dormant," as the phototransduction cascade—which normally occurs in the outer segment of cones to convert light into electrical signals—can no longer function in these atrophied structures[14]. SPVN20 introduces a GIRK (G-protein inwardly rectifying potassium) channel into dormant cones, creating an alternative phototransduction cascade that allows dormant cones to become functional again despite the absence of normal outer segment structure[14]. Critically, opsins remain present on the membrane of dormant cones and can be activated in ambient, natural light, making them responsive to SPVN20-mediated restoration of the phototransduction pathway.

SparingVision dosed the first patient in its NYRVANA Phase 1/2 clinical trial for SPVN20 in 2025[8]. The trial is designed to restore cone vision for people with advanced retinitis pigmentosa and potentially other inherited retinal diseases. Beyond retinitis pigmentosa, SparingVision plans to explore opportunities to evaluate SPVN20 in other inherited retinal diseases where its mechanism of action could be particularly relevant[14]. SparingVision also intends to evaluate the potential therapeutic synergy of its two lead products through combination of SPVN06 and SPVN20 as a single construct, called SPVN30, to address a broader population of retinitis pigmentosa patients[14].

#### Additional Gene Therapy Candidates

At Harvard Medical School, neurobiologist David Corey's laboratory has developed three candidate gene therapies for Usher 1F blindness, each taking a different approach to correcting the disease-causing mutation in the *PCDH15* gene[5]. Usher 1F is a particularly severe form of Usher syndrome in which the gene mutation causes cells in both the eye and ear to stop producing protocadherin-15, an essential protein. The researchers identified that protocadherin-15 in the inner ear helps sensory receptors called hair cells convert mechanical vibrations into electrical signals, which the brain interprets as sound[5]. Without protocadherin-15, this conversion cannot occur and the brain is unable to detect sound. The same protein was also found in light-sensing cells (photoreceptors) in the eye. The researchers designed gene therapies that either replace or repair the defective DNA that codes for protocadherin-15, with delivered therapies inside eye cells allowing cells to make the missing protein[5]. This work represents an important proof-of-concept for therapeutic approaches targeting specific Usher syndrome genetic subtypes.

### Antioxidant and Small Molecule Therapies

#### Nacuity NPI-001 (N-Acetylcysteine Amide)

Nacuity Pharmaceuticals' NPI-001 represents a significant advancement in small molecule therapy for retinitis pigmentosa associated with Usher syndrome. NPI-001 is a proprietary, GMP-grade formulation of N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA) delivered as oral tablets, designed to target oxidative stress associated with retinitis pigmentosa[12]. Preclinical studies indicate that NPI-001 boosts glutathione, the body's most powerful endogenous antioxidant, to prevent chemically aggressive oxygen molecules (reactive oxygen species) from damaging retinal cells[12].

The Phase 1/2 SLO-RP clinical trial demonstrated compelling clinical efficacy. This randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial enrolled 49 patients at four clinical sites in Australia who were administered oral NPI-001 or placebo tablets twice daily and followed for two years[12]. The study measured multiple endpoints including ellipsoid zone (EZ) area determined by spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), an indication of photoreceptor health assessed by masked experts at a central reading center. Retinal sensitivity was assessed by MAIA microperimetry, a measure of functional vision. Database lock was completed in June 2025. Key findings showed that NPI-001 slowed photoreceptor loss by more than 50% over two years[12]. Following daily dosing of NPI-001, the loss of photoreceptors was significantly lower than with placebo, starting at 6 months and continuing throughout the 24-month study[12]. Retinal sensitivity did not reach statistical significance at 24 months; however, a favorable trend was observed in participants treated with NPI-001, who demonstrated nearly 30% slower loss of visual function compared to those receiving placebo[12]. Effects on ellipsoid zone area and retinal sensitivity were highly correlated[12]. Critically, NPI-001 was safe and well-tolerated at dose levels of 500 mg daily, with over 80% compliance with planned dosing[12].

These results provide important clinical validation of NPI-001's potential to slow photoreceptor loss in retinitis pigmentosa associated with Usher syndrome. Notably, NPI-001 is the only small molecule drug for which a measurable treatment effect has ever been objectively demonstrated for retinitis pigmentosa[12]. The trial results constitute initial proof-of-concept for the treatment of retinitis pigmentosa with NPI-001 and provide support for further studies of this drug as a gene-agnostic treatment[12]. Nacuity partnered with the Foundation Fighting Blindness on various initiatives related to NPI-001 development, including trial design, awareness, education, and strategic guidance[12]. The Foundation Fighting Blindness invested up to $7.5 million to advance NPI-001 as a promising, emerging drug for retinitis pigmentosa, Usher syndrome, and related conditions[8][8]. The trials directly informed the design of a confirmatory Phase 3 study planned for initiation in 2026.

Concurrently, Johns Hopkins University and the National Eye Institute are conducting an international Phase 3 clinical trial for NAC—the original, FDA-approved formulation of N-acetylcysteine[8][8]. This trial, known as NAC Attack, is a 45-month study that has enrolled approximately 438 patients at 30 sites throughout the US, Canada, Mexico, and Europe[8][8]. Study participants have been randomly assigned 2:1 to either the treatment or placebo group, with NAC approved by the FDA in 1963 for acetaminophen overdose treatment. The National Eye Institute provided more than $20 million in funding for the NAC Attack trial[8]. If the NAC group shows benefit (slowing of vision loss) at 21 months, participants in the placebo group will begin to receive the treatment[8][8].

#### Topical Antioxidant Eye Drop Formulations

Recent research has explored the potential of topical delivery of antioxidants through gel-forming eye drops for treating retinitis pigmentosa. This approach addresses the challenge of delivering therapeutic antioxidants to the posterior retina through non-invasive topical administration[21]. N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) and N-acetyl cysteine ethyl ester (NACET) were formulated in a hypotonic, gel-forming vehicle designed to provide therapeutic drug delivery to the posterior segment following topical application[21]. The antioxidant activity of NAC and NACET was assessed in an *in vitro* ROS assay in ARPE-19 cells using TBHP as the oxidative stress-generating reagent. A concentration-dependent increase in ROS level was observed with increasing TBHP from 50 μM to 200 μM[21]. When TBHP was co-incubated with either NAC, NACET, or diNAC (two NAC molecules bridged via disulfide bond, masking the active thiol) for 9 hours, a concentration-dependent reduction in ROS generation was observed[21]. ROS generation was reduced by 55.6% and 53.1% of the maximum following co-incubation with 25 μM NAC and NACET, respectively[21]. After increasing concentrations to 50 μM, ROS generation was further reduced to 69.3% and 73.2% of the maximum, respectively[21].

Efficacy was subsequently tested in rodent models of retinal degeneration. Topical administration of NAC and NACET in gel-forming eye drops provided functional and structural protection of photoreceptors in a chemically-induced rat model of retinal photoreceptor death, with once-daily application demonstrating therapeutic benefit[21]. Testing was then extended to a genetically-induced model. The therapeutic efficacy of topical NAC and NACET dosing was more evident in the rat model of paraquat-induced photoreceptor death compared to the *rd10* mouse genetic model, likely reflecting the fact that the rat model involves non-specific photoreceptor degradation due to chemical insult, whereas the genetic mouse model involves disease-specific mechanisms[21]. Nonetheless, treatment demonstrated potential for protection of retina structure and function. Through formulation of water-soluble antioxidants NAC and NACET in a hypotonic, gel-forming vehicle, researchers demonstrated the potential for protection of retinal structure and function in rodents[21]. The authors concluded that a formulation optimized for therapeutic antioxidant delivery to the retina has the potential to provide all retinitis pigmentosa patients a minimally invasive treatment option for slowing progressive photoreceptor death and vision loss[21], though further work in maintaining antioxidant stability and formulation for daily use, as well as studies in larger species, remains necessary.

### Cell Therapy Approaches

#### DSP-3077 (Sumitomo Pharma)

Sumitomo Pharma America announced that the FDA granted Orphan Drug Designation to DSP-3077, an investigational cell therapy for the treatment of retinitis pigmentosa, on March 24, 2026[6]. DSP-3077 is currently being evaluated in a Phase 1/2 clinical study. This designation was granted because retinitis pigmentosa is a rare, progressive hereditary blinding condition affecting approximately 1 in 4,000 individuals, with very limited or, in many cases, no approved therapies available[6]. Retinitis pigmentosa first results in loss of night and peripheral vision but ultimately affects central vision in most cases.

DSP-3077 represents an innovative approach utilizing regenerative medicine principles. The therapy involves delivering retinal sheets—three-dimensional retina-like structures grown in the laboratory—into the eyes of patients with retinitis pigmentosa[6]. The sheets are derived from 3D retinal tissue grown *in vitro*, representing a form of regenerative medicine that uses laboratory-grown retinal tissue to potentially replace damaged photoreceptors in patients' eyes[6]. This approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional gene therapy, offering the potential to restore retinal tissue function through cellular replacement rather than genetic correction. The Phase 1/2 clinical study design and specific outcome measures for DSP-3077 have not yet been fully published but represent an important direction in cell-based regenerative approaches to retinal disease.

## Drug Repurposing Candidates and Off-Label Considerations

### Nicotinamide Riboside

Nicotinamide riboside (NR), a naturally occurring precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), has emerged as a promising candidate for drug repurposing in retinitis pigmentosa. NAD+ is a coenzyme and co-substrate that declines with age and during retinal degeneration, and maintaining NAD+ levels may be therapeutic in retinal disease[43][47]. A seminal study investigated whether systemic treatment with nicotinamide riboside, an NAD+ precursor that is orally deliverable and well-tolerated by humans, is protective in rodent models of retinal degeneration[43][47].

Four mouse models of retinal degeneration were tested, including light-induced retinal degeneration (LIRD) and three models of retinitis pigmentosa: the I307N rhodopsin mouse, the IRBP knockout mouse (IRBP KO), and the *Pde6b*rd10 mouse (*rd10*)[43]. Following intraperitoneal injection with nicotinamide riboside, retinal function was assessed by electroretinogram (ERG), and retinal morphology and inflammation were assessed by optical coherence tomography and post-mortem staining of retinal sections[43]. Retinal NAD+ and NADH levels were enzymatically assayed. AAV8_shRNA vectors were subretinally injected to suppress expression of NMRK1 and NMRK2, kinases unique to the nicotinamide riboside-NAD+ pathway[43].

Each retinal degeneration model exhibited significantly suppressed retinal function, disrupted photoreceptor and retinal pigment epithelium layers, significantly increased accumulation of TUNEL-labeled cells in the outer nuclear layer, and mitochondrial dysmorphology[43]. These deleterious outcomes were prevented by various nicotinamide riboside treatment regimens[43]. Knocking down NMRK1 and NMRK2 expression partially blocked nicotinamide riboside-induced protection, confirming the importance of the nicotinamide riboside-NAD+ biosynthetic pathway[43]. Treatment with nicotinamide riboside also resulted in increased levels of NAD+ in retina (38.69±5.80 versus 87.76±10.36; p<0.01)[43]. Nicotinamide riboside treatment almost completely protected against functional and morphological losses in the LIRD and I307N models and significantly delayed degeneration in the IRBP KO and *rd10* models[43]. This protection may be due to preservation of retinal NAD+ following conversion of nicotinamide riboside to NAD+ via NMRK1/NMRK2 activity[43]. The positive outcomes, coupled with human tolerance to nicotinamide riboside dosing, suggest that maintaining retinal NAD+ via systemic nicotinamide riboside treatment or overexpressing the kinases should be further explored for clinical relevance.

### Photobiomodulation

Photobiomodulation (PBM) using light-emitting diodes or low-power lasers has emerged as an investigational approach for retinitis pigmentosa based on preclinical evidence and early clinical observations. Various treatment strategies are being investigated for retinitis pigmentosa, including light exposure avoidance, vitamin A supplementation, gene therapy, and stem cell therapy. It has been suggested that photobiomodulation might benefit retinitis pigmentosa-afflicted eyes by alleviating oxidative stress, diminishing expression of proinflammatory proteins in the retina, repairing damaged mitochondria, stimulating cytoprotective factor production, and preventing cell death. The therapeutic effects of far-red and near-infrared light are thought to occur through activation of intracellular signaling cascades via photon absorption by cytochrome c oxidase, facilitating improvements in bioenergetics and cellular homeostasis. Studies have demonstrated that red light-mediated photobiomodulation at 670 nanometers can protect the retina from mitochondrial toxicity and has shown protective effects in models of diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, and retinitis pigmentosa.

Early clinical studies and case reports indicate that photobiomodulation therapy and low-level light therapy may yield beneficial effects for retinitis pigmentosa patients, enhancing visual acuity, night vision, and quality of life. A case study by Ivandic et al. explored the application of low-level laser therapy in a patient with retinitis pigmentosa using a continuous wave diode laser (780 nanometers, average output of 10 milliwatts at 292 hertz, 50% pulse modulation) on the retina through the conjunctiva for 40 seconds, administered twice weekly over 2 weeks. After four initial low-level laser therapy sessions, visual acuity improved to 20/20 in both eyes. Visual fields also showed improvement, except for an absolute concentric scotoma in a mid-peripheral area. Five years after cessation of low-level laser therapy treatment, symptoms recurred, leading to four additional sessions, which reinstated the initial success. Over the subsequent two years, 17 additional "as-needed" treatments were administered to sustain the achieved outcome, indicating that low-level laser therapy may enhance and maintain vision in patients with retinitis pigmentosa.

Another case study by Luque-Mialdea and Molina-Seoane presented a 38-year-old patient diagnosed with retinitis pigmentosa at 20 years old displaying progressive decline in visual acuity and visual field. Following implementation of a photobiomodulation therapeutic protocol consisting of nine interspersed sessions, the patient exhibited enhancements in visual acuity, night vision, and quality of life. Clinical assessment demonstrated visual acuity of 10/10 in both eyes and significant improvement in the visual field. Photobiomodulation was found to enhance retinal mitochondrial function and maintain mitochondrial redox state, indicating significant recovery of photoreceptor cell function. These findings suggest that photobiomodulation offers a promising therapeutic strategy for retinitis pigmentosa due to its significant impact on clinical improvement, mitochondrial repair, and retinal layer thickening.

A short-term pilot study with 12 retinitis pigmentosa patients (24 eyes) who underwent treatment involving nine photobiomodulation sessions using the Valeda system, which emits three distinct wavelengths within the yellow (590 nanometers; 4 milliwatts per square centimeter), red (660 nanometers; 65 milliwatts per square centimeter), and near-infrared (850 nanometers; 0.6 milliwatts per square centimeter) spectrum, found that multiwavelength photobiomodulation showed promise in improving best-corrected visual acuity and outcomes from fundus automated perimetry in patients with retinitis pigmentosa without significant adverse effects. However, some studies had limited participant numbers, which restricts insights into long-term outcomes for retinitis pigmentosa. To thoroughly validate the efficacy and safety of photobiomodulation, additional studies with larger patient cohorts and extended follow-up durations remain essential.

### Neuroprotective Agents

#### Brimonidine

Brimonidine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist commonly used as an anti-glaucoma, intraocular pressure-lowering agent, has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in retinal cells independently of intraocular pressure reduction. Previous studies using animal models showed enhanced survival of retinal ganglion cells independently of intraocular pressure. Brimonidine protects retinal ganglion cells from somatic, axonal, and dendritic degeneration in optic injuries involving ischemia, NMDA-induced neurotoxicity, ocular hypertension, optic crush, and optic neuritis. Various mechanisms for brimonidine's neuroprotective effects have been proposed, including neurotrophic factor activation, vasomodulation, glutamate inhibition, and cell-survival signal upregulation, as well as apoptosis downregulation. Specifically, brimonidine increases the transcription of neurotrophic factors (e.g., brain-derived neurotrophic factor and fibroblast growth factor) and their receptors, which regulate various cellular functions including neuronal growth, plasticity, differentiation, and survival. Brimonidine has been shown to not only protect the retina from ischemic damage in a dose- and time-dependent manner but also to support neural regeneration after injury. Brimonidine also mitigates neuronal death and promotes cell survival by decreasing Bax expression (pro-apoptotic) while increasing Bcl-2/xL expression (anti-apoptotic) in injured cells. Furthermore, brimonidine provides anti-cytotoxic benefits by decreasing post-injury glutamate accumulation.

A clinical study by Tsai et al. reported statistically significant reduction in retinal nerve fiber layer damage following the use of brimonidine 0.2% compared with timolol 0.5% in ocular hypertensive patients over 1 year, independently of intraocular pressure reduction. These findings suggest that brimonidine may have potential neuroprotective applications in retinitis pigmentosa and Usher syndrome, though clinical trial data specifically in these diseases remains limited.

#### Fenofibrate

Fenofibrate, a fibrate class lipid-lowering medication, has demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects on the retinal pigment epithelium in preclinical models. Researchers found that palmitic acid (which induces oxidative stress) inhibited retinal pigment epithelium cell viability in a dose-dependent manner and increased levels of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), 3-nitrotyrosin, intracellular adhesion molecule-1, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), and vascular endothelial growth factor[44]. Fenofibric acid (the active form of fenofibrate after metabolism *in vivo*) ameliorated these detrimental effects. Western blot analyses indicated that high-fat diet in mice triggered oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, with dramatically increased expression levels of oxidative stress markers and proinflammatory cytokines in retinal pigment epithelium-choroid-sclera tissues[44]. Fenofibrate treatment down-regulated the expression of NOX4 and TNFα in retinal pigment epithelium tissue[44]. These results suggest fenofibrate treatment ameliorated the level of oxidative stress and inflammatory activity in retinal pigment epithelium cells induced by high-fat diet in mice[44]. The authors concluded that fenofibrate ameliorated retinal pigment epithelium cell damage induced by excessive fat *in vitro* and *in vivo*, in part through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway[44]. However, clinical trial data specifically testing fenofibrate in retinitis pigmentosa or Usher syndrome patients remain limited.

#### Statins

Statins are known for their blood cholesterol-lowering effect but also have diverse effects on different pathophysiological pathways involved in angiogenesis, inflammation, apoptosis, and anti-oxidation. The immunomodulatory effect of statins is exerted either directly on cells, such as interfering with T lymphocyte proliferation, or through inhibition of signaling molecules such as tumor necrosis factor alpha, a potent proinflammatory cytokine that induces apoptosis of retinal cells. By influencing the cytokine balance from proinflammatory to anti-inflammatory, statins modulate the immune response and achieve an immunosuppressive effect. Additionally, statins alter the interaction between the vascular endothelium and lymphocytes by blocking the intercellular adhesion molecule 1 pathway. Statins have been shown to have a number of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, specifically their ability to reduce key inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, interleukin-8, and tumor necrosis factor alpha, as well as prevent leukocyte adhesion to vascular endothelium. Statins also reduce transforming growth factor beta and have Rho-kinase inhibitory activity, which may have beneficial impacts on glaucoma by improving aqueous outflow. Furthermore, statins decrease vascular endothelial growth factor expression, which may benefit the progression of diabetic retinopathy and age-related macular degeneration.

While statins have been extensively studied, the summation of clinical studies offers conflicting results and the use of these drugs remains inconclusive. This may be related to the different treatment regimens and follow-up lengths used in various studies. Clinical trial data specifically testing statins in retinitis pigmentosa or Usher syndrome remain limited.

### Vitamin A Supplementation

Historically, vitamin A supplementation has been recommended for patients with retinitis pigmentosa based on earlier research by the late Dr. Eliot Berson, who conducted multiple randomized clinical trials investigating the effectiveness of vitamin A, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and lutein as treatments[22]. Dr. Berson reported that vitamin A supplementation modestly slowed the loss of vision in people with retinitis pigmentosa, while also finding that vitamin E supplementation accelerated vision loss[15]. The original vitamin A clinical trial measured patients' vision over time using the electroretinogram, a test measuring the retina's electrical response to light.

However, recent research has significantly challenged these findings and raised important safety concerns. A Massachusetts Eye and Ear research team led by Eric Pierce, MD, PhD, director of the Ocular Genomics Institute, and Jason Comander, MD, PhD, director of the Inherited Retinal Disorders Service, determined that high-dose vitamin A supplementation regimen does not slow vision loss in people with retinitis pigmentosa[15]. Their findings came from an analysis of additional data from the original 600-plus patient clinical trial conducted by Dr. Berson between 1984 and 1991[15]. While Pierce and his team found no overall benefit from vitamin A in the new analysis, their findings re-affirmed that people with retinitis pigmentosa should avoid vitamin E supplementation because of its negative effect on retinal health[15]. They noted that the AREDS2 supplement, which reduces the risk of advanced age-related macular degeneration for those with intermediate-stage disease, contains vitamin E and should therefore not be taken by retinitis pigmentosa patients[15]. Regardless of the name or product label of a supplement, people with retinitis pigmentosa should check ingredients to ensure they do not contain vitamin E[15]. Results from the study were published in June 2023 in the journal *JCI Insight*[15].

Researchers additionally noted that vitamin A supplements might be harmful for people with Stargardt disease and related cone-rod dystrophies, but patients can eat a normal diet[15]. Notably, it is worth noting the potential adverse effects of vitamin A supplementation. Excessive vitamin A supplementation was shown to be risky for young retinitis pigmentosa patients or those with *ABCA4* mutations due to the overaccumulation of toxic vitamin A byproducts in the retina[16]. Patients should consult their ophthalmologist before initiating any potential treatment. No systemic side effects or toxicity of high-dose vitamin A supplementation or long-term use of vitamin A supplements were found during systematic reviews, though this recommendation requires continued monitoring[16].

Despite mixed results, the combination of vitamin A, lutein, and fish oil containing docosahexaenoic acid is still recommended as therapy for retinitis pigmentosa patients[22]; however, Rayapudi et al. performed a systematic review of three clinical trials, concluding that there was no evidence to support vitamin A, docosahexaenoic acid, or a combination thereof providing retinitis pigmentosa patients with significant benefit[22]. Furthermore, regular use of high doses of vitamin A can cause a range of side effects and complications[22]. For this reason, vitamin A treatment remains controversial among ophthalmologists and retinal specialists.

## Contraindicated Medications

### Vitamin E Supplementation

High-dose vitamin E supplementation is contraindicated in patients with retinitis pigmentosa due to documented acceleration of vision loss. Research has established that vitamin E supplementation, particularly at doses of 400 IU per day, could lead to a decrease in serum retinol concentration, which may negatively affect the course of retinal degeneration in retinitis pigmentosa[16]. In the Berson vitamin A trial, patients receiving vitamin A 15,000 IU per day plus vitamin E 400 IU per day exhibited significantly worse outcomes than control groups[16]. This finding has been consistently reaffirmed and represents one of the few definitively contraindicated interventions in retinitis pigmentosa management[15].

### Chemotherapy Agents

Multiple chemotherapy agents have been documented to cause retinal toxicity that could exacerbate or mimic retinitis pigmentosa. These include cisplatin, paclitaxel, and docetaxel. A pilot study found evidence for subclinical dose-dependent toxic retinal alterations in patients treated with cisplatin-based chemotherapy for germ cell cancer, representing the first evidence of chronic subclinical retinal toxicity due to cisplatin-based chemotherapy[42][46]. Significantly reduced average retinal nerve fiber layer thickness was demonstrated in 75% of examined eyes, with a linear relationship observed between average retinal nerve fiber layer thickness and the cumulative cisplatin dose administered[42]. These results support the experience of dose-dependent ocular toxicity as observed after high-dose intravenous or intraarterial cisplatin chemotherapy. The data suggest irreversible retinal damage, as there was no tendency toward improvement of average retinal nerve fiber layer thickness after cessation of cisplatin-based chemotherapy[42].

Paclitaxel and docetaxel, both taxane-class chemotherapy agents, were associated with increased risk of epiphora, cystoid macular edema, and optic neuropathy when compared with tamoxifen users in a large pharmacoepidemiologic study[40]. A suspected case of paclitaxel-induced phototoxic maculopathy was reported following endoillumination during vitrectomy surgery, representing the first such case in the literature[36]. The proposed mechanism involves paclitaxel photosensitizing the retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptor cells, increasing the risk of photochemical damage during endoillumination[36]. A likely explanation relates to taxane-induced dermal photosensitivity, thought to be caused by porphyrin aberrations. Since porphyrins have been linked to ocular injury, including phototoxicity of the retinal pigment epithelium, it is feasible that porphyrin aberration due to paclitaxel use may cause retinal pigment epithelium photosensitization[36].

### Antimicrobial and Immunomodulatory Agents

#### Ethambutol

Ethambutol, a first-line antituberculosis agent, may lead to permanent vision loss by inducing a dose- and duration-dependent optic neuropathy[20]. The reported incidence of ethambutol-related ocular toxicity varies widely in different studies, ranging from 1–2.5% for dosage of 15 mg/kg per day to 5–6% for dosage of 25 mg/kg per day, and reaching as high as 18% for dosage of 35 mg/kg per day[20]. Toxicity typically occurs between 3–5 months of usage, though it may present as early as within 1 month and as late as 12 months of use[20]. Toxic optic neuropathy may occur at any time interval after use of ethambutol, but most often it is seen after 3–5 months of use[20]. While ethambutol toxicity generally occurs with doses higher than 15 mg/kg, it has also been reported in lower doses; therefore, all patients on ethambutol treatment need monitoring for ocular toxicity[20]. Typically, patients present with subacute, bilateral, and painless symmetric loss of central vision, though visual involvement may be asymmetrical or sequential[20]. Patients may describe cloudy or blurry vision and difficulty in reading or in distinguishing colors[20]. Color vision loss may occur and typically involves difficulty in distinguishing red and green colors, though blue-yellow defects may also occur[20]. Reduction in contrast sensitivity has also been reported as a marker of ethambutol toxicity[20]. Early changes on optical coherence tomography can be seen as an increase or no discernible change in retinal nerve fiber layer thickness and thinning of ganglion cell complex layer[20].

#### Fingolimod

Fingolimod, a disease-modifying therapy for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis that acts to modulate the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor, is associated with fingolimod-associated macular edema as a known dose-dependent side effect[41][45]. Macular edema has been a well-documented side effect of fingolimod since its original evaluation as an anti-rejection agent for renal transplantation. In the FREEDOMS and TRANSFORMS trials, the risk of fingolimod-associated macular edema was dose-dependent and occurred more often in patients on 1.25 mg versus those taking 0.5 mg, with fingolimod-associated macular edema occurring in 1% to 1.6% of patients on 1.25 mg compared to 0% to 0.5% in the 0.5 mg group[45]. Patient age greater than 41 years and coexisting conditions like retinal vascular disease, diabetes mellitus, diabetic retinopathy, uveitis, and past ocular surgery also increased the risk of developing fingolimod-associated macular edema[45]. The proposed pathophysiological mechanism is based upon the interaction between fingolimod and S1PR1 present on endothelial cells in retinal vessels[45]. S1PR1 signaling is responsible for maintaining cell-to-cell and cell-to-matrix adhesion complexes, and the use of fingolimod is thought to downregulate this receptor, leading to downregulation of adhesion complexes and subsequent increased retinal vascular permeability resulting in edema[45]. Additionally, beyond macular edema, fingolimod-associated retinal hemorrhages and retinal vein occlusion can occur, though visual consequences appear to be mild and resolution is often attained with discontinuation of therapy[41].

#### Hydroxychloroquine

Hydroxychloroquine, a medication prescribed to treat malaria and autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis, has multiple significant systemic side effects, including retinal toxicity that may cause irreversible loss of vision[19]. Long-term use of hydroxychloroquine can cause retinopathy, which may result in severe and progressive visual loss[23]. In the past decade, hydroxychloroquine use has markedly increased and modern retinal imaging techniques have enabled the detection of early, pre-symptomatic disease, and as a consequence, the prevalence of retinal toxicity in long-term hydroxychloroquine users is known to be higher than previously estimated[23]. Modern imaging data suggest that photoreceptors most likely degenerate first and retinal pigment epithelium degeneration develops subsequently in hydroxychloroquine retinopathy[23]. Photoreceptor changes precede retinal pigment epithelium defects on optical coherence tomography imaging and hypo-autofluorescence on fundus autofluorescence, which signifies damage to or loss of the retinal pigment epithelium[23].

#### Tamoxifen

Tamoxifen can lead to corneal toxicity, progression of cataracts, retinopathy, and neuropathy[18]. The reported incidence of ocular toxic side effects among patients receiving tamoxifen ranges from 6.3% to 12%. The most visually significant aspect of tamoxifen toxicity is maculopathy[18]. Although the retinal pigment epithelium is thought to be the primary target of tamoxifen toxicity, recent reports have demonstrated that tamoxifen toxicity also affects photoreceptors[18]. Ultrastructural lesions associated with these agents may appear as crystalloid inclusions in the neuroretina or as crystalloid bodies within the retinal pigment epithelium, which are thought to disrupt lysosomal function[18]. Tamoxifen toxicity of the retina is believed to be mediated by damage to the retinal pigment epithelium through disruption of lysosomes[18]. Cell death occurred within 2 hours of tamoxifen treatment of confluent retinal pigment epithelium cells and was accompanied by lysosomal membrane permeabilization[18]. Blockade of cathepsin B and L activity led to a significant decrease in cell death, indicating that lysosomal destabilization and cathepsin release occur prior to regulated cell death[18].

### Topiramate

Topiramate has been associated with multiple ocular adverse effects, the most well-known of which are choroidal effusion syndrome, angle-closure glaucoma, and myopic shift[17]. Aside from these, other ophthalmic adverse effects may arise in some patients, including retinal problems, uveitis, visual field defects, myokymia, and neuro-ophthalmology complications[17]. If such complications are not identified and treated promptly, they can be severe and vision-threatening, potentially leading to permanent blindness[17]. In addition to retinal striae, topiramate may cause macular and retinal neurosensory detachments[17]. Symptoms improve once topiramate is discontinued[17]. Topiramate may cause damage to the retina through multiple mechanisms, as demonstrated in a published study assessing the effects of chronic topiramate administration in rabbits, which showed a significant reduction in retinal function[17]. In the inner retina, immunohistochemical alterations due to substantial accumulation of gamma-aminobutyric acid were also observed[17].

## Adverse Events Related to Disease Mimicry and Exacerbation

### Isotretinoin

Isotretinoin (Accutane), a potent retinoid used for treating severe cystic acne, has been associated with adverse effects on retinal function that could exacerbate or mimic retinitis pigmentosa. Three of 50 patients treated with isotretinoin at 1 mg/kg per day for cystic acne complained of poor night vision and/or excessive glare sensitivity[24]. These findings raise concerns about the use of isotretinoin in patients with known or suspected retinitis pigmentosa or Usher syndrome.

### Docetaxel Drug Interactions

A case report described docetaxel-induced maculopathy in a patient with a long-term history of rheumatoid arthritis controlled on hydroxychloroquine for 33 years with no evidence of retinopathy who developed bilateral loss of vision after having been on docetaxel chemotherapy for breast cancer[25]. Optical coherence tomography showed bilateral cystic maculopathy with no angiographic evidence of leakage on fluorescein angiography. The researchers hypothesized that impaired transcellular retinal pigment epithelium transport might be the cause of non-leaking cystic maculopathy and suggested that docetaxel-induced maculopathy may be potentiated by concurrent hydroxychloroquine therapy, representing the first case report of docetaxel-induced maculopathy in a setting of hydroxychloroquine therapy where one drug potentially potentiated the retinal toxicity of the other[25].

## Combination Therapies and Synergistic Approaches

### Gene Therapy Plus Neuroprotection

While specific clinical trial data on combination therapies in Usher syndrome remain limited, emerging evidence suggests potential synergistic benefits of combining gene therapy approaches with neuroprotective agents. Therapies targeting inflammation have shown their potential to attenuate photoreceptor degeneration in preclinical models. Regardless of variations in genetic background, inflammatory modulation is emerging as an important role in the treatment of retinitis pigmentosa. Dexamethasone administration to *rd10* mice reduces retinal inflammation, restores cone structure and function, and preserves retinal pigment epithelium integrity by preserving zonula occludens-1 expression. Treatment with an antagonist of the TNF-α receptor such as infliximab and adalimumab resulted in improved retinal health in *rd10* mice. Administration of these inhibitors reduced activation of microglia and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Inhibition of inflammatory processes was also achieved by targeting myeloid differentiation factor 88, which is an adaptor protein for interleukin-1β and toll-like receptors. The blockage of myeloid differentiation factor 88 by a specific peptide increased the function of rod photoreceptors and reduced apoptosis in *rd10* mice compared to non-treated controls.

### Antioxidant Plus Anti-Inflammatory Approaches

Pharmacological modulation of the retinal inflammatory response in combination with antioxidant compounds represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Flavonoids, which exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties and modulate the stability and folding of rod opsin, could be valid options in developing treatment strategies against retinitis pigmentosa. Recent *in vitro* and *in vivo* studies have demonstrated the anti-inflammatory effects of quercetin through the inhibition of pro-inflammatory markers with advantageous effects on cellular health. Quercetin was shown to inhibit inflammatory reactions in mice acutely injured with bright light by suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory molecules, including CCL2, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha. Treatment of *rd10* mice with a mixture of various nutraceuticals decreased the expression of these cytokines, resulting in delayed loss of photoreceptors and preserved function of the retina. Similar beneficial effects decreasing inflammatory markers were found for another flavonoid, myricetin.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The therapeutic landscape for Usher syndrome and retinitis pigmentosa has undergone remarkable transformation over the past five years, transitioning from a state of complete therapeutic nihilism to a robust pipeline of investigational therapies at various stages of development. As of April 2026, the field encompasses multiple promising approaches including gene therapies targeting specific genetic mutations, optogenetic therapies designed to bypass photoreceptor loss, antisense oligonucleotide approaches for exon-skipping, small molecule antioxidant therapies, cell-based regenerative approaches, and neuroprotective agents. The diversity of mechanisms represented in this pipeline reflects both the genetic heterogeneity of Usher syndrome and the multifactorial nature of photoreceptor degeneration.

Approved therapies remain extremely limited, with LUXTURNA representing the sole FDA-approved pharmacological treatment for inherited retinal disease; however, this approval applies exclusively to patients with *RPE65* mutations, a genetic form distinct from the nine genes primarily implicated in Usher syndrome. The vast majority of Usher syndrome patients remain without approved disease-modifying treatments, highlighting the critical unmet medical need in this population. Multiple investigational therapies are now advancing through late-stage clinical development, with several anticipated regulatory submissions and approvals expected within the coming years. MCO-010 has initiated rolling biologics license application submission for optogenetic therapy, laru-zova has completed Phase 2/3 enrollment with topline data expected in late 2026, and multiple antisense oligonucleotide and gene therapy approaches are in Phase 2 or Phase 3 clinical trials.

The identification and understanding of contraindicated medications—particularly vitamin E, chemotherapy agents, and numerous immunomodulatory drugs—remains critical for patient safety and disease management. Healthcare providers managing Usher syndrome patients must maintain vigilance regarding medication prescriptions and counsel patients to avoid high-dose vitamin E supplementation and to report all medications to their ophthalmologists. Drug repurposing approaches, particularly those targeting oxidative stress and inflammation, hold considerable promise as bridge therapies while awaiting regulatory approval of novel investigational agents.

Future treatment algorithms will likely involve combination approaches utilizing gene therapy, cell therapy, optogenetics, and neuroprotective agents tailored to individual patient genetics and disease stage. Early identification of disease through newborn screening and comprehensive genetic testing will enable patient enrollment in appropriate clinical trials and access to emerging therapies. The remarkable progress over the past five years, evidenced by multiple phase 3 trials completing enrollment and rolling regulatory applications being submitted, provides substantial hope for Usher syndrome patients and their families that disease-modifying treatments will become available in the near term.
